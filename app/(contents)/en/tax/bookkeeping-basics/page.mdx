# Basic Bookkeeping/Accounting for Non-Accountant Founders

> **In short:** You don't need a finance degree to run a startup's numbers well. You need to close your books every month instead of at year-end, understand a handful of metrics well enough to explain them in one sentence, and know your cash runway at all times. Most of the financial trouble early-stage founders get into isn't complicated accounting — it's simply not looking.

## Close the Books Every Month, Not Once a Year

A lot of founders treat financial statements as a compliance chore that happens once a year, around [tax filing](/tax/filing-returns) time. That's backwards. If you don't know how the business performed last month, you're making this month's decisions blind.[^shoumik-accounting]

This doesn't require a full-time accountant on day one. It requires a habit: close the books by a fixed date each month — the 5th or 7th working day is common — and actually read the output before moving on. If your books aren't in a state where that's possible yet, start there; see [Accounts, audit, and bookkeeping: what to set up at the start](/tax/bookkeeping-setup) for the legal minimum the Companies Act expects.

## The Handful of Numbers You Actually Need to Understand

You don't need deep accounting knowledge. You need to know what a small set of terms mean well enough that a number on a page tells you something.[^shoumik-metrics]

- **Gross margin** — revenue minus direct cost of goods or services, as a percentage of revenue. Tells you whether the core unit economics work before overhead even enters the picture.
- **Burn rate** — monthly cash outflow minus monthly cash inflow. If this is positive, you're spending down your cash balance every month.
- **Runway** — how many months your current cash lasts at the current burn rate. Covered in detail below.
- **EBITDA margin** — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation, as a percentage of revenue. A rough read on operating profitability that strips out financing and accounting noise.
- **Current ratio** — current assets divided by current liabilities. Below 1 is a liquidity warning: you may struggle to cover short-term obligations even if the business is fundamentally healthy.

You don't need to calculate all five by hand every month. You need to know which one to check when a particular kind of decision comes up — gross margin before a pricing change, runway before a hiring decision.

## Cash Runway: The Metric That Can Actually Kill the Business

Runway is the number of months your startup can keep operating before the cash runs out.[^shoumik-runway] The formula is simple:

**Monthly burn rate = monthly cash outflow − monthly cash inflow**
**Runway = cash on hand ÷ monthly burn rate**

There's no universal rule for how much runway is enough, but 18–24 months is a common early-stage target — long enough to survive a slower-than-planned fundraise without negotiating from a position of desperation.[^shoumik-runway] If you're raising on a [SAFE or convertible note](/funding/safe-convertible-notes), your runway number is one of the first things investors will ask about, alongside the [term sheet](/funding/term-sheets) terms themselves.

When runway gets tight, the discipline that helps most is separating fixed costs (rent, salaries — hard to cut quickly) from variable costs (marketing spend, discretionary tooling — adjustable within a month), and reviewing both against a stated target rather than cutting reactively. Communicate the plan to the whole team; a runway plan that lives only in one spreadsheet the founder checks alone tends to fail quietly.

## Where Early-Stage Bangladeshi Startups Actually Bleed Money

A few patterns show up repeatedly in startups that spend more than the stage of the business justifies:[^shoumik-costs]

- **Hiring ahead of need.** It's tempting to hire for the org chart you'll need in a year. Overlapping roles and inflated payroll are hard to unwind quietly — this is a large share of why funding slowdowns turn into layoffs. Stay slightly understaffed rather than the reverse, and use contract hires for seasonal or project work.
- **Perks that outpace the stage.** A well-appointed office and generous team outings feel like culture-building, but they pull capital away from product and growth at a stage where that capital matters more. Meaningful benefits don't have to be expensive ones.
- **Marketing spend with no attached number.** Sponsorships, broad "brand awareness" campaigns, and unmeasured PR are easy to justify and hard to evaluate. A reasonable early-stage rule: every taka of marketing spend should be traceable to a return, seasonality adjusted. If you can't measure it, that's a reason to be cautious with it, not a reason to skip measuring.
- **Office space that outpaces revenue.** A nicer Gulshan or Banani address doesn't move the metrics that matter to your [next round](/funding/valuation). Spend on space that fits the team you have, not the one you're hoping to become.

None of this means run lean for its own sake — it means every recurring cost should trace back to a reason you could explain to your board in one sentence. If you can't, it's worth a second look.

## Keep Personal and Business Money Separate, From Day One

If you're still operating as a sole proprietorship or informally, two problems compound: investors generally can't put equity into a business that isn't a [limited company](/company-types), and mixing personal and business transactions makes financial due diligence — see [Fundraising data room checklist](/funding/data-room) — far more painful than it needs to be.[^shoumik-accounting]

Open a dedicated business bank account as early as possible, even before you're fundraising. It requires the same documents — [trade license](/trade-license), [e-TIN](/tax/e-tin), [BIN](/tax/vat-bin) — that a compliant company needs anyway, so there's no real reason to delay it.

## Action Plan

- [ ] Set a fixed monthly close date and put it on the calendar — not "whenever there's time."
- [ ] Write a one-paragraph plain-language definition of gross margin, burn rate, and runway, and keep it next to your monthly numbers until you don't need it anymore.
- [ ] Calculate your current runway this week. If it's under 12 months and you're not already fundraising, that's the trigger to start.
- [ ] Open a dedicated business bank account if you haven't already.
