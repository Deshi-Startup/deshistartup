# Start Here: Your First Steps as a Founder in Bangladesh

> **In short:** Start with a person and a problem, learn how they handle it today, then choose a small next step that fits your time and money. You do not need a finished product or a company to explore an idea. Check the requirements for an activity before you actually carry it out.

You may have an app in mind, a problem you keep noticing at work, or simply the wish to build something of your own. The first task is to understand whose life or work would improve and whether the improvement matters enough for a business to form around it. You can start learning while studying, employed or caring for others; a full-time leap is not a prerequisite.

If you already know the task—registering a company, taking payments or hiring—use the [journeys](/en/journeys) to find the relevant sequence. If you are new, work through the questions below in order. The guides and learning tools are free and require no account.

## Begin with a customer problem

“Build an app” tells you what you want to make. It does not tell you why someone would use it. Choose a specific group you can reach and a task you want to understand. What becomes difficult, when does it happen, and what do people do about it now?

For example, “help shops” is too broad to investigate. “Help clothing sellers match courier payments to their orders” gives you a task to ask about. It is still only a possible problem: the sellers may already handle it well with a notebook or an employee. That is something to discover before deciding that software is needed.

Write what you have actually seen or heard separately from what you are assuming. A recurring complaint is a reason to ask more questions, not proof that everyone will buy a solution. If you have no problem in mind yet, [ideas and market research](/en/ideas) helps you find a starting point.

## Find out what happens today

Speak with people who do the task, at a time they agree to. Explain why you are asking and get permission before recording or quoting them. Ask about the last time the problem occurred:

- What happened, and what did you do next?
- How do you normally handle this?
- What went wrong, and when does the current method work well?
- What time or money did it take, if you remember?
- Who would decide to change the process?

These questions help you understand actual behavior. “Do you like my idea?” mainly invites an opinion. Listen before describing your solution, and keep answers that challenge the idea as well as those that encourage you. Do not collect personal records you do not need to understand the task.

Compare the conversations. Are people describing the same difficulty, or different problems that happen to sound similar? Do they already spend effort or money handling it? There is no interview count that automatically proves an idea. The [validation guide](/en/validation) explains how to choose respondents and decide what to test next.

## Test one part before building the whole product

At this point, choose the question whose answer would change your next decision. If you do not know whether the problem matters, more product features will not answer it. If people want the outcome but you do not know whether your approach works, you can try delivering it manually or demonstrate the important task.

An early usable version that delivers the core benefit is often called a **minimum viable product**, or **MVP**. It does not need every feature you imagine. You may also need simpler experiments before building any product at all.

Keep the test honest. A demonstration can show whether someone understands or can use an approach. An offer with a real scope, price and delivery date can test whether someone will buy it. A paid pilot tests more than a compliment, but a few purchases do not establish repeat demand or a large market. Record what actually happened and what remains uncertain.

Before offering paid work, using real data or testing a sensitive activity, check the permissions, safety and delivery obligations involved. A small test is still a real activity. The [legal roadmap](/en/legal-roadmap) helps you identify the relevant preparation.

## Understand what would make it a business

A useful product is part of the answer. You also need a way to find customers, agree what they are buying, deliver it reliably and receive enough money to cover the work. Together, those choices make up the **business model**.

A startup searches for a model that can work repeatedly and grow beyond the founders' personal labor. A service business can develop a scalable product; an online shop can remain a small business. Software, agencies and e-commerce describe different aspects of businesses, not a ladder of ambition. The [startup and business-types guide](/en/start-here/startup-vs-sme) helps you understand those distinctions without forcing a label before you know what you want to build.

As you begin selling, follow the whole transaction. Agree the scope or product, price, delivery date and what happens if something goes wrong. Record the payment against the order. If the customer pays next month but your supplier needs money tomorrow, an expected sale cannot pay tomorrow's bill. [Finding customers](/en/customers), [payments](/en/payments) and [cashflow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit) explain those jobs in more detail.

Company incorporation, tax identity, local permissions and sector approvals are separate questions. Check what applies before the relevant activity, not after assuming that one certificate covers the business. Sharing ownership or hiring also creates agreements and duties that need preparation.

## Choose a first week you can actually carry out

Start with the time and money available after essential household spending, work commitments and care responsibilities. Choose one question to investigate, a few relevant people to approach and a day to review what happened. Leave room for people to decline or reschedule.

Before you begin, write a short note: who you want to learn from, what you need to understand, what you will do and how much time or money you can use. Afterward, add what happened and the next decision. If only one person replies, you have one conversation and a recruitment problem to investigate, not a completed market study.

Continue when the evidence justifies the next small commitment. Change the customer, offer or method when the evidence points elsewhere. Stopping an idea can also be a useful result if it prevents an unsupported investment of time and money.

For help fitting the work around other responsibilities, read [founder life](/en/founder-life). To plan a specific demand test, use the [demand-test planner](/en/journeys/test-demand). The wider [roadmap](/en/roadmap) shows how later work connects; [Browse by Topic](/en/guides) and [Tools and Templates](/en/tools) help when you need a particular guide or worksheet.
