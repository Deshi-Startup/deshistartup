# Working Capital Calculator: Stock, Invoices and Cash Cycle

> **In short:** Stock and unpaid invoices can tie up money even when sales grow. Enter your monthly amounts and average waiting times below. The calculator estimates the value of your stock plus money customers owe you, minus bills you still owe suppliers. It also shows your cash conversion cycle: stock days plus customer payment days, minus supplier payment days. Change one waiting time to see what could improve. This is not your bank balance or the amount you need to borrow.

Use this for an established product business or a business selling on credit. It assumes activity continues at roughly the same pace. For a launch, an Eid stock build-up or a large one-off order, map the actual payment dates in a cash forecast instead.

## Try your numbers

Use amounts from the same business or product line, worked out for a **30-day month**, excluding VAT throughout. For example, take a normal 90-day period and divide its totals by three. The starting numbers are a **hypothetical Bangladesh B2B product business**, not an industry benchmark. All its sales and purchases are on credit.

Change customer collection days from **20 to 10**. The estimate falls from **BDT 5,80,000 to BDT 3,80,000**. That is BDT 2,00,000 less owed by customers once they regularly pay in ten days, not a recurring monthly profit.

## Which “working capital” does this show?

**Net <Term name="working-capital">working capital</Term>** on a balance sheet is current assets minus current liabilities. It includes more than these three operating balances, such as cash and short-term obligations.[^bdc-working-capital]

This tool shows a narrower **operating balance: inventory + receivables - payables**. Receivables are customer invoices not yet collected; payables are supplier bills not yet paid. The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)** is a time measure: inventory days + collection days - supplier payment days.[^acca-working-capital]

A shorter cycle is useful only if the change works in practice. Running out of stock or losing a supplier can cost more than the cash released.

## Get the inputs from your records

Bring your sales records, the cost of goods sold, supplier purchase records and average balances. Choose a period that reflects normal business, rather than one unusually busy month.

- **Credit sales:** sales where customers pay later. Leave immediate cash sales out. Use the amount invoiced, not cash collected this month.
- **Cost of goods sold (COGS):** the cost of the goods sold during the period, including goods sold for cash. Do not enter their selling price or the value of all stock bought.
- **Credit purchases:** goods or services bought from suppliers on credit. Leave purchases paid immediately out. Do not automatically copy COGS here: purchases can differ from the cost of goods sold.
- **Inventory days:** average inventory at cost ÷ daily COGS. For this tool, daily COGS is the 30-day amount ÷ 30.
- **Customer collection days:** average trade receivables ÷ daily credit sales. Use the actual collection pattern, not just “30 days” printed on invoices.
- **Supplier payment days:** average trade payables ÷ daily credit purchases. Match the supplier balances to the purchases you included.[^acca-ratios]

For an initial average balance, use opening plus closing balance divided by two; record the balances more often when they change a lot. Keep the same period and VAT basis for amounts and balances. A courier or payment-provider settlement delay needs its own cash schedule if it is not captured in trade receivables. Customer deposits and supplier prepayments are outside this model.

**If a monthly amount is zero, its matching days must also be zero.** For example, no credit sales means zero collection days. If old invoices or unsold stock remain despite no new activity, use those actual balances and payment dates in a forecast. A zero here does not write them off. No inventory? Enter zero COGS and inventory days only when those costs and balances really are absent; this does not remove payroll or other service costs.

## Check the calculation by hand

The tool works backwards from the day counts to estimate how much money sits in each balance. Inventory uses cost, customer invoices use credit sales, and supplier bills use credit purchases.[^acca-ratios]

```text
Inventory = (monthly COGS ÷ 30) × inventory days
Receivables = (monthly credit sales ÷ 30) × collection days
Payables = (monthly credit purchases ÷ 30) × payment days
Operating cash tied up = inventory + receivables - payables
CCC = inventory days + collection days - payment days
```

In the hypothetical example, monthly credit sales are BDT 6 lakh. COGS and credit purchases are each BDT 3.6 lakh. Stock stays for 30 days, customers pay in 20 days and suppliers receive payment in 15 days. Daily credit sales are BDT 20,000; daily COGS and purchases are each BDT 12,000.

Here is where the BDT 5.8 lakh estimate comes from:

| Balance | Calculation | Estimated BDT |
|---|---|---:|
| Inventory at cost | 12,000 × 30 | 3,60,000 |
| Customer receivables | 20,000 × 20 | 4,00,000 |
| Less supplier payables | 12,000 × 15 | -1,80,000 |
| Operating cash tied up | 3,60,000 + 4,00,000 - 1,80,000 | **5,80,000** |

CCC is **30 + 20 - 15 = 35 days**. Multiplying 35 by daily sales would give BDT 7 lakh, which is **not** this operating balance. It incorrectly values the stock and supplier bills at the selling-price rate.

## Test one realistic change

Keep sales and costs unchanged and compare each option separately with the original example:

| One change | New CCC | New cash tied up | Reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Collect customer invoices in 10 rather than 20 days | 25 days | BDT 3,80,000 | BDT 2,00,000 |
| Hold stock for 20 rather than 30 days | 25 days | BDT 4,60,000 | BDT 1,20,000 |
| Agree 25 rather than 15 days with suppliers | 25 days | BDT 4,60,000 | BDT 1,20,000 |

All three shorten CCC by ten days, but the cash effect differs because sales and costs have different daily values. These are alternative scenarios, not savings to add together. Any collection discount, price change or extra delivery cost would also need to be included in the decision.

Pick a change you can verify: invoice sooner, reduce a slow-moving stock line or negotiate a written supplier term. Do not enter a longer payment period simply because you plan to pay late; supplier credit and discounts can be lost.[^acca-working-capital]

## What a zero or negative result means

A **negative CCC** means this model puts customer payments before the supplier payment deadline. It does not automatically produce a negative taka balance, because the money calculation uses different bases.

A **negative cash-tied-up estimate** means the supplier bills in this estimate are larger than the stock value plus money customers owe you. Those bills still need paying. The result is not profit, spare cash to distribute or proof that the business needs no funding. A zero estimate makes none of those promises either.

The calculator excludes cash already held, rent, payroll, taxes, interest, loan repayments, equipment, deposits, bad debts, returns and minimum cash reserves. It assumes sales, costs and average waiting times stay roughly the same; it does not show the biggest cash shortfall during growth or seasonal buying. The cash needed on a particular date depends on actual receipts and payments. See [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit) for that distinction.
