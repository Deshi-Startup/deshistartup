# Student Founder Semester Plan: Test One Startup Idea

> **In short:** Put exams and course deadlines on the calendar first. Choose one question about a startup idea that you can test in the time left. Set a spending limit and a date to stop taking new work. At the end, decide from customer evidence whether to continue, change the test or pause.

Use this if you are a student exploring a startup alongside your studies. You will leave with one test, a plan for your weekly hours and a plan for exam weeks. You do not need to launch a company this semester. If you already owe customers ongoing service, plan how to meet those promises before reducing your hours.

## Copy your semester plan

Keep this in a notebook or document you can update. For a team, each person should confirm their own hours and unavailable dates.

```text
Semester / test dates:
Exams, coursework deadlines and unavailable weeks:
Hours available each week / per person:
Time held back for delays:
Money available for this test / maximum spending:

Customer group and problem they keep facing:
One question the test should answer:
Smallest test we can deliver:
Who will take part / how we will reach them:
What result would support the next step:
What would make us change or stop:

Who owns each task / backup if needed:
Last day to promise new trial work:
How we will finish or hand over existing work:
Exam pause dates / next review date:
Evidence collected / decision:
```

The sheet is a suggested planning method. Its time and money limits should come from your actual routine and resources, not someone else's success story.

## Find a workable gap in the calendar

Collect your course outlines and current academic calendar. Mark exams, assignments, lab work and any dates you need to confirm. Looking across the whole semester helps reveal weeks when several deadlines meet.[^usd-semester] Add paid work, travel, family duties and rest before choosing startup hours.

Put a few specific work sessions in the remaining gaps. Count customer calls, travel, note-taking and support as work too. Leave some time unassigned for delays. A week with no safe gap can be a pause week; do not spread its missed work across already full weeks.

Agree the unavailable dates with teammates before promising a trial. Ask your course teacher or the relevant university office what permission is needed before using course work, campus facilities, university names or other people's data. Do not assume a class project gives the team permission to sell everything in it.

## Choose a test small enough to finish

Start with a group you can reach and a task they struggle with. Student founders do not have to sell to students. If you have no candidate yet, use the [startup-ideas catalogue](/en/startup-ideas) or [idea-finding guide](/en/ideas/finding-ideas), then bring one problem back to this sheet.

Choose the test according to what you still do not know:

- **Unsure the problem matters:** speak with people who recently faced it. Ask what happened, how they handled it and what the difficulty cost them.
- **Unsure people can use the solution:** show a paper sketch or small demonstration. Ask them to attempt one task and watch where they need help.
- **Unsure someone will pay:** make a clear, small offer only when you can deliver it and have checked any permissions or business requirements that apply. Record the response separately from a usage test.

When you watch someone try a task, focus on learning about their work. Give them room to try it and explain what they do, without turning the session into a sales pitch.[^yc-observation] Write down what happened, not just whether they liked your idea. Friends may help you practise, but speak with people who actually have the problem too.

Set a review question before starting: “Could staff find a repair's status without asking the owner?” is more useful than “Did we finish the app?” No fixed number of interviews proves demand. A class grade, competition prize or friendly compliment does not show that a customer will use or buy the product.

## Worked example: a six-week window before exams

Imagine two students in Khulna testing a repair-status tool for small repair shops. The situation, hours and costs below are fictional. They want to learn whether staff can keep a job's status up to date without the owner chasing them.

Each student can give **3 hours a week** for **6 weeks**. Their total is **2 × 3 × 6 = 36 team-hours**. They hold back **1 team-hour each week**, or **6 hours**, for delays. That leaves **30 planned hours**. These are added working hours, not 30 hours when both students must be together.

They divide those 30 hours into four pieces:

1. **10 hours:** arrange visits, talk to shop staff and owners, travel and review notes. Finish with a clear account of the current problem.
2. **4 hours:** make and rehearse one status-recording sheet using fictional repair jobs. Finish with a demonstration someone else can try.
3. **12 hours:** run a small, agreed trial and handle questions. Check whether staff update and find a job's status. Include the time spent helping them.
4. **4 hours:** review evidence, return or hand over anything the team holds, and tell participants whether the trial is ending or continuing.

The total is **10 + 4 + 12 + 4 = 30 hours**. These numbers show one possible plan; your team may need different hours. If arranging visits takes longer, they reduce the trial or move it beyond exams. They do not quietly take time from coursework or drop the final handover.

For cash, suppose they can safely set aside **BDT 3,000**, after keeping tuition and living costs separate. Their made-up budget allows **BDT 1,000 for travel + BDT 200 for calls + BDT 800 for materials = BDT 2,000**. They leave **BDT 1,000 aside**, without choosing a use for it yet. They record actual spending and both students' time; unpaid work is still a cost. If the next step needs more money, they decide again before spending it.

They agree the trial's end date with the shop. They use made-up repair jobs first. Before using real records, they need the shop's permission and must make sure only the people who need those records can open them. The shop keeps its normal records throughout. This is a limited test, not a promise to run its daily operations through exam week.

If staff use the sheet, the team has early evidence about use. Without an actual purchase, they still do not have payment evidence. They can prepare a priced offer for the next available window instead of calling the idea proven.

## Pause without leaving work behind

Choose the last day for new commitments early enough to finish what you already promised. For each active trial, record its owner, next action, end date and what the participant should do during the pause. A teammate is only a backup after they agree and can do the work.

Tell participants about the pause before it starts. Finish the agreed work or arrange an acceptable handover. Return borrowed items, settle money owed and remove unnecessary access. Keep needed records protected. Do not delete someone else's working records just to tidy your project.

If the idea needs urgent, ongoing support that nobody can cover, limit this semester's work to interviews or a demonstration. A written plan does not create extra availability.

## Make the next decision from your notes

At the review date, compare the plan with actual hours, spending and customer actions. Continue when there is a useful next question and enough time and money to test it. Change the test when the result is unclear or the customer group was wrong. Pause when you cannot fit the work around your studies, pay for it or keep your promises to customers.

Before you begin:

- [ ] Each teammate has confirmed their hours and exam dates.
- [ ] One customer question has a small test and a review date.
- [ ] Essential personal spending is protected.
- [ ] Trial promises fit the available time and permissions.
- [ ] Someone owns the pause, handover and next decision.

Fill the calendar first, then arrange one conversation for an open work session. The [30-day roadmap](/en/start-here/30-day-roadmap) gives more detail on testing an idea; adapt its pace to your semester rather than forcing a month of work into exam week.
