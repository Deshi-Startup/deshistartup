# Test an Idea Before Building It

> **In short:** Choose one belief your idea depends on, then plan a small test with real people. Leave this page with the people to approach, the evidence to collect, a spending limit, and a review date. A promising result earns another test; it does not prove the whole business will work.

Use this when you have an idea but need to decide what to learn before spending more time or money. You can work through it alone, with a cofounder, or in a student group. You do not need an app, a company website, or an advertising budget to start this exercise.

**Your task today:** write one test plan. Set aside about 20–30 minutes as a planning allowance, then schedule the real conversations separately. Already know what you want to test? [Go straight to the planner](#write-your-plan).

## Choose the uncertainty first

An **assumption** is something you believe but have not yet established. Start with one that could change whether you should build the product.

| What you do not yet know | A small test to consider |
|---|---|
| Does this group experience the problem? | Ask about recent events and how they handled them. |
| Can your proposed approach help? | Let someone try a clearly described manual version or prototype on a real task. |
| Will they pay for a specific offer? | Present its scope and price, and record what they actually do next. |

Choose the row that matches your uncertainty. An interview about a problem cannot settle whether someone will pay for your proposed solution. A request for a demo is worth following up, but it is not a sale.

For more test options, use [Five Tests for Demand Before You Build the Product](/en/validation/demand-without-building). You can complete the exercise below without leaving this page.

## See one complete plan

**Fictional example:** A founder is considering scheduling software for training centres in Sylhet. Before building it, she wants to understand whether coordinating class changes causes enough trouble to investigate further.

Her first plan is small enough to fit around other commitments:

- **People:** Managers of independent training centres who currently coordinate class changes by phone.
- **Assumption:** Some managers repeatedly lose time or miss students when a class changes.
- **Recruitment:** Invite eight qualifying managers to a short conversation through public business contacts and introductions. Record invitations and replies separately.
- **Test:** Ask each manager to describe the most recent class change, how they notified people, and what happened afterward. Ask permission to take notes. Do not collect student names or phone numbers.
- **Evidence:** Record recent incidents, time spent where they can recall it, current alternatives, and occasions when the current process worked well. Mark guesses as guesses.
- **Decision rule:** If at least three managers describe a recurring difficulty with a concrete consequence, plan a separate manual trial with interested managers. If most describe a satisfactory process, reconsider the problem or group. Too few replies or vague accounts leave the test inconclusive.
- **Limits:** Up to four hours and BDT 500 for calls or travel, ending seven days after starting.
- **First action and review:** The founder prepares an invitation today and reviews all notes at the end of day seven, even if recruitment falls short.

The counts, time, and budget above are **choices for this fictional test, not market benchmarks**. Choose limits you can afford and explain why your evidence rule would justify the next step. Three accounts would justify exploring this problem further for this founder; they would not measure the size of the market or establish willingness to pay.

With no travel budget, she could invite managers to phone conversations instead. If she can only work in the evening, she should offer times that also suit the managers. Adapt the method to both people's constraints.

## Hear the difference in a conversation

This short fictional exchange shows how to follow up without suggesting the answer. Read the manager's line aloud with a partner, then practise asking your own follow-up before looking at the example.

**Manager:** “It gets messy when the class time changes.”

**A leading follow-up:** “So you need automatic reminders?”

That puts your proposed solution into their answer. It leaves “messy” unexplained.

**A more useful follow-up:** “What happened the last time the class time changed?”

**Manager:** “I called everyone, but two students came at the old time.”

**Next question:** “Could you walk me through how you contacted them and what happened after they arrived?”

You now have an event to explore. You still need to learn how often it happens, how serious it is, and whether the current process is acceptable overall. Keep reports and your interpretation in separate notes. For a longer conversation, use the [customer interview scripts](/en/validation/interview-scripts).

## Check the plan before you use it

Read your answers as if a teammate had to run the test without asking you what you meant:

- Can they identify who qualifies and how to contact them?
- Does the test address one named uncertainty?
- Is the evidence an event or action they can record, rather than “people liked it”?
- Have you written what would make you reconsider, as well as what would encourage you?
- Are the spending limit, end date, and review owner clear?
- Can someone decline without pressure, and have you avoided asking for unnecessary personal information?

For conversations, explain who you are, why you are asking, how long you expect it to take, and how you will use the notes. Ask permission before recording or quoting anyone. For a prototype or manual trial, explain what is available now and what is only planned. If you move on to taking money, first make the delivery and refund terms clear and check the requirements for your activity.

**A filled worksheet is not a quality score.** If a teammate cannot tell what you will do or what you would learn, revise that answer before beginning.

## Run the test, then make a smaller decision

Take your plan into the field. Keep the original decision rule so you can compare what you expected with what happened. Do not move the rule just to make the result look better.

At the review date, write down:

1. How many people you invited, how many replied, and how many took the relevant action.
2. What supports your assumption and what contradicts it.
3. What you still cannot tell from this test, including recruitment problems.
4. Whether to run another bounded test, change the assumption or group, or pause.

A result that changes your mind is useful. A result that leaves you unsure should say so. If there is enough evidence to try a manual version, choose that next test in [the demand-testing guide](/en/validation/demand-without-building). If you cannot yet name a specific customer problem, return to [I have an idea but no product](/en/journeys/idea-but-no-product).

### What the review could look like

In the fictional Sylhet example above, the founder invited eight managers. Five replied and four completed interviews within the four-hour allowance; she spent BDT 220 of the BDT 500 limit. Three described recurring missed notifications with concrete consequences. The fourth said their existing shared calendar worked well.

Her original rule supports exploring a manual trial with interested managers. It does **not** support “three quarters of training centres need our software”: this was a small, selectively recruited group, and no one bought anything.

Her review note is: “Investigate reminders for last-minute changes. Keep the satisfied manager's workflow as counterevidence. Ask interested managers whether they will try a clearly scoped manual process; record acceptance, actual use and any payment separately. Stop adding software features until that test teaches us more.”

If only one manager had replied, the result would instead be inconclusive. She would review recruitment and availability before interpreting silence as lack of a problem.

**You are done with today's planning task when** another person can read your plan and tell you whom you will approach, what you will do, what you will record, and when you will review it. The next step is the first real invitation in your plan.
