# Growth: When to Expand and What to Test

> **In short:** Before opening a new area or increasing sales spend, understand what already works and what will change. Test the new demand and delivery costs at a scale you can afford, then decide whether the results justify a larger commitment.

You have customers and want the business to grow. That might mean selling more to the same group, reaching customers in another city or entering another country. Each option adds work and spending, but not necessarily in the same places. A new city may need a different delivery arrangement; an existing customer may simply need a reliable reason to buy again.

This section is for deciding which expansion is worth attempting and how to learn before committing too much. If customers are still not using or paying for the product, return to [validation](/en/validation) first. A larger launch will not answer why the current offer is failing.

## Find what is limiting the business now

Look at what happens after a customer first buys. Can you deliver what was promised? Do people return when they have the need again? Does the sale leave enough to help pay the rest of the business's bills? Weakness in any of these can become more expensive as volume rises.

**Retention** describes whether customers return over a defined period. For a monthly service, that could mean continuing to use or pay for it next month. For a product bought less often, the relevant period will differ. Comparing [cohort retention](/en/metrics/cohort-retention), where customers who started together are followed over time, can help you see whether recent customers are staying. More new signups can otherwise hide a growing number leaving.

If delivery and repeat use are working but you cannot reach enough suitable customers, a new area or sales channel may be worth testing. If sales are rising while cash gets tighter, first check [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit). The problem may be paying for stock long before collections arrive, which another launch could make worse.

## Work out what changes in the new area

Your current selling price does not guarantee the same result elsewhere. Delivery, promotion, staffing and support may cost more. Customers may buy differently or need something you have not had to provide before.

Variable costs change with sales or activity, such as goods and delivery costs. Fixed costs, such as an agreed monthly rent, do not change with each extra order within the period you are considering. A useful first comparison is **contribution**: sales revenue minus the variable costs included in the calculation. It shows what is left to cover fixed costs, rather than final profit. Suppose a product earns BDT 1,000 per completed order. In your existing area, the specified variable costs are BDT 700, leaving **1,000 − 700 = BDT 300**. In a proposed second city, higher delivery and customer-acquisition costs bring those costs to BDT 850, leaving **BDT 150**.

These are illustrative figures, not local price benchmarks. They show why copying the old sales target would be misleading: each completed order in the new city contributes only half as much under these assumptions. Include the costs of failed deliveries and other losses where relevant, rather than calculating only from the successful orders.

## Use a small launch to answer what remains uncertain

You might now try one customer group in the second city, with a spending limit and enough delivery capacity to keep your promise. Set the period around how long customers need to buy and receive the product. Decide who will handle problems and when you will review the result.

In the example, suppose the test produces 20 completed orders at BDT 150 contribution each. That is **20 × 150 = BDT 3,000**. If setting up the test cost BDT 5,000, its result after that setup cost is **3,000 − 5,000 = −BDT 2,000**, before other business costs. The test has cost money. That does not by itself prove expansion cannot work, because some setup work may not recur. It does mean you need to understand how much further sales would contribute, what additional costs they would create and whether customers would return.

Review delivery failures and complaints alongside the money. Also check when collections actually reach you: a sale can contribute on paper while leaving you short of cash for the next batch. Continue only if the next commitment fits what you learned and what you can afford. Where a result is weak, investigate the cause before repeating the same launch at a larger scale.
