# City Launch Playbook

> **In short:** Test one customer group in one small zone before launching across a city. Check demand, delivery, support and cash together. Set a limit on what you can spend and a date to decide. Early orders are useful evidence, but they do not prove that the service works at full scale.

## Copy the city-test brief

Use this when your startup already serves customers and is considering another city. If the offer has not worked anywhere yet, you are still testing the business itself. A new city adds more unknowns.

```text
City, test zone and customer group:
Problem and offer we will take there:
What already works in our current location:
What may be different here:

Local research and people we still need to meet:
Supply, delivery, payment and support plan:
What we will not offer during the test:
Local owner and backup:

Setup cost:
Weekly fixed costs:
Cash needed while waiting for customers to pay:
Protected cash we will not use:
Maximum cash we can put at risk:

Test start and end:
Customer result and service measures:
Cost and cash measures:
Stop rules:
Decision: continue / change and retest / stop
Who decides and when:
```

Write the limits before booking a long lease or hiring a large team. A city name on your website does not create a working service.

## Choose a city for a reason you can test

Look for the same customer problem, a way to reach buyers and a workable route to serve them. Do not choose only because a city has many people or a founder has family there.

Compare a few places using questions you can check. Can you reach the buyers? Can supplies arrive on time? Who handles problems locally? What would it cost to run a small test?

In a 2019 Future Startup interview, Deligram's Waiz Rahim described piloting in Cumilla before a wider rollout. The team tested how local shops, orders and delivery would work together.[^deligram-city] It is a dated account of a learning process, not evidence that Cumilla is the best launch city today.

## Visit before copying the old plan

Talk to users, payers, possible partners and people using other options. Watch the task in the intended area. Include buyers who are hard to reach through your existing network.

PriyoShop's Dipty Mandal described checking local shop demand and gaps before entering an area.[^priyoshop-city] Apply the idea without assuming all cities behave alike.

Use this short checklist on the ground:

| Question | Evidence to bring back |
|---|---|
| Who has the problem now? | Recent examples from the intended buyers |
| Why would they change? | Current option, cost and an unmet need |
| How do goods or work reach them? | A tested route and timing |
| How will money reach us? | Actual payment method and expected delay |
| Who handles a failure? | Named local owner and a working support route |
| What must be checked before operating? | Relevant site, product, licence, tax and contract requirements for this setup |

The last row needs the right local or professional help. This page does not decide which permissions a new office, warehouse or product requires.

## Test a zone you can serve well

Choose a clear area, offer and set of hours. State those limits to customers before they commit. Do not advertise city-wide service while silently refusing distant orders.

Keep the first offer narrow enough to learn. If you change the product, customer group, price, channel and city at once, a poor result will be hard to explain.

Run the whole path: first contact, order, payment, delivery or use, support and any return. A launch test that stops at signups skips much of the work.

Use a small paid order or service where appropriate. Do not take payments for capacity you cannot provide. If the test must stop, keep enough cash and time to finish or resolve existing customer promises.

## Plan the cash gap before the first order

List costs in three parts: setup, repeated fixed costs and money tied up while waiting for payment. Fixed costs are bills that remain even if few orders arrive, such as a local coordinator's agreed pay.

Shoumik Shahriar's cash-runway guidance stresses understanding money in and out and planning for weaker results.[^shoumik-city-cash] For a city test, also place the payments on a weekly calendar. A profitable order can still need cash before the buyer or courier pays you.

### Worked cash plan

Imagine a Dhaka startup testing packing-supply deliveries to small brands in one part of Rajshahi. All figures and results are invented for this guide.

The business has BDT 250,000 available after existing obligations. It protects BDT 130,000 for the core business. That leaves **BDT 120,000** it can risk on the city test.

The plan needs BDT 20,000 for setup, BDT 15,000 a week in fixed costs for four weeks, and BDT 30,000 to cover the stock-to-payment gap.

```text
Planned cash need = 20,000 + (15,000 × 4) + 30,000
                  = BDT 110,000
Headroom within the limit = 120,000 - 110,000 = BDT 10,000
```

The BDT 30,000 is a timing allowance, not a second charge for goods already included in order costs. Check it against the actual weekly cash forecast. Do not add the same purchase twice.

If late payments double the gap to BDT 60,000, the plan needs BDT 140,000. That exceeds the BDT 120,000 limit. The team must change the order pace or payment arrangement before starting, or choose a smaller test. Hoping that buyers will pay faster is not a cash plan.

## Check whether enough orders can cover the local costs

Suppose an average completed order brings BDT 600 in sales and uses BDT 520 in direct product, packing, payment and delivery costs. The amount left is **BDT 80 per order**. This is contribution: money left to help cover fixed costs, not net profit.

At BDT 15,000 in weekly fixed costs, the test needs **15,000 / 80 = 187.5**, or at least **188 such orders a week**, to cover those fixed costs. This assumes the same average order and costs. It does not yet recover setup costs or cover unlisted central overhead.

For the sales example below, assume the team has capped new stock orders and checked that the revised cash plan fits its limit. The four-week test produces these made-up results:

| Week | Completed orders | Contribution at BDT 80 each |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 90 | BDT 7,200 |
| 2 | 130 | BDT 10,400 |
| 3 | 160 | BDT 12,800 |
| 4 | 140 | BDT 11,200 |
| **Total** | **520** | **BDT 41,600** |

Four weeks of fixed cost total BDT 60,000. The test is **BDT 18,400 short** before setup costs. Including the BDT 20,000 setup gives a **BDT 38,400 loss on the listed costs**. The stock-to-payment timing allowance is not added again as an expense.

The team has learned that early demand exists, but the current volume does not cover the local model. It should not open a second zone just to show growth. It can test a lower fixed cost, denser route or better order mix, if the evidence supports the change and cash remains within the limit.

## Track service and repeat use too

Keep one weekly view of orders, distinct customers, delivery on time, payment received, unresolved cases and direct costs. Count repeat customers separately from total orders. Decide when a customer has had a fair chance to reorder based on the product's real buying cycle.

If you subsidize delivery or use free founder time, show that cost. A model that works only when a founder drives every parcel may still be a useful test, but you have not yet shown that a local team can run it.

Do not let good sales hide late refunds, unpaid suppliers or unresolved customer problems. Those belong in the decision too.

## Decide at the agreed review

Choose one path with a written reason:

- **Continue:** the full service works, the economics have a credible path and the cash plan fits.
- **Change and retest:** one specific issue can be tested within a new limit and end date.
- **Stop:** finish open duties, handle customers fairly and recover or close the remaining stock and costs.

Keep permits, leases, employment and partner terms under the right review before making the next commitment. A successful four-week test does not remove those duties.

- [ ] One zone and buyer group are clear.
- [ ] Local research checked the important assumptions.
- [ ] Orders, delivery, support and payment work end to end.
- [ ] Fixed costs and cash delays fit a protected limit.
- [ ] Results count completed work and real costs.
- [ ] Existing customers can still be served if the test stops.
- [ ] A named person has made the next decision.

Allow time for local visits and at least one full order-to-cash cycle. The four-week example is a teaching plan, not a universal launch schedule.
