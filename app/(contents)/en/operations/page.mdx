# Delivery, Returns and Order Operations

> **In short:** Follow an order until you know both what happened to the parcel and what happened to the money. Check the courier's service and charges for your actual deliveries, and keep returns and unsettled payments visible until they are resolved.

Once you start sending physical products to customers, selling and delivering become closely connected. A late parcel can become a cancelled order. A successful delivery can still leave you waiting for the courier's payment. More orders mean more of these details to handle, so relying on memory soon becomes difficult.

This section is for parcel-based businesses choosing couriers or improving the work between taking an order and closing it. If you deliver software or professional services instead, [product](/en/product) and [B2B sales](/en/b2b) are more relevant starting points.

## Check what you are promising before dispatch

The customer needs to know what will arrive, the amount to pay and when to expect it. Confirm the details that can prevent a delivery: the item and variant, contact number, usable address and availability where relevant. If an item is unavailable or the date changes, tell the customer before sending something different from what they agreed to buy.

Your courier choice needs to support that promise. Compare the routes you use, parcel size and weight, collection arrangements, delivery attempts and the way problems are reported. A low headline charge says little about a service that does not reliably reach your customers. Use the [courier directory](/en/directory/couriers) to make a shortlist, then confirm the current terms directly for your parcels. The [courier SLA scorecard](/en/operations/courier-sla-scorecard) helps you compare the service commitments and your observed results.

## Keep delivery and payment as separate questions

With **cash on delivery (COD)**, the customer pays when the parcel arrives and the courier later passes the collected money to you. That later transfer is **settlement**. A parcel marked delivered therefore does not necessarily mean its money is already in your account.

Record the order ID, dispatch and delivery outcome, expected collection, agreed charges and actual receipt together. When a parcel returns, record that too, inspect the goods and decide whether they can be sold again. A return produces no completed sale, but delivery and other costs may still remain. The [COD risk guide](/en/operations/cod-risk) shows how those costs affect the amount of returns your margin can absorb.

These distinctions matter when the courier pays several orders together. **Reconciliation** means matching the payment and its deductions to your own order records. Without that matching, a smaller bank receipt might look like missing money even when the courier has deducted a legitimate charge from another order.

## Understand a settlement before calling it short

Consider two illustrative orders worth BDT 1,000 each. Assume the seller pays BDT 80 outward delivery per order and a further BDT 40 when an order returns. These are teaching amounts, not a courier's current rates, and the simplified example assumes no other charges.

Order A is delivered, so you expect **1,000 − 80 = BDT 920** after its delivery charge. Order B returns, so there is no sale to collect and its delivery costs are **80 + 40 = BDT 120**. If the courier deducts B's charges from A's collection, the transfer would be **920 − 120 = BDT 800**.

A BDT 800 receipt could therefore be correct, but the arithmetic alone does not prove it. Check that the courier statement actually identifies those two orders and those deductions, and that the deduction method matches your terms. If it does not, keep the difference open and raise it with the order IDs and relevant records. The [COD reconciliation guide](/en/payments/cod-reconciliation) takes you through that matching work.

## Use recurring problems to improve the next batch

After resolving individual orders, look for a pattern. Repeated address failures call for different action from parcels damaged in transit or money arriving after the agreed date. Grouping the reasons helps you change the part of the process responsible, rather than asking everyone to “be more careful.”

Give unresolved cases to a named person and agree when to follow up. Tell customers what is known and what you are doing, without promising a delivery or refund that has not been confirmed. The [support scripts](/en/customers/support-scripts) can help you explain delays and next steps clearly.

Start with a group of recent orders you can trace from sale to delivery or return and payment. Once you can see where they stopped, choose the relevant guide below and fix that part of the process before adding more volume.
