# Cash on Delivery Risk: How Many Returns You Can Absorb

> **In short:** With cash on delivery, parcels come back — the question is how many your margin can absorb before profit hits zero, and the answer depends almost entirely on margin. A product that leaves BDT 308 per delivery stays profitable up to about 79% returns, while one that leaves BDT 34 breaks even at about 30%. Work out your own number with the calculator below, then decide which products keep COD and which need a deposit before dispatch.

## Who needs this, and when

You need this page if you sell with cash on delivery and want three answers: what a returned parcel actually costs, how many returns your margin can absorb, and which products should require a deposit.

You can skip it if you take payment in advance only, or if you already know your per-delivery profit, your per-return loss, and your real return rate.

## The two terms first

**Cash on delivery (COD)** means the customer pays the courier when the parcel arrives, and the courier later sends that money to you. **Return to origin (RTO)** is the share of dispatched parcels that come back unsold — the customer refused, was absent, or the address failed.

A return matters because you pay for the delivery anyway — the sale is zero.

## Where the cost hides

Many sellers count a returned parcel as one delivery charge. In reality a return costs money in at least two places even when the courier brings it back for free — the outbound delivery, which you still pay, and the packaging. If the goods are damaged or the packet opened, a third is added.

Even on a successful delivery, some costs do not show up plainly on a quote:

- **The cash-on-delivery charge.** The courier takes a percentage of the money it collects for you. Steadfast's pricing page states a 1% cash-on-delivery and risk-management charge.[^steadfast-pricing]
- **VAT.** The listed charges exclude VAT and tax, so the bill runs higher.
- **Parcel size.** A light item in a large box can still cost more.
- **Pick-and-drop minimum.** At Steadfast this is BDT 80.[^steadfast-pricing]

One more cost hides on a returned parcel: you pay the outbound delivery whether it is delivered or cancelled, and Steadfast adds no charge for the return leg itself — though other couriers do. So a return costs you delivery plus packaging, not delivery twice.

Delivery charges vary by route, weight, and size, so there is no single number worth memorising. Put your own route into the courier's calculator, then add the items above.

## How many returns you can absorb

You need two numbers: what you keep on each delivered order, and what you lose on each returned one.

> **Break-even RTO = profit per delivery ÷ (profit per delivery + loss per return)**

### A higher-margin product

Say a product sells for BDT 1,200 and costs BDT 800 to buy. Outbound delivery BDT 60, packaging BDT 20, and the COD charge 1% of 1,200, so BDT 12.

- Kept on delivery: 1,200 − 800 − 60 − 20 − 12 = **BDT 308**
- Lost on a return: outbound 60 + packaging 20 = **BDT 80** (Steadfast adds no return freight)
- Break-even RTO = 308 ÷ (308 + 80) = **about 79%**

Eight returns in ten and this product still does not lose money. With a healthy margin, COD is forgiving.

### A lower-margin product

Say the product sells for BDT 600 and costs BDT 480. The other costs barely change, because couriers do not lower their charge for cheaper goods. The COD charge is 1% of 600, so BDT 6.

- Kept on delivery: 600 − 480 − 60 − 20 − 6 = **BDT 34**
- Lost on a return: the same **BDT 80**
- Break-even RTO = 34 ÷ (34 + 80) = **about 30%**

> **Keep in mind:** The cost of a return does not shrink with the price of the item — the profit does. On cheap goods, one return in three wipes out the margin on the other two.

### The pattern across products

Add a middle price and the pattern is easier to see. For a product selling at BDT 800 and costing BDT 600, the COD charge is 1% of 800, so BDT 8. Kept on delivery is 800 − 600 − 60 − 20 − 8 = **BDT 112**, the loss on a return is still **BDT 80**, and the break-even RTO is 112 ÷ (112 + 80) = **about 58%**.

The return loss stays flat at BDT 80 while the profit collapses, so the break-even rate falls with it. Cash on delivery is most dangerous on inexpensive products — and inexpensive products are exactly where customers ask for it most.

| Selling price | Kept per delivery | Lost per return | Break-even RTO |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| BDT 1,200 | 308 | 80 | ~79% |
| BDT 800 | 112 | 80 | ~58% |
| BDT 600 | 34 | 80 | ~30% |

> **Warning:** Below some price the maths goes negative before any return. When per-delivery profit is zero or less, every order loses money even with no returns at all. The calculator below will show you this for your own numbers.

## Bringing returns down

Once you know the break-even rate, measure your actual one. The courier's report shows parcels dispatched and returned; divide once a month and you have the rate.

Start with a confirmation message before dispatch. Copy and adapt this:

```text
Hi, this is [your shop name]. Your order: [item], BDT [price], delivery BDT [amount].
It should reach you by [day]. Reply 1 to confirm, or 2 to cancel.
```

Then the things that help:

- **No answer, no dispatch.** After two failed attempts to reach the customer, hold the order.
- **A partial deposit.** Taking a deposit equal to the delivery charge removes most fake orders.
- **Verify the address.** Record the area, a landmark, and an alternative number.
- **Keep photos and measurements accurate.** Customers refuse parcels when the item does not match expectations.
- **Require deposits on cheap items.** Tighten the terms exactly where the break-even rate is low.
- **Keep a list of repeat refusers.** Do not dispatch to them again without a deposit.

Sellers hesitate over the deposit, fearing lost sales. Rather than applying it to every order at once, switch it on for low-margin products first and compare the rate after a month.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Counting a return as one delivery charge | Return freight and packaging never enter the calculation |
| One COD policy for every product | Low-margin items quietly lose money |
| Ignoring the COD charge and VAT | The bill exceeds the quote |
| Never measuring your own RTO rate | The problem surfaces months late |
| Dispatching to customers who never answer | The return rate rises and you pay freight both ways |
| Setting prices without allowing for returns | Margin exists on paper but never in hand |

## Checklist

- [ ] Worked out profit per delivery and loss per return for each product
- [ ] Calculated the break-even RTO for each product
- [ ] Know last month's actual RTO rate
- [ ] Included the COD charge and VAT in costs
- [ ] Have a written order-confirmation rule
- [ ] Set a deposit rule for low-margin products
- [ ] Keep a list of numbers that repeatedly refuse delivery

> **Rule of thumb:** where the break-even RTO is under about 30%, do not dispatch without a deposit.
