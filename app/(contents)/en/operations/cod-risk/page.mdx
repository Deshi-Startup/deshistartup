# Cash on Delivery Risk: How Many Returns You Can Absorb

> **Key Takeaway:** Returns are unavoidable with cash on delivery, so the question is not whether parcels come back but how many you can absorb. The answer depends almost entirely on margin. A product earning BDT 400 of gross margin stays profitable up to roughly 69% returns; one earning BDT 120 breaks even at about 20%. Work out your own figure, then decide which products keep cash on delivery and which ones require a deposit.

## Where the Cost Hides

Many sellers count a returned parcel as one delivery charge. In reality a return costs money in at least three places — sending it, bringing it back, and the packaging. If the goods are damaged or the packet has been opened, a fourth is added.

Even on a successful delivery, some costs do not show up plainly on a quote:

- **The cash-on-delivery charge.** The courier takes a percentage of the money it collects for you. Steadfast's pricing page states that a 1% cash-on-delivery and risk-management charge applies.[^steadfast-pricing]
- **VAT.** The same page notes that the charges listed exclude VAT and tax, so the bill runs higher.
- **Parcel size.** A light item in a large box can still cost more.
- **Pick-and-drop minimum.** At Steadfast this is BDT 80.[^steadfast-pricing]

Delivery charges vary by route, weight, and size, so there is no single number worth memorising. Put your own route into the courier's calculator, then add the four items above.

## How Many Returns You Can Absorb

The share of dispatched parcels that come back unsold is called RTO — return to origin. The question is the rate at which profit reaches zero.

You need two numbers. What you keep on each delivered order, and what you lose on each returned one.

> Break-even RTO = profit per delivery ÷ (profit per delivery + loss per return)

### A higher-margin product

Sells for BDT 1,200, costs BDT 800. Outbound delivery BDT 60, packaging BDT 20, cash-on-delivery charge 1% of 1,200 = BDT 12.

- Kept on delivery: 1,200 − 800 − 60 − 20 − 12 = **BDT 308**
- Lost on a return: outbound 60 + return 60 + packaging 20 = **BDT 140**
- Break-even RTO = 308 ÷ (308 + 140) = 308 ÷ 448 = **about 69%**

Six returns in ten and this product is still not losing money. With a healthy margin, cash on delivery is fairly forgiving.

### A lower-margin product

Sells for BDT 600, costs BDT 480. The other costs barely change, because couriers do not lower their charge for cheaper goods. The cash-on-delivery charge is 1% of 600 = BDT 6.

- Kept on delivery: 600 − 480 − 60 − 20 − 6 = **BDT 34**
- Lost on a return: the same **BDT 140**
- Break-even RTO = 34 ÷ (34 + 140) = 34 ÷ 174 = **about 20%**

This is the whole point. The cost of a return does not shrink with the price of the item, but the profit does. On cheap goods, one return in five wipes out the margin on the other four.

Put the two calculations side by side and the decision becomes clear: cash on delivery is most dangerous on inexpensive products — and inexpensive products are exactly where customers ask for it most.

## Bringing Returns Down

Once you know the break-even rate, measure your actual one. The courier's report shows parcels dispatched and returned. Divide once a month and you have the rate. Then the things that help:

- **A confirmation rule.** Before dispatch, confirm price, delivery charge, and expected day by call or message.
- **No answer, no dispatch.** After two failed attempts to reach the customer, hold the order.
- **A partial deposit.** Taking a deposit equal to the delivery charge removes most fake orders.
- **Verify the address.** Record the area, a landmark, and an alternative number.
- **Keep photos and measurements accurate.** Customers refuse parcels when the item does not match expectations.
- **Require deposits on cheap items.** Tighten the terms exactly where the break-even rate is low.
- **Keep a list of repeat refusers.** Do not dispatch to them again without a deposit.

Sellers hesitate over the third, fearing lost sales. Rather than applying it to every order at once, switch it on for low-margin products first and compare the rate after a month.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Counting a return as one delivery charge | Return freight and packaging never enter the calculation |
| One cash-on-delivery policy for every product | Low-margin items quietly lose money |
| Ignoring the COD charge and VAT | The bill exceeds the quote |
| Never measuring your own RTO rate | The problem surfaces months late |
| Dispatching to customers who never answer | The return rate rises and you pay freight both ways |
| Setting prices without allowing for returns | Margin exists on paper but never in hand |

## Checklist

- [ ] Worked out profit per delivery and loss per return for each product
- [ ] Calculated the break-even RTO for each product
- [ ] Know last month's actual RTO rate
- [ ] Included the cash-on-delivery charge and VAT in costs
- [ ] Have a written order-confirmation rule
- [ ] Set a deposit rule for low-margin products
- [ ] Keeping a list of numbers that repeatedly refuse delivery

Rule of thumb: **where the break-even RTO is under 25%, do not dispatch without a deposit.**
