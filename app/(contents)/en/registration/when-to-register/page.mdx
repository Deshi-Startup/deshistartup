# When to Register a Company

> **In short:** Is customer money arriving regularly? Does a contract or bank account need to be in the company's name? Does a co-founder need real shares, or is an investor conversation moving forward? Any one of those is a sign to think about registering. There's no rush while you're still validating an idea or talking to your first few customers. Once you register, RJSC filing and accounting obligations start every year whether you're generating revenue or not. Registering before you've actually understood the market yet sends both money and time into paperwork instead of the business.

Do you rush to RJSC in your first week, or wait until you have ten paying customers? Founders genuinely do both, and each choice carries its own risk. This guide doesn't hand you a fixed number of weeks or months, because the right timing depends on the kind of business. Instead, it names the events that should start the registration clock.

[Which structure to register under](/company-types) is a separate question from this page. This one is only about timing.

---

## The Question Isn't "Whether," It's "When"

Almost every business that grows eventually registers. So the real question was never yes or no — it's timing. Register too early and you carry yearly paperwork and fees for an idea that hasn't proven itself yet. Wait too long and you lose credibility with customers, banks, or potential investors, and any contracts or money already sitting under your personal name take extra work to move into the company's. The signals below are how you tell where that middle ground actually sits.

---

## Signs It's Time to Think About Registering

One signal by itself doesn't mean you need to file with RJSC today. But when two or three of these line up together, waiting longer starts to cost you.

- **Regular customer money is arriving in meaningful amounts.** An occasional order and steady monthly revenue are different situations. Once the second one starts, keeping personal and business accounts separate stops being optional.
- **A contract needs the company's name on it.** Corporate clients and larger suppliers often want a registered entity's name, not an individual's. Once a contract like that is on the table, there's usually little room left to wait.
- **The business needs a bank account or payment gateway in its own name.** Once business money mixes into a personal account, untangling it for accounting and tax later gets messy.
- **A co-founder needs real shares.** Calling someone a "50:50 partner" out loud and actually recording shares are two different things. Shares only exist inside a company structure — a verbal understanding doesn't create them.
- **An investor or fund conversation is moving forward.** Investment is made against company shares, not against an individual. It's better to register before that conversation starts than to fall behind it meeting by meeting.
- **You're hiring real staff, not just asking someone you know for occasional help.** Salary, leave, and responsibility are safer to handle under a registered entity's name.
- **A sector license needs to sit under the company's name.** Fintech, education, health, or import/export approvals often require a registered entity rather than an individual applicant.

---

## What Registering Early Actually Costs

It's a mistake to treat registration as a one-time expense. Once you're registered as a company, RJSC filing and return obligations continue every year, whether you're selling anything or not.[^rjsc-guidelines] That means accounting or filing costs keep running even against zero revenue.

So if you're still at the stage of talking to customers to validate an idea, there's no rush to register. Keeping learning notes and tracking your first sales matters more right now. Closing a company down isn't as simple as opening one, either — it takes its own paperwork, fees, and time. Registering on a "why not, we might need it later" basis is a decision that's hard to walk back.

## Trade License, e-TIN, and Company Registration: Three Different Steps

Many people assume starting a business always means registering a company with RJSC. In practice, a [trade license](/trade-license) plus your own <Term name="e-tin">e-TIN</Term> can carry a solo or small-scale business a long way — without RJSC private-limited registration at all. The reverse assumption is also wrong: holding a <Term name="trade-license">trade license</Term> alone doesn't make you a company. A trade license is local-authority permission to operate; RJSC registration creates a separate legal entity. Which one you actually need right now depends on your ownership structure — see [sole proprietorship vs. private limited](/company-types) for that comparison.

## A Quick Decision Checklist

If more than two or three of these are true, think about registering now. If they're all false, there's no rush yet.

- [ ] Regular customer money has come in over the last month or two
- [ ] A client or supplier has asked for a contract in the company's name
- [ ] The business needs a bank account or payment gateway of its own
- [ ] A co-founder discussion about share ownership is underway
- [ ] An investor conversation about funding has started
- [ ] You're hiring real staff, not just asking a friend for occasional help
- [ ] A sector license needs the company's name on it

Rule of thumb: **Hold off on registering while you're still validating the idea. Once money, contracts, or ownership genuinely start being shared, don't wait any longer.**
