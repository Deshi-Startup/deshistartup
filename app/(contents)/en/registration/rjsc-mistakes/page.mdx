# The RJSC Mistakes That Get Expensive Later

> **In short:** When an application stalls, most founders assume there's a typo in the paperwork. A misspelling, a missing signature, or share numbers that don't match will indeed get a query from RJSC — you fix the document, resubmit, and lose time rather than money. The genuinely expensive mistakes aren't errors at all; they're decisions: which name you took, how much authorised capital you declared, and how narrowly you wrote the company's objects in the MoA. Changing that objects clause later needs a court order, not just a form.[^companies-act-memorandum] And the day the certificate lands, several clocks start running — 14 days, 28 days, 60 days — that nobody reminds you about.[^companies-act-filings] So before you file, ask whether those three decisions will still hold three years from now.

Name clearance and the registration steps are not this page's job — those are in [Name clearance](/registration/name-clearance) and [Private limited company registration](/registration/private-limited). This page covers the mistake and what it costs.

---

## The name: clearance isn't the end of the risk

Assuming that RJSC's clearance makes a name safe is wrong in two directions.

Section 11 of the Companies Act 1994 says no company may be registered by a name identical to that of an existing registered company, or so nearly resembling it that the name could likely be used to deceive.[^companies-act-name] The load-bearing word is "resembling." A name can be blocked without being an exact match — and a question can surface even after clearance.

The second direction is less well known. If a company does get registered under such a resembling name, through inadvertence or otherwise, it must change its name within 120 days on the Registrar's direction.[^companies-act-name] Failing to comply carries a fine of 500 taka per day for the company and 100 taka per day for every officer in default.[^companies-act-name] The name risk, in other words, does not fully close when the certificate arrives.

And the cost of a name change is never just RJSC's fee. Signage, packaging, the domain, bank paperwork, the trade licence, invoice books — all of it changes with it. Ten extra minutes choosing the name is the cheap option.

There is also a formal way to ask. On payment of the prescribed fee, a company may apply to the Registrar for information on whether a specified name is registered or proposed to be registered, and the Registrar must respond within 30 days.[^companies-act-name]

Separately, where the Government declares a name undesirable by Gazette notification, no company may be registered under it without the Government's prior written consent.[^companies-act-name] Names or abbreviations of the United Nations or the World Health Organisation cannot be used in any form without specific authorisation.[^companies-act-name]

## Authorised capital: "let's declare more, we'll grow into it"

This is the most common expensive mistake, and the easiest to avoid.

Many founders think a larger authorised capital makes the company look bigger. What it actually does is raise the government fee directly. Share capital fee is zero up to 10 lakh taka; above that a fee accrues per lakh, and the stamp on the AoA moves up a tier as well. The full tiers and arithmetic are in [RJSC fees and timelines](/registration/rjsc-fees).

Since this isn't money you have to deposit anywhere, "what's the harm in declaring more" feels reasonable. The harm is that you are paying a fee on a number you may not reach in three years. The Act provides for increasing it later, and you pay only on the increase when you make it.

The mistake usually comes from conflating authorised, issued, and paid-up capital. All three are explained separately in [Share capital, directors, and shareholders](/registration/shares-and-directors).

## The objects clause: the one mistake that ends up in court

This is the single place where the correction path stops being easy.

The MoA states what the company is set up to do. Many founders write only today's business — say, "web development services." Two years on, when the same company wants to sell a software product or start a training business, the objects clause has to change.

Section 12 of the Companies Act requires a special resolution to alter the objects — but that alone is not enough. Sub-section (2) is explicit: the alteration does not take effect until confirmed by the Court on petition.[^companies-act-memorandum] The Court must be satisfied that sufficient notice went to debenture holders and to anyone whose interests would be affected, and that objecting creditors have either consented or had their claims discharged or secured.[^companies-act-memorandum]

So the price of one badly written line is a lawyer, a court, and several months. Writing the clause far wider than you need isn't the answer either — a bank or an investor reading it should be able to tell what the company actually does. Getting that balance right is a conversation to have with a corporate lawyer or company secretary while the documents are being drafted. What goes into the MoA and AoA is covered in [Memorandum and Articles of Association](/registration/moa-aoa).

## The registered office: an address does more than hold your desk

The registered office is where official letters and notices are sent. A home address can work, but two things matter.

The Act requires a company to have a registered office from the day it begins business or from the twenty-eighth day after incorporation, whichever comes first.[^companies-act-office] Notice of the address, or of any change to it, must reach the Registrar within 28 days.[^companies-act-office] Carrying on business without complying carries a fine of up to 200 taka per day.[^companies-act-office]

Second, the trade licence has to come from the city corporation or municipality that covers this address. Putting down a convenient address at registration and then moving the office to another area means redoing paperwork in three places at once — the trade licence, the bank, and RJSC.

## Document mismatches: they cost time, not money

RJSC sends a query when documents don't line up. The usual causes are familiar:

- name spellings or parents' names that don't match the NID or passport
- share numbers that read three different ways across the MoA, the AoA, and the subscriber page
- an address written one way on one document and another way elsewhere
- a missing photo, signature, or date

None of this is worth panicking over, but there are two traps. First, leaving a query unanswered lets the delay grow on its own. Second, many founders respond to a query by rebuilding the entire application, when fixing the specific point RJSC flagged would have been enough. If there's a foreign shareholder, have the encashment certificate paperwork ready in advance — details in [Starting a foreign-owned company](/registration/foreign-owned-company).

## The clocks that start the day the certificate is issued

The quietest mistake is treating registration as the finish line. Several deadlines begin the moment the certificate is issued.

| What's due | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Return of the first directors' particulars | 14 days[^companies-act-filings] |
| Notice of the registered office address | 28 days[^companies-act-office] |
| Return of any new share allotment | 60 days[^companies-act-filings] |
| First list of members and first AGM | 18 months[^companies-act-filings] |

The full calendar and the late penalties are in [The RJSC filing calendar](/registration/annual-returns). For the order in which TIN, trade licence, and the bank account come after the certificate, see [What to do after company registration](/registration/after-registration).

## What isn't actually a mistake

One belief causes trouble in the opposite direction: that a private limited company needs a separate "commencement certificate" before it can start trading.

The Act is clear here. The restriction on commencing business does exist, in section 150 — but sub-section (6) states that nothing in that section applies to a private company.[^companies-act-commencement] A private limited company can start work as soon as it has the Certificate of Incorporation. If someone quotes you a fee for this step, ask which section it comes from.

Likewise, the Certificate of Incorporation is itself conclusive evidence in law that the Act's registration requirements were complied with.[^companies-act-commencement] If anyone later suggests the registration wasn't done properly, you have an answer.

## Checklist

- [ ] Searched separately for names that closely resemble other registered names
- [ ] Set authorised capital from real need, and checked it against the fee tiers
- [ ] Wrote the objects clause against a three-year plan and had a professional review it
- [ ] Chose a registered office address that matches the trade licence area
- [ ] Reconciled names, addresses, and share numbers across the NID, MoA, AoA, and subscriber page
- [ ] Put the 14-, 28-, and 60-day dates on the calendar the day the certificate arrived
- [ ] Noted that an RJSC query means fixing the flagged point, not rebuilding the application

Rule of thumb: **At RJSC, a spelling mistake can be fixed; a decision mistake costs money and months. Read the name, the capital, and the objects clause one last time before you file.**
