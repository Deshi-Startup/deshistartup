# Certified Copies from RJSC: What You Can Get, and What You Can't

> **In short:** When a bank, investor, tender, or court asks for a "certified copy," they are not asking for a photocopy from your folder. They want a copy that RJSC itself has certified. Section 347 of the Companies Act 1994 gives that right to anyone: on payment of the prescribed fee, any person may inspect the documents kept by the Registrar, and any person may have a copy or extract of any document certified by the Registrar.[^companies-act-inspection] Because the right belongs to everyone, your competitor or a prospective investor can pull the same record. And there is the trap: RJSC can only certify what has actually been filed. If Form XII never went in after a director change, the certified copy still carries the old director's name. The good news is that a late document can still be filed on payment of the late fee.[^companies-act-enforcement] So before you go for copies, check what is actually in the file.

Which return is due when is covered separately in [The RJSC filing calendar](/registration/annual-returns). This page is about getting certified copies out of what has already been filed.

---

## What a certified copy actually is

A certified copy is a copy taken from RJSC's record, on which the Registrar's office certifies that it matches their document. The original certificate or a photocopy of the MoA in your own folder does not do this job, because nothing outside your office has verified it.

It usually gets asked for where the other side wants to see the government's record rather than take your word:

- opening a company bank account or applying for a loan
- an investor's due diligence
- bidding for a government or corporate tender
- proving the company's existence and ownership in court or arbitration
- applying for a licence or another registration
- replacing a lost or damaged original

Who asks for what varies by institution. Before going for copies, ask the other side exactly which document they need certified — otherwise you pay the fee twice.

---

## Who the Act gives this right to

Section 347(5) actually contains two separate rights.[^companies-act-inspection] First, on payment of the prescribed fee, any person may inspect the documents kept by the Registrar. Second, on payment of the fee, any person may require a copy or extract of any document, or part of one, to be certified by the Registrar.

Two things follow.

The right is not limited to directors or shareholders, so your accountant or lawyer can collect copies without you going in person.

For the same reason, your company's filed record is open to others. A prospective investor, a large buyer, a competitor, or a creditor can pay the fee and see who the directors are, how the shares are split, and when you last filed anything. Keeping your paperwork current is not only a compliance question — it is a negotiating one.

---

## What RJSC can certify

The answer is simple: only what has been filed with them. Over a company's life, these are the documents that reach RJSC's record:[^companies-act-filed-record]

- the Certificate of Incorporation
- the MoA and AoA
- notice of the registered office address
- the return of directors', managers', and their particulars — Form XII in RJSC's paperwork
- returns of share allotment
- the annual list of members and summary of share capital, i.e. Schedule X
- the audited balance sheet and profit and loss account

Read that list and the point becomes clear: a certified copy from RJSC is a mirror of your filing history. Get the filing right and the copy comes out right.

---

## The trap that surfaces during due diligence

The uncomfortable moment usually arrives just before an investment or a large contract.

Two years ago a co-founder left and a new director came in. Everyone knows this. But if Form XII was never filed, RJSC's record does not know it. The certified copy comes back printed with the old director's name, and the other side asks which version is true.

The same applies to shares. Without a return of allotment, the ownership split on paper stays where it was, however many board resolutions you produce.

The real point: RJSC's copy does not prove your claim — it proves your filing.

The good news is that the door doesn't close. Section 350 provides that any document or return required or authorised to be filed or registered under the Act may still be filed or registered after the time specified, on payment of the prescribed late fee — without prejudice to any other liability.[^companies-act-enforcement] So clear the backlog before due diligence starts, rather than balking at the late fee.

The pressure isn't one-directional either. Under section 349, where a company is in default and fails to make good within 14 days of being served notice, the Court may — on an application by a member, a creditor, or the Registrar — order the company and its officers to file within a specified time, with costs.[^companies-act-enforcement]

## What it costs

RJSC's fee schedule lists the certified copy and inspection items separately:[^rjsc-fees-schedule]

| Item | Fee |
|---|---:|
| Inspection of records | 100 taka |
| Copy of the incorporation certificate | 100 taka |
| Copy of the commencement certificate | 100 taka |
| Non-judicial stamp on the MoA, AoA, or another document, each | 20 taka |
| Court fee stamp, per application | 20 taka |
| Copying a document, per 100 words or part | 5 taka, minimum 100 taka per document |

Work an example. You need certified copies of two documents, the MoA and the AoA. At 1,200 words the MoA's copying charge comes to 60 taka, but the 100-taka minimum per document applies, so it counts as 100. A 4,000-word AoA comes to 200. Non-judicial stamps on two documents add 40, and the court fee stamp on the application adds 20. That is 360 taka in total.

The amount looks small, but the useful part is how it scales — with the length and the number of documents. So rather than pulling copies of everything, find out first which one was actually asked for.

Fees can change. Before paying, treat RJSC's current fee schedule or the amount on the portal's challan as final. The full table is in [RJSC fees and timelines](/registration/rjsc-fees).

## What you need when you apply

According to RJSC's own published process description, the application is made in a prescribed form, and on receipt of the application, stamp, and fee the office issues a certified copy of the records applied for.[^rjsc-process-brief] Have ready:

- the company name and RJSC registration number, exactly as they appear on the record
- which document you want copied, and from which year or which filing
- the applicant's identity and contact details
- the fee and stamps

After applying, the status of a certified copy application can be checked on RJSC's portal.[^rjsc-process-brief] RJSC publishes no fixed number of days for delivery. Don't apply the day before a bank or tender deadline — leave yourself weeks, not hours.

## Checklist

- [ ] Asked the other side exactly which document needs to be certified
- [ ] Checked whether that document has in fact been filed at RJSC
- [ ] Filed any pending return for a director, address, or share change first
- [ ] Cleared the backlog with the late fee where filings were overdue
- [ ] Estimated the cost from the number and length of the documents
- [ ] Wrote the company name and registration number as they appear on the record
- [ ] Applied at least a few weeks before the deadline
- [ ] Kept the copies and the challan in one folder

Rule of thumb: **A certified copy proves your filing, not your claim. Fix the file before you order copies, or you'll get old information back with a government seal on it.**
