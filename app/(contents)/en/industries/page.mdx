# Industry Guides: Understand the Business Behind the Product

> **In short:** Choose a sector by looking at the work customers need done and the people needed to deliver it. Before adapting a business model, follow one real purchase or service from the customer's first request through delivery and payment. That is where you find what your product must fit around.

A business idea can sound straightforward until you try to run it in a particular sector. A booking tool needs more than a calendar if the provider regularly cancels. A produce-ordering service needs more than orders if nobody can collect the goods before they spoil. The software may work while the service the customer paid for still fails.

Sector research helps you discover those dependencies before you build around the wrong assumption. You do not need to become an expert on an entire industry first. Begin with one kind of customer, one recurring task and the people involved in completing it.

## Follow the work, including the parts outside your product

Ask a potential customer to describe the last time they bought or used the service. Who made the request, who approved it, who did the work and when did money change hands? The person using a tool may not be the person who pays for it. If you interview only the user, you can understand the task well and still miss why a purchase is refused.

Then look at the hand-offs. In a fresh-produce collection service, for example, the grower, collection staff, transport provider and receiving buyer all affect whether an order arrives in usable condition. Together they form the **value chain**: the people and organisations through which the product moves before reaching its customer. An app can help them coordinate, but it cannot make missing transport capacity appear.

For each hand-off, find out who takes responsibility when it fails. A late notification and a spoiled delivery have different consequences, even if both begin with the same missed message. That difference affects what you must test, what customers expect you to cover and which partners you need before promising the service.

## Choose a starting point that matches the business

The available sector journeys bring together guides for the following situations:

- **Education:** [Build an edtech startup](/en/journeys/build-an-edtech-startup) when you need to connect a learning problem with the learner, the paying customer and a way to deliver the teaching.
- **Agriculture:** [Build an agritech startup](/en/journeys/build-an-agritech-startup) when production, collection, trading or another part of the agricultural value chain shapes the service.
- **Financial services:** [Build a fintech startup](/en/journeys/build-a-fintech-startup) when the proposed work involves money or a financial service. Establish the activity and its regulatory boundaries before a live test.
- **Local services:** [Build a local-services startup](/en/journeys/build-a-local-services-startup) when finding customers is only part of the problem and reliable service providers must also be available.
- **Travel:** [Build a travel-tech startup](/en/journeys/build-a-travel-tech-startup) when bookings depend on partners, availability and what happens if the trip changes.

These are ways to investigate a business, not rankings of attractive sectors. A large market can still be difficult for your team to reach, while a smaller customer group may have a problem you understand and can serve well.

## Bring the sector research back to your first test

After following a recent transaction, write a short account of it in ordinary language. Include who wanted the result, who paid, who supplied it, what went wrong and what the customer did next. Separate what you heard from what you still need to check. “The buyer pays after inspection” is different from “we assume payment arrives on delivery.” That difference matters when you have to pay a supplier first.

Choose the uncertainty that would most change your plan. If transport is the constraint, investigate the route and available capacity before testing another ordering screen. If the buyer is unclear, find the person who approves spending. [Customer interviews](/en/validation/customer-interviews) can help with those conversations; [market research](/en/ideas) can help you check the size and alternatives once the customer is specific.

Also describe the activity accurately when checking [licenses and approvals](/en/licenses). Supplying software to a business and performing its regulated work yourself are different proposals. Do not assume that calling either one a technology startup settles the requirements.
