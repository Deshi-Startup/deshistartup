# Islamic Startup Financing: Murabaha, Mudaraba, and Revenue-Sharing

> **In short:** Shariah-compliant funding replaces interest with trade and partnership. For inventory and equipment, Murabaha lets a financier buy the asset and resell it to you at a disclosed markup; for working capital, Mudaraba investors take a share of revenue until a capped return; for venture rounds, ordinary equity works. Avoid interest-bearing convertible debt, late fees that profit the lender, and guaranteed returns. Start by itemising what you are buying — Murabaha needs a real asset, not cash.

Imagine Asif, founder of an organic pantry and cold-pressed oil brand in Sylhet. The winter harvest is coming. He needs BDT 15 Lakh to buy bulk mustard seeds, glass bottles, and a commercial oil-expeller machine.

Asif and his co-founders follow Islamic financial principles. They will not take an interest-bearing bank loan. But handing an angel a permanent 20% stake, just to buy one season of seeds, is worse: the seeds get used up in four months and the stake lasts forever.

So how does a faith-conscious founder raise capital that is neither interest nor equity?

This guide covers the principles, the three instruments, what each one really costs, and the clauses that quietly break compliance in an otherwise standard contract.

---

## Who needs this, and when

| Situation | Recommended Instrument | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Inventory, raw materials, or equipment purchase** | **Murabaha (Cost-Plus Sale)** | The financier buys the physical asset from the supplier and sells it to you on deferred instalments with a disclosed, fixed profit markup — no interest.[^aaoifi-shariah] |
| **Institutional venture fundraising** | **Musharaka (Equity / Common Shares)** | Ordinary equity is accepted as Shariah-compliant in mainstream rulings because the investor shares real commercial risk and reward — provided the business stays in permissible sectors and no return is guaranteed.[^bsec-alternative-rules] |
| **Seasonal working capital from retail syndicates** | **Mudaraba / Revenue-Share Notes** | Investors take a fixed percentage of monthly revenue until an agreed profit cap is reached — commonly 1.12x–1.25x of their money on the platforms operating in Bangladesh.[^biniyog-microsyndicate] |
| **Businesses in restricted sectors** | Ineligible | Conventional banking, gambling, tobacco, alcohol, adult entertainment, and speculative derivatives cannot receive Shariah-compliant financing.[^bb-islamic-guidelines] |

Read the table by what you are *buying*, not by how much you need. That is the split that matters here. If the money turns into a thing you can point at — seeds, bottles, a machine, a delivery van — Murabaha fits, and it costs you no ownership. If the money disappears into salaries and server bills for three years, no asset sale can carry it, and you are looking at equity.

You can skip this guide if your business is comfortable with conventional bank debt or standard interest-bearing convertible notes.

## The question everyone asks first: is a markup just interest with a new name?

Asif's brother-in-law puts it bluntly over dinner: *"The bank wants 15 lakh back plus 1.8 lakh. A moneylender wants 15 lakh back plus 1.8 lakh. You have changed the word, not the transaction."*

It is the right question, and a guide that skips it is not worth reading. Here is the answer in the shape this site uses for hard ideas.

### 1. Name

**Riba** — usually translated as "interest", literally "increase".

### 2. Plain meaning

Money that grows only because time passed, while nobody carries any commercial risk.[^bb-islamic-guidelines]

### 3. Asif's two contracts, side by side

Under a **conventional loan**, the bank puts BDT 15,00,000 into Asif's account. The bank never sees a mustard seed. Its return grows month by month whether the seeds arrive, rot in a truck, or never ship at all.

Under a **Murabaha**, the financier buys the seeds, bottles, and expeller from the supplier for BDT 15,00,000 and — for that moment — owns them. Then it sells them to Asif for BDT 16,80,000, payable over twelve months.[^aaoifi-shariah]

### 4. The four tests that separate them

1. **What is being sold?** The use of money, or actual goods. A Murabaha is a sale contract, not a loan contract.[^aaoifi-shariah]
2. **Who loses if the warehouse burns down before handover?** Under a loan, Asif still owes every taka. Under a Murabaha, the financier owned the goods at that moment and eats the loss. That ownership risk is the whole justification for the markup.[^aaoifi-shariah]
3. **Does the number grow if Asif pays late?** A loan balance keeps compounding. A Murabaha price is fixed at BDT 16,80,000 and stays there — late or not. This is why a compliant contract cannot let the financier keep late fees as income.[^bb-islamic-guidelines]
4. **Can the debt be rolled into a new, bigger one?** Interest debt can be rolled forever. A Murabaha price cannot be renegotiated upward.

Test 3 is the one Asif can verify on a statement rather than argue about at dinner. If the amount owed is different in month fourteen than it was in month twelve, whatever he signed was not a Murabaha.

> **Keep in mind:** Scholars are not unanimous that every product marketed as Murabaha is clean. The common criticism is "synthetic Murabaha", where the bank never genuinely acquires the goods — the paperwork says sale, the substance is a loan. Both AAOIFI's standards and Bangladesh Bank's Islamic banking guidelines require real acquisition, so the gap is in practice, not in the rules.

### 5. What Asif does now

Ask two questions in writing, before signing:

- *"Who pays my supplier — you directly, or do you transfer cash to me?"*
- *"If I pay three months late, what is the total I owe, and where does any late charge go?"*

If the first answer is "we credit your account" or the second answer is a bigger number that the financier keeps, the contract is not what its cover page claims. The safe correction for both is in [Common pitfalls](#common-pitfalls-and-shariah-traps) below.

## The minimum mental model: four rules every contract must pass

Whatever the instrument is called, four criteria have to hold under AAOIFI standards and Bangladesh Bank's Islamic banking guidelines:[^aaoifi-shariah] [^bb-islamic-guidelines]

| Principle | Meaning | Startup Application |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Prohibition of Riba (Interest)** | Money cannot generate money automatically, without commercial asset risk. | Capital providers must take real trade or equity risk; compounding interest is forbidden. |
| **2. Elimination of Gharar (Ambiguity)** | Contracts must be transparent, with no hidden clauses. | Profit-sharing ratios, asset specs, markups, and payment schedules are agreed upfront. |
| **3. Asset-Backed Real Value** | Financing must be tied to real goods, equipment, or equity. | Funds cannot be a bare cash advance with a markup; the financier buys tangible items. |
| **4. Ethical Business Scope** | Core operations must be permissible (Halal). | No conventional banking, gambling, tobacco, alcohol, or speculative derivatives. |

Rules 1 and 3 are the same rule seen from two sides, and together they explain every structure below: someone has to own something real, or share a real loss, before they are entitled to a return. Rule 2 is the one founders break by accident — an unwritten "we'll sort out the profit split later" is itself a defect, not a friendly gesture.

## The three models, and what each one costs you

| Financing Model | Primary Use Case | Financial Mechanism | Ownership Dilution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Musharaka (Equity Partnership)** | Venture-scale tech fundraising | Investors buy ordinary shares in your [private limited company](/registration/private-limited) or [Singapore holding company](/funding/fdi-rules). Profits and losses are shared proportionally.[^bsec-alternative-rules] | **10%–25%** per round |
| **2. Murabaha (Cost-Plus Asset Sale)** | Machinery, inventory, hardware | The financier buys the equipment directly from the supplier and resells it to you on deferred instalments with a fixed, disclosed markup. | **0%** (non-dilutive) |
| **3. Mudaraba (Revenue-Share Syndicate)** | Working capital for seasonal inventory | Silent investors (*Rab-ul-Mal*) provide capital; the startup (*Mudarib*) manages operations. Investors take a fixed percentage of monthly revenue until an agreed return cap is reached.[^biniyog-microsyndicate] | **0%** (non-dilutive) |

A **syndicate** is a group of small investors pooling money into one campaign, usually through an online platform.

Notice that two of the three cost no ownership at all. That is the real headline for a founder like Asif: the choice is not "interest or dilution". Only Musharaka touches the <Term name="cap-table">cap table</Term>, and it is the wrong tool for one season of mustard seeds anyway. The interesting decision is between the other two — and that decision is arithmetic, which is the next section.

## Instrument comparison

| Feature | Conventional Loan | Venture Capital Equity | Murabaha (Cost-Plus) | Mudaraba / Revenue-Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Underlying Mechanism** | Interest-bearing debt | Share ownership | Sale of tangible goods[^aaoifi-shariah] | Profit-sharing partnership[^aaoifi-shariah] |
| **Dilution of Ownership** | 0% | 10%–25% per round | 0% | 0% |
| **Collateral Required** | Land / fixed deposit | None | Charge over the purchased asset | Personal / corporate guarantee |
| **Return Structure** | Fixed compounding interest | Capital appreciation on exit | Fixed pre-agreed markup | Agreed % of revenue, up to a cap |
| **Default Consequence** | Legal recovery & compounding penalty | Investor writes off the loss | Asset repossession; late fees go to charity[^bb-islamic-guidelines] | Recovery of remaining principal; loss shared |
| **Best Used For** | Established cash-rich companies | High-burn tech with large scale potential | Physical inventory, machinery, equipment | Working capital, retail inventory cycles |

The row to read twice is **Default Consequence**. It is where the difference stops being theological and starts being personal. A bad season under a conventional loan means a compounding penalty and a recovery case. Under a Murabaha it means the financier takes back the expeller. Under a revenue share it means the payments simply get smaller, because they were always a percentage of sales that no longer exist.

## Asif's decision: the full arithmetic

Two offers land on Asif's desk for the same BDT 15 Lakh.

### Option A: Murabaha asset financing, 12-month term

- **Supplier invoices for seeds, bottles, and machinery:** BDT 15,00,000, paid by the financier directly to the supplier.[^aaoifi-shariah]
- **Disclosed profit markup, 12% flat:** BDT 1,80,000.
- **Total repayment obligation:** BDT 16,80,000.
- **Monthly instalment:** BDT 16,80,000 ÷ 12 = **BDT 1,40,000 a month**, the same every month.
- **Equity surrendered:** **0%**.

### Option B: Mudaraba revenue-share note, 1.15x cap

- **Capital injected by the syndicate:** BDT 15,00,000.[^biniyog-microsyndicate]
- **Repayment rule:** 6% of gross monthly sales until investors have received BDT 17,25,000 — that is 1.15 times their money.
- **If the season is strong (BDT 40 lakh sales a month):** the payout is BDT 2,40,000 a month, and the note finishes in about **7.2 months**.
- **If the season is slow (BDT 15 lakh sales a month):** the payout falls automatically to BDT 90,000 a month, and the note runs about **19.2 months**. Asif never faces an instalment he cannot pay.

### Now compare them properly

Most founders stop at "1.8 lakh versus 2.25 lakh" and pick the smaller number. That comparison is wrong, because the two offers hold your money for different lengths of time. A quoted cost only means something once you say *how long you had the cash*.

> **Rule:** A cost quoted on the full amount is roughly half the real cost, because you are handing the money back as you go. Over the life of a level repayment you have, on average, only about half of what you borrowed. So work out the cost per year on the full amount, then double it to compare like with like.

Run all three cases through that rule:

- **Murabaha:** BDT 1,80,000 on BDT 15,00,000 is 12%, over 12 months. That is 12% a year on the full amount. Double it: **about 23% a year.**
- **Revenue share, strong season:** BDT 2,25,000 on BDT 15,00,000 is 15%, but over only 7.2 months. Per year that is 15% × 12 ÷ 7.2 = 25%. Double it: **about 50% a year.**
- **Revenue share, slow season:** the same 15%, stretched over 19.2 months. Per year that is 15% × 12 ÷ 19.2 = 9.4%. Double it: **about 19% a year.**

| Offer | Total paid back | Months it takes | Roughly, cost per year |

| **Murabaha (12% flat)** | BDT 16,80,000 | 12 | ~23% |

The finding is the opposite of the intuition. **Revenue sharing is cheapest exactly when the business does badly, and most expensive exactly when it does well.** It is not a gentler loan. It is insurance — and like all insurance, you pay the premium in the good years.

For Asif that turns a vague preference into a real question: how confident is he about the winter harvest? If he expects a strong season, Murabaha is the cheaper contract. If he genuinely cannot predict it, the revenue share costs him BDT 45,000 more in the good case — and that BDT 45,000, paid over seven months instead of twelve, is what he is spending to make sure a bad case cannot break him.

> **Keep in mind:** The "double it" step is a rule of thumb for comparing offers, not a rate any financier will quote you. Its job is to stop a shorter, more expensive contract from looking cheaper than a longer one.

## Work out your own number

Fill this in for any two offers before you sign either. Copy it into a note on your phone and take it to the meeting.

```text
Murabaha quote
  Supplier invoice total (A):                    BDT ____________
  Disclosed markup (B):                          BDT ____________
  Total I repay (A + B):                         BDT ____________
  Number of monthly instalments (N):             ____________
  Monthly instalment ((A + B) / N):              BDT ____________
  Cost on the full amount (B / A):               ____________ %
  Roughly per year (that % x 12 / N, then x 2):  ____________ %

Revenue-share quote
  Capital in (A):                                BDT ____________
  Return cap (e.g. 1.15x):                       ____________ x
  Total I repay (A x cap):                       BDT ____________
  Share of monthly sales taken (S):              ____________ %
  My realistic monthly sales:                    BDT ____________
  Monthly payout (sales x S):                    BDT ____________
  Months to finish (total repaid / payout):      ____________
  Cost on the full amount (total / A - 1):       ____________ %
  Roughly per year (that % x 12 / months, x 2):  ____________ %

Two questions to ask out loud, and write the answers down
  Who pays my supplier — you, or me?             ____________
  If I pay 3 months late, what do I owe, and
  where does the late charge go?                 ____________
```

You know you are done when you can finish this sentence: *"Offer A costs me about ___% a year, offer B costs about ___% a year if sales go well, and I am choosing ___ because ___."*

## What it costs and how long it takes

Islamic finance removes interest. It does not make capital free, and the paperwork is heavier than a conventional loan, not lighter. Ranges below are marketplace and bank practice, so always get written quotes:[^biniyog-microsyndicate] [^bb-islamic-guidelines]

- **Murabaha via an Islamic bank:** the markup is priced case by case and typically lands near conventional loan rates once fees are included. Approval follows the bank's normal credit appraisal — weeks to months. Ask for the total repayment figure (purchase price + markup), never just a "rate", and put it through the worksheet above.
- **Platform syndicates (Biniyog.io and similar):** the total return is shown upfront as a cap (1.12x–1.25x), and campaigns fill in days to weeks. These figures are the platforms' own published ranges — company claims, not regulator-set rates.[^biniyog-microsyndicate]
- **Documentation:** a Murabaha needs itemised supplier proforma invoices, and a *Wakala* (purchasing agency) agreement if the financier appoints you to buy on its behalf. Budget for a lawyer on your first deal; you are reusing the same template for every season after that.

The timing difference matters more than the cost difference for a seasonal business. Asif cannot start a bank appraisal in October and expect seeds in November. A platform campaign that fills in two weeks may be worth its higher price simply because the harvest does not wait.

## Common pitfalls and Shariah traps

These are the four that survive into otherwise careful contracts, because each one looks like ordinary commercial boilerplate:

| Mistake | Why It Violates Shariah | Safe Correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Late fee penalties as lender revenue** | In conventional finance, lenders profit from penalty interest; in Islamic finance the financier cannot keep late fees as income.[^bb-islamic-guidelines] | The contract must state that late charges are donated entirely to a verified charitable account. |
| **Convertible notes with fixed accrued interest** | A convertible note that accrues 6%–8% a year before conversion is Riba.[^aaoifi-shariah] | Use pure post-money SAFEs — a SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is an investor's payment today for shares issued at a later priced round, with no interest accrual. See [SAFEs and Convertible Notes](/funding/safe-convertible-notes). |
| **Guaranteed returns on ordinary equity** | A promised fixed dividend on ordinary shares breaks the risk-sharing rule.[^aaoifi-shariah] | Returns must reflect actual operating profits or market exit valuations. |
| **Cash transfers for Murabaha** | Handing cash to the founder instead of buying the asset turns the transaction into a disguised loan.[^aaoifi-shariah] | The financier must pay the supplier directly, or issue an explicit purchasing agency agreement (*Wakala*). |

The last row is the one to guard hardest. It is also the easiest to wave through, because a founder short of cash in October is not inclined to argue when a bank offers to just credit the account.

## Checklist

- [ ] Verified the business, products, and supply chains operate within permissible (Halal) industries.[^bb-islamic-guidelines]
- [ ] For equipment or inventory, approached a Murabaha provider (Biniyog.io or an Islamic bank) with itemised supplier proforma invoices.[^biniyog-microsyndicate]
- [ ] Got the total repayment figure (price + markup) in writing before signing, and ran it through the worksheet above alongside a conventional quote.
- [ ] Asked, in writing, who pays the supplier and what is owed if payment runs three months late.
- [ ] For institutional equity rounds, confirmed the SAFE contains no interest-bearing or compounding terms.[^bsec-alternative-rules]
- [ ] Built a verified monthly revenue tracking system if pursuing a Mudaraba or revenue-sharing campaign.[^biniyog-microsyndicate]
- [ ] Confirmed late-payment fees go to charity, not lender profit.[^bb-islamic-guidelines]
