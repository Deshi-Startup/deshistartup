# Investor Due Diligence and Your Data Room

> **In short:** Due diligence is not one document request. It is four checks running at once: commercial, financial, technical and legal. Some of it is done by the fund's own team. The rest is done by an audit firm, a law firm and sometimes a tech firm the fund hires to check its own reading. Deals rarely die here because of bad numbers. They die because a small, cheap compliance gap got found by a stranger instead of being disclosed by the founder. Your next step is the three-hour audit at the end of this page. Open every folder below and mark each line **have it / can get it in a week / do not have it**.

Say Ridwan co-founds a logistics-tech company in Dhaka with eleven staff. Revenue last year was BDT 3.4 crore. He burns about BDT 22 lakh a month. A local fund has just given him a term sheet for BDT 6 crore, and told him diligence takes four weeks.

Three things in his company will turn those four weeks into ten. His monthly VAT returns stopped nine months ago, when his accountant left. The contractor who wrote the first version of his dispatch software in 2023 never signed anything. And two years ago he personally guaranteed an SME loan for his brother's trading business.

None of this is fraud. Each one is ordinary mess that a busy founder carries. All three will come out. And one of them costs him more than the other two put together, though not the one he expects.

---

## Who needs this, and when

- **You have a signed term sheet.** Read this today. Diligence starts within days.
- **You are three to six months from raising.** This is the best time to read it. Everything expensive here is cheap when you have months instead of days.
- **You are applying to a government or DFI-backed fund.** Their diligence is usually heavier on documents, not lighter. Start here, then see [the Startup Bangladesh checklist](/en/funding/startup-bangladesh-dd).
- **You are raising from foreign investors.** The checks are the same. The structure around them is not. See [international investors](/en/funding/international-investors) and [FDI rules](/en/funding/fdi-rules).
- **You are pre-revenue with no term sheet in sight.** You do not need a data room. You need customers. Come back when someone asks for one.
- **You just want the folder list.** Skip to [the six folders](#the-six-folders).

By the end you will have a marked-up list of what exists, what is missing, and which gaps you should disclose rather than try to fix.

## What due diligence actually is

Diligence splits into four checks that run at the same time and ask different questions.[^ab-dd-playbook]

| Check | The question it answers | What it reads |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | Is this a real business in a real market? | Product, market, unit economics (acquisition cost, MRR or ARR, gross profit), and how an exit could happen |
| **Financial** | Are the numbers true, and what debts are hiding? | Accounts, planning, cash, audit, tax |
| **Technical** | Does the thing work, and does the company own it? | Technical assets, scalability, security, code quality |
| **Legal** | Can the fund safely buy shares in this company? | Constitution, contracts, who owns the <Term name="ip">IP</Term>, disputes, compliance |

The order matters less than the overlap. One missing contractor agreement is a technical question about who owns the code, a legal question about title, and a financial question about whether the fund needs an indemnity. So one gap can stall three checks at once.

### The odds, stated plainly

In a 2024 Accelerating Bangladesh session on the diligence playbook, Md Tazul Islam, Investment Lead at BD Venture Ltd, put the funnel at roughly **10% of companies reaching the diligence stage** and **under 1% getting investment**. He described this as normal for high-growth technology companies, where competition for money is fierce.[^ab-dd-playbook]

He made a second point that founders take harder than they should. A fund can turn down a good business simply because it does not meet that fund's own return target. If the fund needs about ten times its money back over four to five years, a company on track to return five times gets a no. That is not a judgement on the business, and not a judgement on you. Funds invest other people's money against a mandate.[^ab-dd-playbook]

Treat that as planning information. A clean data room is not there to win an argument about how good you are. It is there so the only reason you get turned down is the mandate, not the mess.

## Who actually reads your folder

Founders picture one analyst. In practice a fund uses both its own people and outsiders.[^ab-dd-playbook]

- **Inside the fund:** the investment team doing the analysis, a research function, accounts professionals, and sometimes an in-house or advisory technology expert.
- **Outside the fund:** an **audit firm** on the financials, a **law firm** on the legal file, and for technology companies sometimes a specialist technical reviewer.

So two things are true. Your documents get read by people with no context and no relationship with you, and a scanned page that "everyone at the company knows means X" means nothing to them. And an existing audit report counts for a lot, because an independent firm already signed it. Funds still re-check, and the session was blunt about why: malpractice exists in the market.[^ab-dd-playbook]

> **Keep in mind:** Name every file so a stranger knows what it is without opening it. `2026-06-30 Audited Financial Statements signed.pdf` beats `final final audit.pdf` by more than it sounds. The reviewer is billing hours to your deal.

## The six folders

Your data room should contain six main folders. Here is what goes in them and why investors care.[^shoumik-data-room]

### 1. Corporate & Registry Documents
**Why it matters:** Investors need to check the company legally exists and who has the authority to make decisions.
- **Certificate of Incorporation:** Certified copy from the RJSC.
- **Constitutional Documents:** <Term name="moa-aoa">Memorandum of Association</Term> (MOA) and Articles of Association (AOA).
- **Form XII:** The official list of current company directors.
- **Minutes Books:** Board resolutions, AGM and EGM minutes.
- **BIDA Registration:** Needed if you are raising foreign investment.

**Checked against:** the RJSC record. Whatever you upload has to match what the registry says on the day they look. The AGM and annual filing duties behind Form XII and the minutes book are covered in [annual compliance](/en/tax/annual-compliance).

### 2. Financial Statements & Tax Compliance
**Why it matters:** Investors are looking for unpaid tax bills that could turn into big penalties later.
- **Audit Reports:** Financial statements audited by a CA firm for the past 1 to 3 years.
- **Tax & VAT Registrations:** Current <Term name="e-tin">e-TIN</Term> and Business Identification Number (<Term name="vat-bin">BIN</Term>/VAT) certificates from the NBR.
- **Filing Receipts:** Annual corporate income tax return receipts and monthly VAT returns (Mushak 9.1).
- **Banking Records:** Statements for every active account, and details of any loans.

**Checked against:** the returns themselves, and your bank. A company's income tax return is due on Tax Day, which is the 15th day of the seventh month after the income year ends, unless that falls before the following 15 September.[^ita-tax-day] The VAT return is due within 15 days of each month ending.[^vat-return-penalty] Both dates, and the rest of the yearly clock, are in [the tax calendar](/en/tax/tax-calendar).

### 3. Legal & Commercial Contracts
**Why it matters:** They want to see that your big customer contracts are real and enforceable.
- **Trade License:** Current <Term name="trade-license">trade license</Term> from the City Corporation.
- **Office Lease:** Registered lease for your office or warehouse.
- **Material Contracts:** Main agreements with suppliers, B2B customers or payment gateways.

**Checked against:** the other party, sometimes directly. Expect reference calls to your two or three biggest customers. Expect the reviewer to check that revenue in your accounts is backed by a contract that says so.

### 4. Technology & Intellectual Property (IP)
**Why it matters:** The company has to legally own the code and the brand it makes money from.
- **Trademarks:** Registration certificates or filing receipts for your brand name and logo.
- **Domain & Software Ownership:** Proof that the company, not a founder personally, owns the domain and the code repositories.

**Checked against:** registry records and signatures. This is where Ridwan's 2023 contractor becomes a problem. Without a written assignment, the company cannot show it owns the code it earns money from, and goodwill will not fix that quickly.

### 5. Human Resources & Employment
**Why it matters:** Informal employment terms let an unhappy ex-employee claim they own a piece of your IP.
- **Organogram:** Current structure and profiles of senior people.
- **Employment Contracts:** Standard offer letters and NDAs signed by core team members.
- **Contractor IP Assignment:** Written agreements moving IP rights from outside developers or agencies to the company.

**Checked against:** your own payroll. Names on the organogram who are not on payroll, and payroll entries with no contract, are both questions.

### 6. Cap Table & Prior Investment
**Why it matters:** Investors need to know exactly who owns what before they buy in.
- **Cap Table:** An up-to-date breakdown of shareholder equity, including [cap table](/en/funding/cap-table) calculations.
- **Prior Convertible Instruments:** Legal documents for any existing [SAFE notes or convertible debt](/en/funding/safe-convertible-notes).

**Checked against:** the share register and every instrument you have ever signed. A cap table is a claim. The signed documents are the evidence. If they disagree, the documents win, and the conversation moves to [valuation](/en/funding/valuation) and [term sheets](/en/funding/term-sheets) on worse terms.

## Your own credit history is in scope

This surprises people, so here it is plainly. Diligence looks at you, not only at the company.

**Startup Bangladesh's investment application** is the government-backed fund's own form. It asks founders to disclose their other business involvements, their loan history including bank name, amounts and whether anything is overdue, and whether they are a **guarantor** on someone else's loan.[^sbl-application]

Private funds ask the same question a different way. In the diligence playbook session, Tazul Islam described the financial check like this. Ask for bank statements and every financial document, audit them, then look at the **CIB report**, Bangladesh Bank's Credit Information Bureau record, for existing loans, default history, whether tax and VAT are being paid, and whether the unit economics hold up.[^ab-dd-playbook]

So Ridwan's guarantee on his brother's loan is not a private matter. A reviewer will find it. Found by them, it looks like he was hiding something. Disclosed by him in week one with a one-paragraph explanation, it is an ordinary risk item.

> **Rule:** If a third party can find it, disclose it first. Being found out is rarely fatal. Hiding it usually is.

## Why the cheap problem costs the most

Ridwan's three gaps are not equally expensive, and the ranking is not what you would guess.

**The fine is small.** Not filing a VAT return on time carries a penalty of BDT 10,000 under the VAT and Supplementary Duty Act 2012.[^vat-return-penalty] Nine missed months:

```
9 unfiled monthly VAT returns x BDT 10,000 = BDT 90,000
```

**The delay is not.** Diligence that should close in four weeks runs to ten while the returns are rebuilt and filed, the contractor is tracked down, and the guarantee is explained. Six extra weeks at his burn rate:

```
Monthly burn                           BDT 22,00,000
Six extra weeks = 1.5 months
Runway used up during the delay        BDT 33,00,000
```

```
Ratio: 33,00,000 / 90,000  =  about 37x
```

A BDT 90,000 problem costs him BDT 33 lakh of runway. That is before you count the risk that the fund's investment committee window closes, or that market conditions turn against him while he waits. This is the most useful number on the page, and it holds generally. In diligence, the cost of a compliance gap is almost never the fine. It is the calendar.

The same point has a longer tail. Tazul Islam described unpaid tax or VAT turning up years later as a demand for arrears plus penalty, and pointed out that such a demand reaches your investor too. What a founder filed away as a private shortcut becomes a reputational and legal problem for the people who backed them.[^ab-dd-playbook] Separately from the webinar, the legal exposure is real. Beyond late filing, leaving output tax out of a return carries a penalty of twice the amount left out, and claiming input tax credit you were not entitled to carries twice the amount wrongly taken.[^vat-return-penalty]

## Red flags, and what each one signals

| Red flag | What the reviewer concludes | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Missing monthly VAT returns** | Either the company cannot run a process, or it is hiding revenue. A clean annual audit does not cover for it | File the gap before diligence. If you cannot, disclose the gap and your plan to fix it, in writing |
| **Directors with no personal e-TIN, or NID spellings that do not match RJSC** | Sloppy records, so every other document now gets checked twice | Match name spellings across NID, RJSC and bank records before you upload anything |
| **No written IP assignment from early developers** | The company may not own its product | Get the assignment signed now, even years later. Disclose the period it does not cover |
| **A cap table that disagrees with the signed instruments** | The founders do not know who owns the company | Rebuild the cap table from the documents, not from memory |
| **Revenue in the accounts with no contract behind it** | Revenue quality is unproven, and your multiple drops | Attach the contract, purchase order or invoice trail to every big revenue line |
| **Undisclosed founder loans or guarantees** | A candour problem, plus a claim on the founder's attention and assets | Disclose in the first week, with amounts and status |
| **Documents that appear only after they are asked for** | There is more you have not shown | Publish the whole folder at the start, gaps included |

The right-hand column says one thing seven ways. **Disclose early, in writing, with a plan.** Every fix on this page is cheaper in week one than in week six.

## What to do in the next three hours

1. **Make the six folders** in Google Drive, Notion or Dropbox, named exactly as above.
2. **Go through every line** in them and mark it *have it*, *can get it in a week*, or *do not have it*.
3. **Move everything in the third group into a one-page disclosure memo.** What is missing, why, what you are doing about it, and by when.
4. **Fix the two cheapest gaps today.** Usually that means renewing an expired trade licence and sending the IP assignment to whoever never signed one.
5. **Pull your own records before they do.** Company bank statements for every active account, and an honest list of every loan and guarantee in your name.
6. **Name one owner.** One founder or finance lead answers every diligence request. Requests handled by whoever is free come back inconsistent, and inconsistency triggers a second look.

> **Warning:** Do not send an open folder link. This folder holds bank statements, salaries, customer contracts and your own loan disclosures. Share it per person, with view-only access and no downloads where your tool allows it, and only after the fund has signed a term sheet or an NDA. Turn off link sharing. Keep a log of who was given access and when, and remove access when the process ends or the deal dies. If a fund asks for the folder before any document is signed, send the pitch deck and offer a call instead.

**You are done when:** you can give a named reviewer access to the folder and a one-page disclosure memo, and everything they find over the next six weeks is already on that page.

## Checklist

- [ ] Six folders made and filled, with file names a stranger can read without opening them
- [ ] Certificate of incorporation, MoA/AoA and current Form XII match the RJSC record
- [ ] Board, AGM and EGM minutes present and signed
- [ ] Audited financial statements for every year the company has existed
- [ ] e-TIN and BIN certificates current, plus income tax return receipts for each year
- [ ] Every monthly VAT return filed, or the gap disclosed with a plan to fix it
- [ ] Bank statements for all active accounts, and every loan and facility listed
- [ ] Trade licence current, office lease registered
- [ ] Main customer, supplier and gateway contracts uploaded
- [ ] Trademark filings, domain and repositories in the company's name
- [ ] IP assignment signed by every founder, employee and contractor who touched the product
- [ ] Employment contracts and NDAs for the core team, and an organogram that matches payroll
- [ ] Cap table rebuilt from signed instruments, including SAFEs and convertible notes
- [ ] Founder loans, guarantees and other business involvements disclosed in writing
- [ ] One named person owns every diligence response
