# Bootstrapping in Bangladesh

> **In short:** Choose a small goal that you can pay for with business cash and customer payments. Set a limit on how much of your own money you will add. Plan for late payments and fewer sales. Decide when to pause before cash gets tight. Keep household emergency money and money you owe to others out of your spending pot.

## Decide whether this path fits the next stage

<Term name="bootstrapping">Bootstrapping</Term> means building with your own money and money the business earns. You do not rely on investors buying part of the company to fund this stage. You can choose to raise money later. This choice does not have to last forever.

Use this to decide whether savings and customer cash can pay for your next goal. It works best when you can sell a useful version without spending a large sum first.

Some ideas need a factory, long approval process or years of research before the first sale. A tiny test may not prove that such a business will work. Find out what a useful test would cost. Then consider other funding or a smaller first goal.

In a 2024 Future Startup interview, Homefectionery's Umayer Islam described using the firm's own funds for much of its growth. It also raised outside money at chosen points.[^fs-homefectionery-funding] This is one firm's story. The useful question for you is: **how will you pay for the next step?**

## Name a milestone that changes a decision

“Survive for six months” is a cash objective. “Get three customers to pay, use the service and renew without custom development” is evidence about the business.

A **milestone** is a result you want to reach by a set date. Give it a spending limit and a next decision. You might aim for repeat orders or a setup process you can use for every buyer. Or you might teach someone to handle support without daily help. Choose a goal that fits your business.

Write it now:

```text
By [date], we will establish [specific evidence].
We can spend up to BDT [amount] to learn this.
If it works, we will [next step].
If it does not, we will [change, pause or close the experiment].
```

## Put a boundary around founder money

Start with cash the business can use now. Keep household rent, basic living costs and emergency savings separate. Set a limit on what you will add. Agree it with anyone who shares the money or would bear the loss.

Record each payment a founder puts in. Is it a loan the firm must repay, or money for <Term name="equity">equity</Term>, meaning a share of ownership? Get help to put the right terms in writing. Money from family can also come with promises you must keep.

Customer advances also have a job: funding the delivery you promised, with provision for refunds where needed. A large balance after collecting advance orders can coexist with very little money available for other spending.

## Build cash scenarios before cutting costs

<Term name="burn-rate">Cash burn</Term> is the gap when the business spends more cash than it brings in. <Term name="runway">Runway</Term> estimates how long your cash can cover that gap. Use money received, not bills sent to buyers.

Shoumik Shahriar's runway article explains why you need to know what comes in and goes out. It also advises planning for weaker sales.[^shoumik-runway] Build a **downside case**: a plan for when things go worse than expected. A buyer may pay late or a supplier may need money early. Do not count a possible investment as cash you already have.

ExitStack's August 2026 review reports that a few deals made up much of the startup funding in the first half of the year.[^exitstack-funding] This does not tell you whether your startup will raise money. Base promises to staff and suppliers on funds you can use.

## Worked example: a small software product

Imagine a two-founder startup in Rajshahi selling scheduling software. All amounts below are fictional inputs, not salary, price or runway benchmarks.

The business has BDT 450,000 after setting aside money it already owes. The founders keep BDT 90,000 as a **buffer**, or cash reserve. They also plan a one-time setup purchase of BDT 60,000. This leaves **450,000 − 90,000 − 60,000 = BDT 300,000** to spend on the next stage.

The firm plans to spend BDT 90,000 a month. This includes founder pay and its regular bills. It expects to collect BDT 50,000 a month. The cash gap is **90,000 − 50,000 = BDT 40,000**. Divide the spending pot by that gap: 300,000 / 40,000 = **7.5 months before reaching the buffer**. This works only if costs and receipts stay the same.

Compare that with slower collections before committing to the purchase.

| Scenario | Collected per month (BDT) | Net burn per month (BDT) | Months above buffer |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Base assumption | 50,000 | 40,000 | 7.5 |
| Slower sales or collections | 25,000 | 65,000 | About 4.6 |
| No collections | 0 | 90,000 | About 3.3 |

The starting working budget is BDT 300,000 in every row and monthly outflow is BDT 90,000. These rough durations omit variation within the month. They are not exact dates when the bank account reaches zero.

The downside gives the founders less time to learn. They choose an eight-week paid-customer test and defer any second purchase until that test produces evidence. They also write the actual weekly payment dates: a yearly bill in week two can create trouble even when the monthly average looks safe.

Use this blank version:

```text
Cash already available to the business:
Less amounts needed for existing obligations:
Less chosen protected buffer:
Less one-time commitments for this stage:
= Working cash budget:

Monthly operating cash outflow:
Less monthly operating cash collected:
= Net cash burn:

Working cash budget / positive net burn = approximate months above buffer

Downside collections:
Downside costs and payment dates:
Next milestone, review date and spending ceiling:
```

Do not subtract the same obligation twice if it is already in your outflow forecast. If net burn is zero or negative, division does not give a useful runway estimate. Keep the weekly forecast: a profitable month can still contain a cash shortage.

## Make customer-funded growth workable

Sell something you can deliver more than once. You could charge for setup, one month of use or a small batch. The price must cover the work. You must also be able to deliver what you promise.

Set limits on side work. If advice or custom services fund your product, give each its own time and budget. Five custom clients may pay the bills but leave no time to build the product. Track those sales separately. They do not prove that people will buy the product itself.

For each sale, ask: when does money arrive, what must be spent before then, what could be refunded, and how much support follows? A long prepaid contract can improve today's bank balance while committing months of future work.

## Cut commitments before cutting the product's usefulness

Review unnecessary subscriptions, premature office costs, large stock purchases and custom work with no price. Compare recurring commitments with cheaper ways to test the same assumption. Shoumik also cautions that aggressive cuts to delivery costs can harm the customer experience.[^shoumik-runway]

Do not call unpaid founder labor “free.” Put the minimum cash each founder needs into the cash plan, and separately record the time invested. Borrowed devices and free tools can reduce an early bill, but they do not remove access, security or maintenance needs.

## Decide the stop rules while you can still act

Set a review date before cash reaches your buffer. Also review the plan if you miss the goal. Keep enough time and cash to change course and meet promises you have already made. The right reserve depends on your bills and risks.

At that review, choose among a smaller offer, higher price, faster collection, fewer new commitments, a different funding source or an orderly stop. Do not use new customer advances to conceal an inability to deliver earlier orders. Get professional help early if you cannot meet contractual, tax or employment obligations.

- [ ] The milestone proves something specific about the business.
- [ ] Founder contributions have a limit and a written record.
- [ ] Household money and committed business money are separated.
- [ ] The plan includes founder pay, delivery cost and collection delays.
- [ ] Base and downside cases use visible assumptions.
- [ ] Weekly dates reveal cash gaps hidden by monthly averages.
- [ ] A review trigger leads to a decision before the buffer is consumed.

Allow a focused half-day to assemble a first plan if the bank records and commitments are ready. Update it weekly.
