# Building a Pitch Deck That Survives the Second Reading

> **In short:** A deck's job is to earn the next meeting, and then to survive being read alone, without you in the room. Give every slide one job and one piece of evidence, and cut anything that is an opinion dressed up as a number. The fastest way to lose a serious investor in Bangladesh right now is a problem slide carrying statistics an AI tool wrote and nobody checked. Your next step is the eleven-slide template below, filled with numbers you can defend.

Say Ayesha sells refurbished laptops with a twelve-month warranty to university students. In nine months she has sold 240 machines at an average of BDT 24,000. That is BDT 57.6 lakh of revenue, or about 27 laptops a month.

Her <Term name="pitch-deck">deck</Term> has 22 slides. Slide 3 says the market is worth USD 2.1 billion. Slide 9 is a long product tour full of screenshots. The ask is on slide 21 and says "seeking investment to scale operations".

Every one of those is fixable, and together they are why an investor read to slide 4 and stopped.

---

## Who needs this, and when

- **You are raising in the next six months.** This page builds the deck.
- **You are pre-revenue, at idea stage.** You still need a deck, and it is a *different* deck. See [what changes with stage](#the-deck-changes-with-stage).
- **You have a deck and no meetings.** The problem is usually slides 2, 3 and 7, in that order.
- **Your meetings go well and then go silent.** The problem is usually evidence, not delivery. Skip to [the evidence column](#the-eleven-slide-template).
- **You are pitching a foreign fund.** The deck is the same. The room is not. Read [international investors](/en/funding/international-investors) for the call structure and the numbers you must know without checking.
- **You have not decided what you are asking for.** Do [valuation](/en/funding/valuation) and [financial projections](/en/metrics/financial-projections) first. The ask slide is a result, not a guess.

By the end you will have eleven slides, each with one job, one piece of evidence, and nothing else.

## What a deck is for

Three readings happen. Most decks are built for the wrong one.

1. **The skim.** Someone spends a minute or two deciding whether to open a calendar. They read the cover line, the problem, and the <Term name="traction">traction</Term> number. Nothing else.
2. **The meeting.** You talk over it. The slides back you up rather than replacing you.
3. **The second reading, alone.** Your deck gets forwarded to a partner who has never met you and will not ask you anything. This reading kills decks, and it is the one you cannot attend.

> **Rule:** Every slide has to make sense with the sound off. If a slide only works while you are talking, it is speaker notes, not a slide.

That one rule removes most of what goes wrong in most decks. The chart with no takeaway. The diagram nobody can follow. The six-word bullet that meant something in your head.

## The deck changes with stage

The same eleven slides carry different weight depending on what you can prove.

| Stage | What you have | Where the weight goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Idea** | Research, maybe a prototype, no revenue | Problem clarity, solution, market opportunity, and why *you* |
| **MVP with early users** | Product live, some usage, little or no revenue | Evidence of use, what you learned, and the path to money |
| **Traction** | Paying customers and a channel that repeats | Numbers, retention, unit economics, and the growth plan |

In a 2025 Accelerating Bangladesh workshop on building pitch decks with ChatGPT, the facilitator worked on a participant's idea-stage concept and had the tool focus on exactly that first row, problem clarity, solution and market opportunity, before anything else. Another participant with about a hundred users and a few institutional relationships pointed out that traction changes the deck from a normal one into something more advanced.[^ab-deck-workshop]

That is the right instinct. **Do not build a traction-stage deck when you are at idea stage.** A slide that says "Traction: coming soon" is worse than no slide. It tells the reader you know what is missing and hoped they would not notice.

## The eleven-slide template

Copy this. One job per slide, one piece of evidence per slide, nothing else on it.

| # | Slide | Its one job | Evidence it must carry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cover** | Say what the company does in one line a stranger can repeat correctly | Company name, one-line description, your name, date, contact |
| 2 | **Problem** | Make the reader feel one specific person's pain | Who has it, how often, what it costs them today, with a source |
| 3 | **Solution** | Show what you do about it, in one sentence and one image | The actual product, not a metaphor |
| 4 | **How it works** | Answer "but how?" in three steps | Three steps, with real screens or photos |
| 5 | **Market** | Show the opportunity is worth years of your life | A bottom-up number, with the working shown |
| 6 | **Business model** | Explain how money reaches you and what you keep | Price, contribution per unit, who pays |
| 7 | **Traction** | Prove somebody already chose you | The one chart that goes up, with real units and dates |
| 8 | **Go-to-market** | Show you know how the next customers arrive | The channel that works now, and its cost per customer |
| 9 | **Competition** | Show you know the landscape and where you win | Honest alternatives, including "does nothing" |
| 10 | **Team** | Explain why this team, this problem | What each founder has done that is relevant |
| 11 | **The ask** | State the amount, the <Term name="runway">runway</Term>, and what it buys | Amount, months of runway, use of funds, milestone |

Two columns matter more than the rest. The **job** column is a test. If you cannot say what a slide's one job is, delete the slide. The **evidence** column is the second reading. It is what the partner who never met you actually weighs.

Eleven is a working number, not a rule. Appendix slides are fine and often useful. What is not fine is a main deck of 22 slides where the ask is slide 21.

## Four rewrites

Most decks fail in the same four places. Here is each of Ayesha's slides, before and after. She is imaginary, but the weak versions are the ones founders really write.

**Slide 2, the problem.**

> Weak: *"Laptops are expensive in Bangladesh and students struggle to afford them."*
>
> Strong: *"A university student needs a working laptop by orientation week. A new entry-level machine costs BDT 55,000 to 70,000. The used market is cheaper but comes with no warranty, so a failure in month three means starting over. We interviewed 60 students. 41 had bought used, and 12 of those had a dead machine within a year."*

Anyone could write the weak version without leaving their house. The strong one names the person, the moment, the price, and evidence the founder gathered herself. See [customer interviews](/en/validation/customer-interviews) for how to collect it honestly.

**Slide 5, the market.**

> Weak: *"The refurbished electronics market is worth USD 2.1 billion."*
>
> Strong (fill in the brackets from your own sources): *"[N] students enrol each year across the universities we can reach in Dhaka and Chattogram [source, date]. If [12%] buy a laptop in their first year at an average BDT 24,000, that is BDT [N x 0.12 x 24,000] a year in our reachable market. Workings and sources on slide 14."*

The strong version is not better because the number is bigger. It is better because every input is visible and each one can be challenged on its own: the enrolment figure, the 12%, and the price. A single unsourced total is the fastest way to look unserious, because the reader cannot check it and assumes you did not either. Build the number from the bottom up: [market sizing](/en/ideas/market-sizing) and [TAM, SAM and SOM](/en/ideas/tam-sam-som).

**Slide 7, traction.**

> Weak: *"Strong growth, great customer feedback, 4.8 rating."*
>
> Strong: *"240 laptops sold in 9 months, averaging 27 a month, BDT 57.6 lakh of revenue so far. Contribution of BDT 4,000 per unit after refurbishment cost and warranty reserve. 18% of buyers came from a previous buyer's referral."*

Ratings are not traction. Units, months, taka and a referral rate are traction, because each one can be wrong.

**Slide 11, the ask.**

> Weak: *"Seeking investment to scale operations."*
>
> Strong: *"Raising BDT 1.5 crore for 18 months of runway. 60% inventory (BDT 90 lakh), 25% team (BDT 37.5 lakh), 15% operations and marketing (BDT 22.5 lakh). Target: 1,200 units a year and a second city by month 15."*

An ask with no amount reads as indecision. An ask with no milestone reads as a request for pocket money. The amount should come out of your [financial projections](/en/metrics/financial-projections), not out of the air. What you can reasonably ask for, and at what price, is [valuation](/en/funding/valuation).

## The statistics problem, and why it is worse now

AI tools write a very convincing problem slide. That is exactly the danger.

In the same 2025 workshop, the facilitator asked ChatGPT to generate a problem slide for a participant's idea. The tool produced the slide **along with statistics** about the problem. Nobody in the room had asked for them, and they arrived with no sources attached.[^ab-deck-workshop]

That is normal behaviour for these tools, and it is how founders get caught. An investor who works in your sector will recognise a figure that does not exist, or will ask where it came from. "ChatGPT said so" ends the meeting, and it ends it in a way that puts every other number in your deck in doubt.

The rule is the same one that applies to your model.

- **AI is good at structure.** Slide order, cutting a paragraph to a line, turning your notes into a clean sentence, drafting speaker notes, generating layout options.
- **AI is not a source.** Every statistic on your deck needs a citation you have opened yourself, and a date. If you cannot find the source, delete the claim. A problem slide built from your own 60 interviews is stronger than one built from a plausible-sounding global figure anyway.

> **Warning:** Put the source and the date under every external number, on the slide itself, in small text. It costs nothing, it survives the second reading, and it tells the reader your other numbers can be checked too.

## Questions to expect, and what they test

Have a one-sentence answer ready for each of these before your first meeting. The question is rarely about the answer. It is about whether you have thought about it.

| Question | What it is really testing |
|---|---|
| "What is your <Term name="gross-margin">gross margin</Term>?" | Whether you understand what you sell |
| "How do customers find you today?" | Whether growth repeats or was luck |
| "What if a competitor copies this next month?" | Whether you know what actually protects you |
| "Why is this the right team?" | Whether you can be honest about a gap |
| "What is your burn and runway?" | Whether you watch the bank account |
| "Which assumption are you least sure about?" | Whether you stay honest under pressure |
| "What would you do with half the money?" | Whether the ask is reasoned or a round number |
| "Who has left, and why?" | Whether you talk to unhappy customers |

The seven numbers you should know without opening a spreadsheet are in [international investors](/en/funding/international-investors). The last question on that list is the one founders answer worst, and it is the cheapest to prepare for.

**Answer the question asked, then offer the follow-up.** Getting defensive in the Q&A does more damage than a weak number. A weak number is a fact. Defensiveness is a preview of working with you.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Statistics with no source, especially AI-written ones | One challenged figure puts every other number in doubt | Source and date under every external number |
| A 22-slide main deck | The reader stops before your ask | Eleven slides, plus an appendix |
| A vague ask on the last slide | Reads as indecision | Amount, runway, use of funds, milestone |
| Slides that need you in the room | They fail the second reading, and you cannot attend it | Every slide makes sense with the sound off |
| A traction slide of ratings and testimonials | Reads as no traction | Units, months, taka, retention |
| Top-down market size with no working | You look like you did not check either | Build bottom-up, put the workings in an appendix |
| A competition slide where you win every row | Nobody believes it | Include "does nothing", and name a row where you lose |
| A traction deck at idea stage | "Coming soon" advertises the gap | Weight the deck to the stage you are actually at |
| A product tour instead of a product slide | Attention goes to screens instead of the problem | Three steps, one image each |

The first row is the newest and the most damaging, because it is now so easy to produce.

## Checklist

- [ ] Cover line is one sentence a stranger can repeat correctly
- [ ] Every slide has one stated job, and slides without one are deleted
- [ ] Every external number has a source and a date on the slide
- [ ] No statistic came from an AI tool without you checking it yourself
- [ ] Problem slide names a specific person, moment and cost
- [ ] Market size built bottom-up, with workings in an appendix
- [ ] Traction slide uses units, dates and taka, not ratings
- [ ] Business model states the price and the contribution per unit
- [ ] Go-to-market names the channel that works now and its cost per customer
- [ ] Competition includes "does nothing" and one row where you lose
- [ ] Team slide explains relevance, not job titles
- [ ] Ask states amount, months of runway, use of funds and the milestone it buys
- [ ] Main deck is about eleven slides, and everything else is appendix
- [ ] Deck read cold by someone who has never heard the pitch, with their questions written down

**You are done when:** someone who has never met you reads the deck alone and can say what you do, what you have proved, what you want and what it buys. And their questions are about the business, not about the slides.
