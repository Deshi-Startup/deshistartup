# Investor Board Report Template

*This guide is adapted from Shoumik Shahriar's ["The Board Deck Playbook"](https://shoumik.substack.com/p/the-board-deck-playbook) on SS Notes.*[^shoumik-board-report]

> **In short:** Once you have investors on the cap table, the board deck is how you manage that relationship between check-ins. Most Bangladeshi startups run this quarterly. The deck's job is not to impress — it's to give the board an honest, numbers-first read of the business, so the "asks" section actually gets acted on instead of triggering a round of surprised questions.

## Who's in the Room

A typical early-stage board meeting has the directors (which usually includes at least one investor nominee once you've raised beyond friends-and-family), any board observers your investors negotiated for in the [Term Sheet](/funding/term-sheets), and the founder-CEO. Bring your finance lead — whoever owns the [bookkeeping](/tax/bookkeeping-basics) and can answer a follow-up question on burn without checking a spreadsheet later. Who counts as a director and how meetings get called and minuted is a Companies Act matter, not a preference — see [Board meetings, AGMs, and the minute book](/registration/board-meetings-agm).

## The Shape of the Deck

Keep it to what fits in an hour of discussion, not a document that reads like an annual report. Ten sections cover most quarters:

1. **Cover.** Logo, company name, meeting date. Nothing else.
2. **Agenda.** Circulate this to board members *before* the meeting, not on the call — if someone wants an item added, you want to know that in advance.
3. **Key metrics recap.** One slide, the handful of numbers that define whether the quarter went well. Not a dashboard dump — pull the headline numbers from your [founder dashboard](/metrics/founder-dashboard) and stop there.
4. **Financial overview.** This is where you go deep: P&L summary, cash position, monthly burn rate, and months of runway remaining. If a raise is in motion, state where it stands and what's needed to close it.
5. **Highlights & lowlights.** The real wins and the real losses — not a padded list. If a lowlight is bad enough to matter, it belongs here, not buried in an appendix.
6. **Updates & projections.** What changed since last quarter, and a next-quarter view. Ask your board in advance what level of projection detail they actually want; some want unit-level forecasts, others want a one-line directional read.
7. **Product & tech.** Ship dates, major technical bets, anything that changes the roadmap. Weight this heavier if you're a tech-first business.
8. **Team.** Headcount, org shape, and hires in progress. Board members who've built companies before often have useful candidate referrals — but only if they know the role is open.
9. **Priorities & next steps.** The two or three things that matter most for next quarter, in both the core business and any growth bets.
10. **Asks.** Be specific. "Help us find a CFO" gets nothing done. "We need an introduction to a CFO candidate with e-commerce experience, ideally by next month" gets a name in your inbox. Try to walk out of the meeting with a named owner and a rough timeline against each ask.

Add an appendix only if there's supporting detail a board member might want to dig into after the call — don't use it to hide bad news you didn't want to put on a main slide.

## What Changes for a Bangladesh Board

1. **Minutes are a legal record, not a courtesy.** Whatever gets decided in the meeting — approving a budget, ratifying a hire, agreeing to the next fundraising step — needs to land in the minute book per [Board meetings, AGMs, and the minute book](/registration/board-meetings-agm). Don't treat the deck as the record; the deck is the discussion aid, the minutes are the record.
2. **Report in BDT, convert once.** If you have a foreign investor on the board, keep the primary numbers in BDT and add a single USD conversion line using a stated exchange rate — don't run two parallel currencies through the whole deck, it invites reconciliation questions that have nothing to do with the business.
3. **Time zones matter more than they should.** Diaspora and Singapore/UK-based investors are common on Bangladeshi cap tables. Confirm the meeting time works across time zones before you circulate the agenda, not after someone's already annoyed.

## Dos and Don'ts

**Do:**
- Let the numbers carry the argument. Investors trust trend lines over adjectives.
- Flag the concerning stuff in the pre-read, not live on the call — nobody makes their best decision when they're blindsided.
- Come out of "Asks" with a name and a date attached to each item.

**Don't:**
- Turn the deck into an operational deep-dive. The board doesn't need every campaign's CAC, just the number that tells them if marketing is working.
- Pad the deck with metrics that flatter you but don't inform a decision — that's a vanity slide, and experienced board members recognise one immediately.
- Tell a story that's rosier than reality. A board that's surprised by bad news it should have seen coming stops trusting the deck, and then starts asking for direct access to your systems instead.

## Action Plan

- [ ] Build a reusable deck template with the ten sections above; fill in the numbers each quarter instead of rebuilding the deck from scratch.
- [ ] Circulate the agenda and pre-read at least a few days before the meeting — never the morning of.
- [ ] After the meeting, write the asks and their owners into a tracker, and check it before the next board meeting rather than at the start of it.
