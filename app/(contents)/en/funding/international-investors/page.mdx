# International Investors: Pitching, Due Diligence, and Etiquette

> **In short:** Foreign investors invest in lines, not dots: they want 3–6 months of honest, metric-driven updates before the check. Know your seven numbers cold, spend the first 12 minutes of a 25-minute call on product and traction rather than country macros, and keep an audit-ready data room under a clean [foreign holding structure](/funding/fdi-rules). The two deal-killers are defensiveness in Q&A and a messy <Term name="cap-table">cap table</Term>.

Imagine Sami, founder of an enterprise healthtech startup in Dhaka. Through a mutual contact he secures a 25-minute video call with an investment principal at a Singapore venture fund.

Twelve minutes in, Sami is still on macro slides — Bangladesh's 170 million people, smartphone penetration, GDP growth. The investor nods politely and asks a single question: *"What is your monthly net burn and gross margin?"*

Sami hesitates. *"We are very frugal and spend carefully as needed."*

Forty minutes later a templated rejection lands: *"Thank you for sharing your journey. While the Bangladesh market is exciting, this is too early for our mandate."*

Sami reads that email as a verdict on Bangladesh. It is not. The investor passed because a founder who cannot state his own gross margin from memory has told them something about how the company is run — and no amount of market size fixes that. Everything below is about not being Sami on that call.

## Who needs this, and when

| Startup Profile | Relevance | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Venture-Scale Tech (SaaS, FinTech, B2B, Logistics)** | **High** | The domestic institutional venture pool is small — disclosed startup deals total roughly USD 20–50 million a year, and many go undisclosed.[^lightcastle-investors] Raising institutional Seed ($250k–$1M) or Series A ($2M+) almost always means foreign funds. |
| **Global Export Software & AI** | **High** | Foreign investors bring USD capital plus commercial distribution networks across Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America. |
| **Traditional SME / Lifestyle Business** | **Low** | International VCs need 10x–30x exit potential in USD terms to justify frontier currency and liquidity risk.[^fivehundred-frontier] Traditional businesses should look at [bank loans](/funding/bb-startup-fund) or [revenue-share financing](/funding/islamic-finance). |

The dividing line in that table is not company quality. It is whether a fund can plausibly return its whole fund from your one deal. A profitable BDT 4-crore-a-year business is a better company than most startups and still a bad fit for a venture fund — those two facts are not in conflict, and hearing "no" is not a judgement on the business.

You can skip this guide if your business does not need institutional venture equity, or you are bootstrapping on customer revenue.

## The minimum mental model: the three frontier filters

When a venture investor in Singapore, London, or Silicon Valley evaluates a Bangladeshi startup, they run it through three lenses:[^fivehundred-frontier] [^accelerating-asia-etiquette]

| Filter | What the Investor Worries About | How a Prepared Founder Answers |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The Currency & Exit Hurdle** | BDT depreciation against USD, and friction repatriating capital at exit.[^lightcastle-investors] | Shows high gross margins and keeps a clean [holding company in Singapore or Delaware](/funding/fdi-rules) so foreign exit liquidity exists. |
| **2. Capital Efficiency** | Will this startup burn $500k in 6 months without reaching product-market fit? | Shows that Dhaka's cost structure delivers 18–24 months of runway where a US competitor gets 6. |
| **3. Execution Velocity ("Lines, Not Dots")** | Can these founders hit quarterly targets and navigate hard local logistics?[^yc-pitch-library] | Proves execution by sending 3–6 months of transparent, metric-driven monthly updates *before* asking for a check. |

Notice what none of the three filters ask about: the size of the Bangladeshi market. That question was settled before the call was booked. Sami spent twelve minutes answering a question nobody had.

### 1. The currency and exit hurdle

The taka has historically depreciated against the US dollar. A foreign fund investing dollars therefore needs higher local-currency growth just to stand still in its own reporting — frontier funds typically target a 25%+ USD net return a year.[^lightcastle-investors] Investors also price in the friction of getting capital back out, which is why they want a clean [offshore holding company](/funding/fdi-rules) before they wire anything.

### 2. Capital efficiency as a moat

In Silicon Valley, a seed-stage SaaS company might burn $40,000–$80,000 a month on engineering. A Dhaka tech startup can often build comparable software on $5,000–$12,000 a month. Those are rough order-of-magnitude figures, not survey data — use them to frame the argument, not as a citation. The point stands regardless of the exact numbers: $100,000 buys 18–24 months of execution in Dhaka and about 6 months elsewhere. Said plainly on a call, that is an advantage, not an apology.

### 3. Lines, not dots

An investor meeting you once sees a single data point — a "dot". They cannot tell from it whether you are accelerating or flattening out. Send brief, honest monthly updates for 3–6 months before asking for money and those dots join into a visible trajectory — a "line".[^yc-pitch-library] The email structure is on the [Monthly Investor Update Template](/funding/investor-updates) page.

This is the cheapest advantage in this entire guide and the one founders skip most often, because it requires starting six months before you need the money.

## Early-stage equity dilution benchmarks

Founders in emerging markets tend to make one of two opposite mistakes: handing over 30%–40% for an early angel check, which leaves the **cap table** — the record of who owns what — broken for every future round, or demanding Silicon Valley revenue multiples in Dhaka.[^carta-benchmarks]

  unit="%"
  max={40}
  data={[
    { label: "Target Pre-Seed / Seed Dilution", value: 15 },
    { label: "Acceptable Upper Limit", value: 20 },
    { label: "Danger Zone (Series A Red Flag)", value: 35 }

/>

| Benchmark | Dilution | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Target pre-seed / seed dilution** | 15% | A healthy early round leaves room for later investors. |
| **Acceptable upper limit** | 20% | Above this, later funds start asking who owns the rest. |
| **Danger zone** | 35% | A typical Series A red flag — the cap table looks un-investable. |

The reason the danger zone is a hard stop is arithmetic, not taste. If you give away 35% at pre-seed, then 20% at seed and 20% at Series A, the founding team is under 42% before the company is five years old — and a fund pricing a Series B has to ask whether the founders are still motivated enough to run it for another decade. Protecting the first 15% protects every round after it.

These are Carta's published US seed-stage benchmarks — a starting point, not a law. Frontier rounds sometimes dilute more for the same check.[^carta-benchmarks]

## The seven numbers you must know cold

On an investor call you should never say *"let me check my model"* for any of these. Know all seven from memory:[^yc-pitch-library] [^accelerating-asia-etiquette]

| Metric | Plain Meaning | Target Frontier Benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| **Monthly gross revenue / GMV** | Total transaction value processed on your platform per month. | Steady 15%–25% month-over-month (MoM) growth. |
| **Monthly net revenue** | Gross revenue minus refunds, discounts, and platform or gateway fees. | Shows real monetisation, not just volume. |
| **Gross margin (%)** | (Net revenue − direct delivery and server costs) ÷ net revenue. | **SaaS:** 70%–85%; **logistics/marketplaces:** 15%–30%; **commerce:** 30%–50%. |
| **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** | Total sales and marketing spend divided by new paying customers won. | Count all ad spend and sales commissions. Details in [Unit Economics](/metrics/unit-economics). |
| **LTV to CAC ratio** | Lifetime gross profit per customer divided by CAC. | **3.0x or higher** — lifetime value at least triple the acquisition cost. |
| **Monthly net cash burn** | Total cash out minus total cash in, per month. | State a clear figure, e.g. "$4,500 a month". |
| **Cash runway** | Total bank balance divided by monthly net burn. | Minimum **6–12 months** left before you start a new round. |

Investors are not testing recall for its own sake. Each number is a proxy for a habit: knowing your burn means you watch the bank account, knowing your CAC means you measure marketing rather than trust it, and knowing your gross margin means you understand what you actually sell. That last one is where Sami came apart, so it is worth taking apart properly.

### Gross margin, the number Sami fumbled

**1. Name.** Gross margin.

**2. Plain meaning.** Out of every 100 taka a customer pays you, how many are left after the costs of serving that specific customer.

**3. Sami's own numbers.** His healthtech platform bills a clinic BDT 20,000 a month. Serving that clinic costs him BDT 3,000 in cloud hosting, BDT 1,500 in SMS and payment gateway fees, and BDT 2,500 for the part-time support agent who handles their tickets.

**4. The arithmetic.**

```
Direct cost to serve = 3,000 + 1,500 + 2,500 = BDT 7,000
Gross profit         = 20,000 - 7,000        = BDT 13,000
Gross margin         = 13,000 / 20,000       = 65%
```

Note what is *not* in that list: Sami's own salary, office rent, and the two engineers building next quarter's features. Those costs exist whether or not this clinic signs, so they sit below the gross margin line. Founders routinely include them, report a much worse margin than they have, and confuse everyone including themselves.

**5. What Sami says next time.** *"Gross margin is 65%, mostly cloud and gateway fees. Net burn is $4,200 a month against $9,000 in the bank, so about seven months of runway — which is why I'm raising now rather than in December."*

That is fourteen seconds. It answers the question asked, volunteers the follow-up question, and demonstrates the habit the investor was actually testing for.

## The 25-minute remote pitch

First calls with foreign venture analysts or partners run 25–30 minutes on Zoom or Google Meet. Spend the time deliberately:[^yc-pitch-library] [^accelerating-asia-etiquette]

| Time Window | Presentation Segment | Core Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| **00:00–02:00** | Professional check-in | One-sentence value proposition and meeting alignment. |
| **02:00–05:00** | Customer pain | The specific, painful problem in Bangladesh or the global market. |
| **05:00–12:00** | Product & traction | Live product demo, 6-month revenue growth, and cohort retention. |
| **12:00–17:00** | Market & moat | Target market size (TAM — the total annual spend if you won every possible customer), business model, and founder unfair advantage. |
| **17:00–25:00** | Q&A & next steps | Direct answers to investor risks, and agreeing data room access. |

**Cohort retention**, which appears in the 05:00–12:00 slot, means grouping customers by the month they joined and tracking how many of each group are still paying three, six, and twelve months later. It is the single most revealing chart in an early-stage pitch, because it separates a business that keeps customers from one that merely keeps buying new ones.

The structure puts product and traction before market size on purpose. That ordering is the exact inverse of what Sami did, and it is the most common fixable error in this guide.

### Critical remote pitching etiquette

- **Hardware and audio quality:** test your microphone, lighting, and connection before the call. Echoey audio or a dark camera feed reads as low professional polish, fairly or not.[^accelerating-asia-etiquette]
- **Skip the country overview:** foreign investors already know Bangladesh has 170 million people and high mobile penetration. Spend at most 60 seconds on macro context and the rest on your product, retention cohorts, and distribution economics.[^accelerating-asia-etiquette]
- **Address failures honestly:** when asked about problems, never claim everything is fine. Say something like *"our courier return rate spiked to 19% in Q2 because of regional floods, so we added mandatory phone verification and brought it back to 11%"*. That answer proves operational maturity in a way no growth chart can.[^accelerating-asia-etiquette]

The third point is worth more than it looks. Investors already assume something is broken — every startup has something broken. The information they are actually gathering is whether you know which thing, and what you did about it.

## The due diligence data room checklist

When an investor agrees to write you up in an investment memo, they will ask for access to a virtual data room — a secure Google Drive, Notion, or DocSend folder. Build these four folders before you are asked, because assembling them under a deadline is when cap table problems get discovered by the wrong person:[^fivehundred-frontier] [^carta-benchmarks]

### 1. Corporate & legal

- <Term name="incorp-certificate">Certificate of Incorporation</Term> ([Singapore Pte. Ltd. or Delaware C-Corp](/funding/fdi-rules)).
- Memorandum & <Term name="moa-aoa">Articles of Association</Term> (MoA / AoA).
- BIDA foreign investment registration and bank encashment certificates.
- Intellectual property assignment agreements signed by all founders, employees, and contractors.

### 2. Cap table & prior financings

- Fully diluted [cap table spreadsheet](/funding/cap-table) showing ordinary shares, preferred shares, and the **ESOP pool** — shares reserved for employee options.
- Signed [SAFEs, convertible notes, or previous term sheets](/funding/safe-convertible-notes).
- 10–12 slide pitch deck in PDF.

### 3. Financials & unit economics

- Historical monthly profit and loss statements for the last 12–24 months.
- Three-year bottom-up financial forecast modelling monthly cash flow and burn.
- Corporate bank account statements for the last 6 months.

### 4. Product & commercial validation

- Technical architecture overview and security compliance documentation.
- Key enterprise contracts, customer case studies, or letters of intent.
- Cohort retention and customer churn summaries.

The IP assignment line in folder 1 is the one that quietly kills deals. If the contractor who wrote your first version in 2023 never signed anything, your company does not clearly own its own product — and that is discovered in week two of diligence, not week one of the pitch.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Breaks the Deal | Safe Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Defensiveness during technical Q&A** | Investors probe weaknesses to see how founders handle pressure; defensiveness signals low coachability.[^accelerating-asia-etiquette] | Acknowledge the risk calmly: *"That's a real risk. Here's our current mitigation..."* |
| **Messy cap tables with dead equity** | Giving 30% to inactive advisors or non-working relatives — **dead equity**, shares held by people no longer contributing — without <Term name="vesting">vesting</Term> makes the startup un-investable.[^carta-benchmarks] | Put standard 4-year **vesting** (ownership earned over time) with a 1-year **cliff** (nothing vests before 12 months) on founder and advisor equity. See [Cap Table Basics](/funding/cap-table). |
| **Pitching US multiples to frontier investors** | Demanding a 25x revenue multiple in Dhaka because Silicon Valley SaaS trades there ignores currency and liquidity reality.[^lightcastle-investors] | Benchmark against actual South Asian seed rounds — typically $1.5M–$4.0M **post-money caps** (the company value implied after the round) for early revenue. |
| **Disappearing between rounds** | Reaching out only when cash runs out reads as desperation.[^yc-pitch-library] | Send monthly updates whether the numbers are up or down. See [Investor Updates](/funding/investor-updates). |

Rows one and four are the same mistake at different speeds: both are a founder managing the investor's impression instead of giving them information. The relationship that produces a check is built out of unflattering facts delivered on time.

## Your seven numbers, on one card

Fill this in and keep it open on a second screen during every investor call, until you no longer need it.

```text
  Month:  ____________

  1. Monthly gross revenue / GMV:        BDT/USD ____________
     MoM growth vs last month:           ____________ %
  2. Monthly net revenue:                BDT/USD ____________
  3. Gross margin:                       ____________ %
     (what the direct costs are:  ____________________________)
  4. CAC:                                BDT/USD ____________
  5. LTV : CAC ratio:                    ____________ : 1
  6. Monthly net burn:                   BDT/USD ____________
  7. Cash in bank:                       BDT/USD ____________
     Runway (bank / burn):               ____________ months

  The one thing that went badly this month, and what I did:
  ____________________________________________________________

  My raise: ____________ at a ____________ post-money cap,
  which gives me ____________ months of runway.
```

The last two lines are not decoration. A founder who cannot say what the money buys, in months, has not finished deciding how much to raise.
