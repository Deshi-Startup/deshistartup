# How to Find and Choose a Co-founder

> **In short:** Look for someone who can own work the business needs for years. Discuss goals, time and money early. Then do a small, real project together before making a long-term commitment. A good conversation shows interest. Working together shows how you plan, keep promises and deal with problems.

## First decide what kind of help you need

Use this if you are building a startup and considering a founding partner. A co-founder shares the work and major choices from an early stage. They may also own <Term name="equity">equity</Term>, a share of the business. This is a much bigger choice than finding someone for one task.

Start with the gap, not the title.

| What you need | An option to explore |
|---|---|
| One clear piece of work, such as a logo | A paid project with a specialist |
| A repeated task with clear direction | An employee or suitable outside service |
| Advice on a decision you will make | A mentor or adviser |
| Someone to own a core part of building the company | A possible co-founder |

You can build alone while you learn what is missing. Do not give someone a founding role just because you feel busy or do not have cash for a short job.

In a 2020 Future Startup interview, Accelerating Asia's Amra Naidoo stressed skills that cover different parts of the work. She also pointed to teams with a history of working together.[^aa-founders] Treat that as a useful test: what can the two of you do together that neither can cover alone?

## Write a search brief

Use this before asking people for introductions. Keep private customer records and secret business information out of a public post.

```text
We are exploring/building:
The customer and problem:
What we have learned or built so far:

I can own:
The other founder would need to own:
Work we would still need help with:

Location and ways we can work together:
Time I can commit now:
When I could work full-time, if relevant:
What is funded and what is not:

First step: a conversation, then a small agreed project
How to contact me:
```

“Looking for a technical co-founder” says little. “Looking for someone who can build and run the first version of a stock-ordering tool” gives the person a clearer job to consider.

Be open about the stage. Do not describe an idea as a funded company or unpaid work as a paid role. You do not need to promise a share split in a first post.

## Search where you can see how people work

Start with former teammates, classmates, people you have built projects with and trusted introductions. You can also meet people through founder events, university groups, sector meetups and co-founder platforms. Check that an event or service is active before paying or travelling.

Look beyond people who are like you. A good partner may bring a different skill or view. But the two of you still need to agree on the business you want to build and how to treat people.

Keep a short record of each conversation: what the person wants to do, what work they have done and the next step. Ask to see work they can share. A famous employer or a confident pitch does not tell you how they will work with you.

## Discuss the hard topics before the trial

Each person should write their own answers first. Then compare them. These are original prompts to adapt, not a test with right scores.

1. **The goal:** What kind of company do we want? What would count as success in three years?
2. **The work:** Which tasks will each of us own? What do we dislike but still need to do?
3. **Time:** How many hours can we really give? What would need to change before either person leaves a job?
4. **Money:** What can each person safely put in? What pay do we need, and when? What happens if funding takes longer?
5. **Choices:** How will we decide when we disagree? Which choices must both approve?
6. **Conduct:** What promises will we never make to win a sale? How will we protect customer and employer information?
7. **Change:** What happens if one of us needs to reduce hours, change roles or leave?

Share limits that affect the work without demanding private details about health or family life. “I cannot work evenings” may be enough to plan around.

YC's co-founder guidance pairs questions with a trial project and open reference checks.[^yc-cofounder] The questions help you find gaps. They do not replace time spent working together.

## Try one small project together

Choose work that resembles the real business. It should include a deadline, a customer question and a decision you may disagree about. Avoid a large unpaid build that one person can use while the other gets nothing.

Agree these points in writing before starting:

```text
Question or result for this trial:
Start date and end date:
Hours each person can give:
Tasks and one owner for each:
Cash budget and who pays:
How any paid work will be handled:

Existing work or ideas each person brings:
Who may use the work created during the trial:
Data and accounts we may access:
What happens to files and costs if we stop:

Check-in times:
Final review date:
```

Get suitable legal help for ownership, payment and confidentiality terms where needed. Calling something a trial does not settle who owns the work. Use made-up records or data you have permission to use. Keep test accounts separate.

## Worked example: a small stock-ordering tool

Imagine a founder in Kushtia who knows local distributors but cannot build software. Another person can build software and wants to learn sales. They are considering a service that helps shops send clearer stock orders. This example is made up.

They agree on a three-week trial. Each can give eight hours a week, so the plan has **3 × 8 × 2 = 48 person-hours**. They set a BDT 3,000 limit for travel and simple test materials. The budget is an example, not a local cost quote.

The first person owns six buyer conversations. The second owns a small demo using fake product records. They both attend two conversations and review the notes before choosing what to build.

In week two, buyers ask for a credit feature. One founder wants to build it at once. The other wants to test the ordering task first. They write down the extra work and agree to keep it outside the trial.

The demo works, but one person misses two agreed work sessions without notice. That matters. The final review covers both the product and how they worked. They may continue, run one more bounded test to resolve a specific concern, or stop. A working demo is not enough to prove a lasting partnership.

## Make the next decision clearly

Ask each other for references from people who have worked with you. Agree whom to contact and when. Ask about keeping promises, handling disagreements and what helps the person do good work. Do not contact a current employer without permission.

At the end, choose one of three paths:

- **Continue:** the work and goals fit; prepare formal terms with suitable advisers.
- **Test one open question:** name the question, new limit and end date.
- **Stop:** settle agreed costs, return access and handle the work as agreed.

If you continue, record roles, time, ownership, pay, decision rights and what happens if someone leaves. Do not treat a chat, this checklist or a suggested share split as a complete legal agreement.

## Before committing

- [ ] We know why this needs a co-founder rather than a short paid task.
- [ ] Our skills cover the main work, and gaps are visible.
- [ ] We discussed real time and money limits.
- [ ] We have done useful work together and reviewed how it went.
- [ ] References are checked openly, with permission.
- [ ] Work ownership and costs are clear, including if we stop.
- [ ] We have a plan to get formal terms checked before committing.

Allow a focused hour for the brief, then time for several conversations and an agreed trial. There is no reliable deadline for finding the right partner. Start by writing the work you cannot cover. If you decide to continue together, use the [founder role matrix](/en/cofounders/role-matrix) to turn the plan into daily ownership.
