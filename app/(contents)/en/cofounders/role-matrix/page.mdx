# Founder Role Matrix: Who Does What

> **In short:** Give each repeated piece of work one clear owner. Write what they may decide, when they must ask others and who covers them when they are away. Review the table as the business changes. A job title alone does not explain who will chase a payment or answer a late customer message.

## Copy the role table

Use this when two or more founders are building together and work keeps falling between them. A **role matrix** is just a table of who does what. It helps run the business; it does not grant legal power to sign contracts or move money.

```text
Work area or repeated task:
Result we expect, and by when:
Owner: one person who makes sure it gets done
People who help:
What the owner may decide alone:
When they must ask others:
Who must approve those choices:
Backup when the owner is away:
Where the records live:
When we will review the result:
```

You can put these fields in spreadsheet columns. Start with the few tasks that cause delays, not a list of every small action in the company.

## Separate doing the work from deciding

The **owner** makes sure a result happens. They may do the work or ask someone else to help. A **helper** supplies work or advice. The **approver** gives permission for a choice outside the owner's agreed limit.

For example, a founder may own customer support and arrange a replacement within an agreed policy. They may still need approval before promising a new feature or a large refund.

Give the owner enough time, access and help to do the job. Naming someone is not useful if every small step still waits for all the founders.

In his 2025 Future Startup interview, GoZayaan's Marc Thiry connects clear roles and goals with work that serves the customer.[^gozayaan-roles] Apply that idea to your own team: each row should name a result someone can check.

## Build the table from actual work

Spend a week noting repeated tasks and decisions. Look for work that was missed, done twice or delayed while everyone waited.

Group related tasks only when one owner can truly cover them. “Finance” may be too broad. “Update the cash forecast every Friday” and “send and follow up on invoices” are easier to own.

For each row, ask:

1. What would done look like?
2. Who has the skill and time to make that happen?
3. What can they decide without a meeting?
4. Which money, customer or legal risks need another approval?
5. Who takes over if they are away?

Also record the work no founder can cover. You may need training, outside help or a smaller plan. Do not quietly place all unclaimed work on the most reliable person.

## Worked example: two founders and a growing workload

Imagine a Dhaka startup selling reusable storage products. Rafi handles suppliers, and Mita handles sales. These people, tasks and limits are made up for the example.

Both reply to suppliers, but neither checks late customer cases. A refund is promised twice. They make this first table.

| Work and result | Owner | Can decide alone | Must ask first | Backup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Confirm stock and update the list each morning | Rafi | Correct a count after checking records | Replace a material or supplier | Mita |
| Reply to open customer cases by the promised update time | Mita | Give a checked status update | Offer a remedy outside the agreed policy | Rafi |
| Order routine packaging within the approved monthly budget | Rafi | Place an order up to BDT 5,000 | Spend above that limit or add a new payment term | Mita |
| Update cash and unpaid bills every Friday | Mita | Match receipts and flag gaps | Change a payment date promised to a supplier | Rafi |

The BDT 5,000 limit is their chosen example, not a safe limit for every company. An order below it must still fit the approved budget and the person's real authority. Several small orders cannot be used to avoid the approval limit.

Rafi receives a quote for BDT 6,500. It is above his limit, so he asks Mita before accepting. He shares the quote, why it is needed and the effect on cash. Mita has enough information to decide; she does not need to repeat all his research.

They also set support hours and a backup handover. The table has now fixed two real gaps: who owns the reply and who checks spending.

## Agree how to handle a dispute

Some choices are easy to reverse. Others create long duties or change the company's direction. Treat them differently.

For a small reversible choice within the agreed rules, let the named owner decide after hearing the relevant views. Record the choice and what result will trigger a review.

For a choice that needs shared approval, do not pretend silence means yes. If the founders disagree, write the options, costs, missing facts and date by which a decision is needed. Get the right adviser or agreed decision-maker involved.

```text
Decision needed:
Why it matters now:
Option A: cost, benefit and main risk
Option B: cost, benefit and main risk
Facts we still need:
Who has authority to approve:
Decision date:
What we do while waiting:
```

Do not use this table to override company documents, board rules, bank controls or a signed founder agreement. Serious deadlocks about ownership or control need proper legal help.

## Make handovers easy

For each important task, keep a short note showing how it is done and where the records are. Use shared work accounts with suitable access, not shared passwords posted in chat.

A useful handover says: what is open, what was promised, what happens next and where the supporting record is. It should let the backup do the work without guessing.

If the owner is away, say who is covering and when the owner returns. A backup does not gain new spending or signing rights just by covering a task.

## Review work and the working relationship

Use a short weekly review to check missed results, too much work on one person and decisions that keep getting stuck. Change the table when the work changes.

Also leave time to discuss how it feels to work together. YC recommends a regular founder check-in alongside a way to choose and track tasks.[^yc-founder-sync] A status meeting alone may not surface a growing disagreement.

Try these prompts:

- Which promise did we miss, and why?
- Where did we both work on the same thing?
- What did someone decide without enough information?
- Which row needs a new limit, helper or owner?
- Is there a concern we have avoided discussing?

The aim is to fix the working system, not to build a scorecard of who is the better founder.

## Your first version is ready when

- [ ] Each important task has one owner and a visible result.
- [ ] The owner has time, tools and enough freedom to do it.
- [ ] Shared-approval choices and spending limits are clear.
- [ ] The backup knows where the work records are.
- [ ] No row conflicts with actual legal or bank authority.
- [ ] A review date is booked.

Allow 45–60 minutes for a first table based on work you already know. Try it for two weeks, then fix the gaps. Start with the last task that both founders assumed the other would handle.
