# Founder Agreement Basics

> **Key Takeaway:** The MoA and AoA you file with RJSC when you register a company are the company's rules, not your own understanding with your co-founders. Rely on "we've talked about it" and the day someone feels they're pulling less weight, or wants to leave, you'll have your biggest fight at your worst possible moment, with nothing written down. A founder agreement is a separate, written contract between the founders themselves, laid out in advance: roles, ownership, what happens if someone leaves, and how disagreements get resolved. Do it before incorporating if you can — do it later, and power has usually already settled unevenly into one person's hands.

## Why This Is a Separate Document From the AoA

[The MoA and AoA](/en/registration/moa-aoa) are the company's constitutional documents, filed with RJSC, and they set the rules for the company as an entity. A founder agreement isn't that. It's a direct contract between the individuals involved, enforceable under ordinary contract law, never filed with RJSC, and one you can sign well before the company is even registered.

The two documents answer different questions. The AoA says how the board votes. A founder agreement says who's actually doing how much work, whose idea the business started from, and what happens to someone's shares if they vanish six months in. If the two documents give opposite answers to the same question, you'll end up arguing about which one governs right when a dispute is already underway — so lay them side by side while drafting and reconcile them.

## Seven Questions You Shouldn't Leave Blank

**1. What each person is actually bringing.** One writes code, one handles sales, one puts in the first capital. Name each contribution instead of settling for "they're an equal partner too." It's easier to write this down now than to reconstruct who brought what six months later.

**2. How much time each person commits.** Full-time versus evenings after a day job is a difference worth writing down on day one. If one founder is in full-time and another gives ten hours a week, and equity is split equally anyway, that reality needs to be on paper — leaving it unwritten turns into a grievance six months in.

**3. Who decides what.** Set the line for which everyday decisions — spending, hiring, pricing — one person can make alone, and which need everyone's sign-off. This matters especially in a 50:50 split; the deadlock scenario is covered in the [MoA and AoA](/en/registration/moa-aoa) guide.

**4. Who owns the work product.** Code, a brand name, a design, a customer list — if one founder built any of it alone before the company existed, it's still their personal property, not the company's. State explicitly in the founder agreement that this work is now being assigned to the company. Without that assignment, if the company folds or someone leaves, whoever built it remains its legal owner.

**5. What happens to shares if someone leaves.** Nobody should get their full share on day one — it's earned in stages, a structure called vesting. Why it matters and how to actually implement it in Bangladesh is covered in full on [Equity Splits and Vesting](/en/cofounders/equity-and-vesting). At minimum, the founder agreement should state what happens to someone's shares if they leave within the first year.

**6. What path to take when you disagree.** When two founders' votes deadlock, decide in advance what happens: talk it out first, then a neutral third party, then a buyout. Without defined steps, disagreements drag on for months because nobody wants to be the one who backs down.

**7. Outside work.** Write down whether anyone can take on part-time consulting or get involved in something in the same space. Skip this and one day you'll find a co-founder giving time to a similar venture, with no written basis to raise the issue.

## It Matters More With Family or Friends

Many teams in Bangladesh come together from university friends, cousins, or family. That same closeness makes putting things in writing feel like an insult — like you're saying "I don't trust you." In practice, it's the opposite. The closer the relationship, the more painful an unwritten falling-out becomes, because the business argument spills straight into family gatherings.

Don't think of writing it down as proof of distrust — think of it as insurance. You don't buy insurance because you expect an accident; you buy it so you can recover if one happens. A founder agreement works the same way — it isn't written in anticipation of the relationship breaking down, it's written so that if it does, the business and the relationship don't both get destroyed completely.

## When to Write It, How to Finalize It

Start the draft before registering the company, especially question 4 (who owns the work product). The less ambiguity you carry into incorporation about what was built beforehand, the better. You can draft it yourselves, but have a lawyer who understands company law review it before anyone signs. It costs a few thousand taka, cheap next to the problem it prevents.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Relying only on a verbal understanding | Each person's memory differs, and neither has proof |
| Treating the AoA as the founders' own understanding | The company's rules and the personal relationship get conflated |
| Not assigning the work product to the company | A departing founder remains the legal owner of the actual code or brand |
| Not writing leaving terms | Someone who leaves after two months keeps their full share |
| Only starting the draft in a moment of goodwill | Disagreements begin before the draft is even finished |
| Skipping the agreement because it's family or friends | Business disputes spread into family relationships too |

## Checklist

- [ ] Wrote down each person's role and time commitment
- [ ] Set the line between solo decisions and everyone's sign-off
- [ ] Assigned code, brand, and other work product to the company
- [ ] Wrote leaving terms and the first-year share rule
- [ ] Wrote the steps for resolving a disagreement
- [ ] Wrote the rule on outside work
- [ ] Reconciled this against the AoA and fixed any conflicts
- [ ] Had a lawyer review it before signing

Rule of thumb: **the closer the relationship, the more the written agreement matters. Write it to protect the relationship, not because you expect it to fail.**
