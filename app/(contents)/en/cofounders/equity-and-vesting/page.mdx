# Co-founder Equity Splits and Vesting in Bangladesh

> **Key Takeaway:** Most teams take the easy road the moment they get a co-founder and split equity 50:50, because the alternative conversation feels awkward. The problem shows up about six months in, when one person is working full-time and the other half-time, and the shares are still equal. Vesting reduces that risk — instead of handing over shares all at once, it earns them in stages over time. The catch: no company law anywhere calls this "vesting" by name. It's always built through a separate contract layered on top of the shares. This page answers two separate questions: how to decide the split, and how to actually build vesting protection in Bangladesh.

## Deciding the Split Isn't a Fairness Question

An equal split has one big advantage: the conversation stays short and comfortable. The problem sits in the same place — most co-founder teams don't actually contribute equally. One brought the idea, one put in the first capital, one works full-time while another gives evenings after a day job. Put these differences on the table before you decide the split, not after.

At minimum, discuss these questions separately when setting the split.

- Whose idea was it originally, and how much weight should that carry
- Who's full-time, who's part-time
- Who brought the first capital or assets
- Whose skills or network are hard to replace with someone else
- Who's taking on more risk — quitting a job versus staying part-time

No single answer decides the split by itself, but writing the questions down grounds the conversation in reasoning instead of feelings. What to avoid: defaulting to an equal split "just to avoid a fight." When contributions turn out unequal six months later, that decision becomes the real fight.

A co-founder who joins later gets their share through a new allotment, which dilutes everyone else's percentage. The dilution math is on [Share Capital, Directors, and Shareholders](/en/registration/shares-and-directors).

## What Vesting Actually Means

Vesting means shares are "earned" in stages over time instead of handed over all at once. Say Rafi and Sumaiya start a company together, 50% each. Three months in, Rafi gets a good job offer and leaves. Without vesting, Rafi keeps that 50% for life, despite never doing another day of work for the company. Sumaiya could spend years building the business alone, and Rafi's share never shrinks.

The most common structure in startups worldwide is four-year vesting with a one-year cliff. The cliff means nothing is earned at all until the first full year passes; the moment that year is up, 25% vests all at once, and the remaining 75% vests gradually, month by month, over the following three years. In Rafi's case, leaving at three months means he never cleared the cliff — with vesting in place, his earned share would have been zero.

These specific numbers (four years, a one-year cliff) aren't a legal requirement anywhere — they're a convention popularized by accelerators like Y Combinator. Your team can set two years or three instead if you want, but dropping the cliff entirely is risky: without one, someone who quits after a single month still walks away with some vested shares.

## No Company Law Calls It "Vesting"

This is where most of the confusion happens. Vesting isn't a special class of share, in any country's company law. Even in the US, a share legally becomes the full property of its owner the moment it's allotted. Vesting is built through a separate contract layered on top of that ownership — a "repurchase right" held by the company or the remaining founders, which says that if someone leaves before a set time, their unvested shares can be bought back cheaply.

The same logic applies in Bangladesh. Under the Companies Act, 1994, once a share is allotted it becomes the shareholder's own property, and a company generally cannot buy back its own shares except under specific legal conditions.[^companies-act-buyback] So vesting protection doesn't come from any RJSC form directly. It comes from a repurchase clause written into the founder agreement, and enforcing that clause still runs through the ordinary share transfer process — Form 117, stamp duty, and updating the member register — the full steps for which are on [Share Capital, Directors, and Shareholders](/en/registration/shares-and-directors).

## How to Actually Set This Up

Registering a private company with RJSC requires at least two subscribers to sign the MoA holding real shares, so co-founders usually receive actual shares on day one, not some pending "option." Vesting here isn't a mechanism for withholding shares — it's a conditional repurchase arrangement. Write at least these three things into the founder agreement.

1. **Vesting period and cliff.** How many years until the full share is earned, and how many months must pass before anything vests at all.
2. **Repurchase price.** Usually set at a nominal or par value for the unvested portion, not market value. Write this as an exact number — don't leave it as something vague like "fair price."
3. **How it gets executed.** Whether the company or the remaining founders do the repurchasing, and what paperwork — a transfer instrument, board approval — is prepared in advance to make the transfer actually happen.

The third point gets overlooked the most. Writing the clause into a contract doesn't make the transfer happen automatically — it still needs the departing founder's signature. To reduce that risk, many lawyers recommend preparing a pre-signed transfer instrument at the time shares are allotted and holding it with a neutral third party, such as a company secretary, so it can be executed directly once the conditions are triggered. Don't try to set this up yourself — have a lawyer who understands company law draft it.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Defaulting to an equal split just to avoid discomfort | Unequal contributions turn into a grievance later |
| Assuming vesting exists automatically under RJSC or company law | No legal basis for protection if someone leaves |
| Setting up vesting with no cliff | Someone who quits after one month still keeps some vested shares |
| Leaving the repurchase price vague | The price itself becomes a fresh fight during a dispute |
| Not arranging a pre-signed transfer in advance | You end up depending on a departing founder's cooperation |
| Not planning for a later founder's dilution in advance | The share math becomes a fresh headache every time |

## Checklist

- [ ] Discussed idea origin, time, capital, and risk separately before setting the split
- [ ] Didn't default to an equal split just to avoid discomfort
- [ ] Wrote the vesting period and cliff
- [ ] Wrote the repurchase price as an exact number
- [ ] Arranged the paperwork for executing a transfer in advance
- [ ] Had the whole arrangement drafted by a lawyer

Rule of thumb: **legally, a share becomes full ownership the moment it's allotted. Vesting is a conditional repurchase contract layered on top of that ownership — and you're the one who has to write it.**
