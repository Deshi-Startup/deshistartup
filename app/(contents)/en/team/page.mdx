# Team and Hiring: Decide What Help You Need

> **In short:** Hire when there is important work the current team cannot sustainably cover and a clear job for someone to take on. Define that work, check whether you can fund and manage the role, then assess candidates using tasks that reflect the job.

You are spending too much time answering routine questions, deliveries are falling behind or product work keeps waiting while you handle sales. Hiring may help, but “we need another person” does not yet explain what that person would do or what would improve after they arrived.

This section is for founders making those early hiring decisions. It connects the work you need done with choosing a candidate and preparing to employ or engage them. If you want someone to share ownership and broad responsibility for building the company, begin with [co-founders](/en/cofounders) instead.

## Find the recurring work behind the overload

Look back at a normal week. Which important tasks were delayed, repeated or done badly because nobody had enough time? Separate work that genuinely needs another person's attention from work that could be simplified, stopped or made clearer for customers.

Suppose a small shop-software team spends much of each day helping new customers set up their accounts. Before advertising for an “operations person,” examine the questions. If everyone gets stuck at the same confusing screen, improving that screen may remove much of the work. If customers still need individual help, there may be a continuing support role even after the product is improved.

The [first-hires guide](/en/team/first-hires) helps you compare which role would relieve the most consequential constraint. Include the cost of pay, equipment, training and supervision in that decision. A new person needs time to learn; hiring does not immediately return every hour you currently spend on the task.

## Describe the job so someone can understand it

For the support role, explain what customers need to complete and what the employee will be responsible for. That might include explaining setup steps, keeping accurate records and passing unresolved problems to the right colleague. “Good communication” is too broad on its own; answering a setup question accurately and clearly is something you can observe.

State who manages the person, the expected working hours, where they work and the pay range you can afford. Decide who will train them and which systems they will need. If no one can answer their questions or review their work, the role is not ready just because its budget has been approved.

A short written brief is enough to begin, provided it describes the actual work and its limits. Use the [hiring scorecard](/en/team/hiring-scorecard) to turn those responsibilities into consistent assessment criteria: the things you will look for when comparing candidates.

## Assess the work rather than confidence alone

For the software-support role, give candidates the same fictional customer question and a short set of setup instructions. Ask them to draft a reply and identify anything they cannot resolve. You can then compare accuracy, clarity and whether they know when to ask for help.

A fluent reply that invents an answer is less useful than one that explains the known steps and flags a real limitation. Give candidates enough information to attempt the task fairly. Explain how long it should take and how the sample will be used; do not disguise unpaid production work as a hiring exercise.

Use the same criteria when reviewing the responses and interviewing candidates. Record specific reasons for the decision, rather than relying only on who seemed most confident. Keep the information you collect relevant to hiring and limit access to the people who need it.

## Prepare to support the person you choose

Before making an offer, settle the actual working terms and check which employer obligations apply. A label such as intern or freelancer does not by itself determine the legal relationship. Pay, hours, supervision, applicable leave, confidentiality and the end of the arrangement need to match how the person will really work. Obtain appropriate advice where the classification or terms are uncertain.

For the support hire, prepare the setup instructions, show where to record an unresolved issue and name the colleague who will help. Give access only to the systems and information required for the role. Arrange an early review of actual work so misunderstandings can be corrected before they become habits.

The [first-employee journey](/en/journeys/hire-first-employee) gives the sequence from choosing the role to getting someone started. You can use the guides below as each decision arrives, rather than building a large hiring process before you know which job the business needs.
