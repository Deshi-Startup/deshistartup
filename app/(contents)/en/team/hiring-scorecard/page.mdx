# Hiring Scorecard: Compare Evidence, Not Interview Confidence

> **In short:** Decide what good work looks like before you interview anyone. Ask the same core questions, use a short job task, and check must-have skills before looking at total scores. Interview confidence and conversational polish easily mask execution gaps. Protect your team by scoring observable evidence from structured work samples, and run a paid 1–2 day trial project before extending an offer to finalists.

## Copy the scorecard

Use this after you define the job. A **scorecard** lists the skills you need, how you will check them and what you saw. It helps you make a fairer choice. A score cannot tell you for sure how someone will do the job.

```text
Role and main outcomes:
Candidate reference:
Person doing the check and date:
Same question/exercise version used:
Agreed tools, format and changes needed to take part:

Skill to check:
Why it matters for this role:
Needed from day one, or something we can teach:
Source: interview / sample task / reference checked with permission
What a satisfactory answer or performance looks like:
Observed behavior or work:
Rating: 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 / not assessed
Uncertainty or follow-up needed:

Decision:
Must-have skills met? What shows this:
Main strength:
Main concern and evidence:
Training needed and who can provide it:
Next step: progress / further evidence / decline
Decision owner and date:
```

Give each person a private reference number. Use it on score sheets instead of repeating personal details. Keep only what you need for hiring. Limit who can see it and decide when to delete it, based on your needs and duties.

## Choose abilities from the work

Start with the actual day-to-day work, not a list of generic personality traits. In early-stage hiring, founders frequently mistake interview confidence and conversational fluency for operational ability. A candidate who speaks polished English may charm an interview panel yet struggle when handed a messy Google Sheet or asked to reconcile fifty disputed cash-on-delivery parcels. Similarly, assuming that a reserved candidate lacks drive is equally misleading. Evaluating structured work samples tests actual execution, regardless of presentation style.

Define abilities through actions you can verify:
- If the role requires reconciling courier payouts, test whether they spot missing payments, verify bank transaction IDs, and flag discrepancies instead of silently overwriting formulas.
- If the role requires customer operations, test how they de-escalate an upset buyer whose delivery is three days late.
- “Feels like one of us” or “speaks smart English” is not a job qualification unless the specific role involves international client-facing communication.

The U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM) advises using the same core questions and structured rating standards for every candidate.[^opm-structured] It also recommends work-sample tests mirroring actual duties to evaluate skills required from day one.[^opm-work-samples] If you plan to teach your company's specific ERP or inventory software, do not test whether they already know your internal tools; test how quickly they comprehend an unfamiliar spreadsheet logic.

## Define the rating scale before the interviews

Write what each score means before the interviews. The scale below is a starting example. Adapt it to the job; it is not a proven test for every role.

| Rating | Observable meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Cannot complete the core task or misses a material issue after clarification |
| 2 | Completes part of the task but needs substantial guidance |
| 3 | Completes the expected task accurately and knows when to ask for help |
| 4 | Meets the standard and adds a useful check or improvement, explaining why |

“Not assessed” means you have not checked the skill. It is not zero. Decide whether you need another task or question. Do not give a four because you like the person, or a one because they pause before answering.

Describe good work for each skill. For accuracy, a three might mean spotting a payment listed twice and finding the right balance without help. For communication, it might mean explaining the problem in plain words while keeping private details out.

## Use a short, comparable interview

Ask everyone the same core questions and allow clarifying follow-ups. Examples for this operations role:

1. “Tell us about a time two records disagreed. How did you investigate, and what did you do when the evidence was incomplete?”
2. “Describe a task you learned recently. How did you check that you were doing it correctly?”
3. “A customer wants an answer before you can verify it. How would you respond, and what would you do next?”

Listen for what the person did, how they checked it and what happened. For a team example, ask which part they did. If the job does not need past paid work, allow useful examples from study or volunteer work.

## A complete work sample you can adapt

Imagine hiring for a commerce startup in Chattogram. The task below uses made-up records. Give everyone the same instructions and about 25 minutes to work. Then discuss the answers. Ask in advance if they need a change to the format or tools to take part. Explain whether they may use a calculator, spreadsheet or AI tool.

The task is to review three parcels and prepare a concise follow-up. The charge values are invented for assessment and are not a courier's rates.

| Parcel | Information in the packet | Payment evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A | Order BDT 1,500; advance received by shop BDT 300; courier collected BDT 1,200 | No courier settlement statement yet |
| B | Courier collected BDT 2,000; statement S1 deducts BDT 100 and shows payout BDT 1,900 | Export repeats transaction TX9, BDT 1,900, twice |
| C | Order BDT 1,000 returned; customer paid nothing; charge notice BDT 80, billed separately | No customer receipt; charge is not included in S1 |

The repeated TX9 entries refer to the same transaction, not two separate transfers. Include that note in the packet; this tests reconciliation, not a candidate's ability to guess missing facts.

Ask the candidate to:

```text
1. Explain whether parcel A's collection amount is correct.
2. State the cash receipt for settlement S1 and explain the duplicate.
3. Describe how parcel C affects collection and charges.
4. List what still needs evidence before the accounts can be closed.
5. Draft a short courier enquiry containing only the needed details.
```

A good answer explains each parcel:

- **A:** 300 + 1,200 = BDT 1,500, so the collection amount is right. But you still need to check whether the courier paid the shop.
- **B:** 2,000 − 100 = **BDT 1,900**. Count TX9 once, then check it against the real bank statement.
- **C:** The buyer paid nothing. The BDT 80 fee is a separate bill. Taking it off S1 would create a gap that should not exist.

The person should ask for A's payout record and B's bank record, and keep C's fee notice. They should not make up a number to balance the sheet. The courier message needs the order or payout number and a clear question. It does not need a buyer's ID card or the firm's full bank statement.

The task checks both maths and judgment. One maths slip does not mean someone is dishonest. Ask how they check their work. Then use the rules you wrote before the test.

## Check must-have skills before the total

Suppose accuracy counts for 40% of the total score, judgment for 25%, communication for 20% and learning for 15%. These are **weights**: they give some skills more say in the total. They add up to 100%. Before testing anyone, you also set a rule: accuracy and judgment each need a three. The person must use these skills without someone checking every step.

These are choices for this example. Use rules that fit your job and training plan. Do not change them later to help someone you prefer.

Compare two fictional assessments.

| Criterion | Weight | Candidate A | Candidate B |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Accuracy | 40% | 2 | 3 |
| Judgment | 25% | 4 | 3 |
| Communication | 20% | 4 | 3 |
| Learning | 15% | 4 | 3 |

Candidate A totals 2 × 0.40 + 4 × 0.25 + 4 × 0.20 + 4 × 0.15 = **3.2**. Candidate B totals **3.0**. A has the higher total, but scores only two for accuracy. Your rule requires at least a three. If you relied solely on gut feelings or unweighted averages, Candidate A—who spoke articulately and exuded confidence—would have gotten the job, exposing your business to chronic reconciliation leakages. Candidate B, while less flashy in conversation, met every must-have operational threshold and advances.

A high total score must never mask a deficiency in a non-negotiable core skill.

## The final verification gate: A paid 1–2 day trial project

A 45-minute interview and a 25-minute test evaluate how someone performs under observation. They do not reveal how someone actually works across an entire day: their proactive communication habits, how they respond to constructive criticism, or whether they can operate when instructions are not completely defined.

Before extending a formal offer to your top one or two finalists, invite them to complete a **paid 1–2 day trial project** (either on-site at your office or asynchronously remote).

### Four operating rules for trial projects

1. **Always pay a fair daily honorarium:** Pay a clean stipend (e.g., BDT 2,000 to BDT 4,000 per day, paid via bKash or bank transfer immediately upon completion), regardless of whether you extend an offer. Demanding unpaid multi-day "assignments" or free trial shifts alienates serious professionals and exploits jobseekers. A paid honorarium establishes mutual respect and signals that your company values professional labor.
2. **Never use live client or production work:** A hiring exercise is an assessment, not free operational labor. Never ask a candidate to fix live customer bugs, write production marketing copy, or deliver client work. Use historical data (such as anonymized courier settlements from six months ago or a simulated staging bug).
3. **Set clear daily milestones:** Provide a written one-page brief outlining the expected deliverable, tools permitted, and two scheduled check-ins (for example, a 10:00 AM kickoff and a 4:30 PM review).
4. **Score what cannot be faked in an interview:**
   - **Communication cadence:** Do they share concise updates at agreed check-ins, or do they disappear for seven hours and present an unexpected result?
   - **Question quality:** When they encounter an incomplete dataset, do they investigate first and ask specific, context-rich questions, or do they freeze and wait to be spoon-fed?
   - **Feedback receptivity:** When you point out a discrepancy or suggest a workflow adjustment on Day 1, does their Day 2 work incorporate the change, or do they become defensive?
   - **Documentation cleanliness:** Can another teammate understand their notes, formulas, or code without needing a live walkthrough?

## Write your scores before the team discussion

Ask each interviewer to write notes and scores before the group meets. Start with scores that differ. Did people see different things? Did they use the scoring rule differently? Did someone reward confidence without checking the skill?

Write what happened: “counted the same transfer twice until asked to check.” A label such as “bad with numbers” says too little. If you have not checked a key skill, use a short follow-up task. Ask the person's permission before checking references, and ask only about the job. Do not surprise them by calling their current boss.

## Before making the decision

- [ ] Each evaluated skill matters for the actual job.
- [ ] We know which skills we need now and which we will teach.
- [ ] Questions, tool rules and standards are identical and comparable across candidates.
- [ ] Each score has a note describing observable candidate actions.
- [ ] Skills we have not checked are marked as unknown rather than assumed.
- [ ] Must-have skills are checked before looking at weighted averages.
- [ ] Finalists completed a short, paid trial project on historical data.
- [ ] Trial honorarium was paid promptly regardless of hiring outcome.
- [ ] Training needs have a designated onboarding buddy or manager.
- [ ] The candidate receives a clear, timely next step or respectful decline.

Allow about an hour to draft the scorecard and try it with a teammate. Fix unclear instructions before using it with applicants. This is a suggested work plan, not proof that the test will predict good hires. After hiring, compare the person's early work with the test results. Use what you learn to improve the next test.
