# Selling to Businesses and Organisations

> **In short:** Before proposing more work, find out who needs your product, who can approve buying it and how the organisation pays. Use the next conversation to resolve the decision holding up the sale, then agree a specific next step with the person responsible.

A business likes your product. Someone has seen a demonstration, asked for a proposal or offered to introduce you to their manager. You now need to work out whether that interest can become a purchase, and what you must do to get there.

This is **business-to-business sales**, usually shortened to **B2B**: selling to another business or organisation. A small shop, a large company and an NGO may buy very differently. In a small business, the owner might use the product, approve the expense and pay you. In a larger organisation, those decisions may belong to several people. Understanding the difference helps you avoid spending weeks persuading someone who cannot approve the purchase.

## Find out how this customer buys

Start with the problem that brought your contact to you. What happens today, who does the work, and what goes wrong? A useful answer describes an actual task. “We need better software” tells you less than “the administrator copies each branch's timetable into a spreadsheet and calls people when two sessions clash.”

Suppose you sell scheduling software to a training business in Chattogram. The administrator wants an easier way to manage classes. A demonstration can show whether your product helps with that task, but it cannot tell you whether the branch manager has a budget or whether head office must approve a new supplier.

You can ask your contact: “If this works for your team, who else would need to see it before you could buy it?” Follow that with questions about budget, supplier requirements and the expected decision date. The aim is to understand the customer's process, not to bypass the person helping you.

Keep the roles separate in your notes. The **user** does the work with your product. The person approving the expense decides whether the organisation will buy. Finance may then need its own documents before paying. One person can fill all three roles, but you should establish that rather than assume it.

## Propose a trial that can lead to a decision

When a customer needs to see the product working before committing, a **pilot** is a limited trial: a defined piece of work, for a defined period, on agreed terms. Its purpose is to answer a buying question. A pilot with no end or decision owner can turn into ongoing support without a purchase.

For the training business, you might first demonstrate a sample timetable with dummy data. If the administrator finds it useful, the next discussion with the manager could be about trying it at one branch. Before beginning, agree which scheduling tasks are included, who will use it, what support you will provide and when both sides will review the result. Settle whether the pilot is paid and what happens to any work or data when it ends.

The review needs a question the customer can answer: can the branch prepare and update its timetable using the product, with the level of support you can afford to provide? If the answer is yes, who decides on continued use, at what price? The [pilot-to-contract guide](/en/b2b/pilot-to-contract) explains how to put the agreement and review together.

## Settle the route from approval to payment

A purchase decision still leaves practical details. Ask who signs, whether a **purchase order** is required, and what the invoice must contain. A purchase order records the customer's order; an **invoice** requests payment. Neither means the money has reached your account.

Agree the price, work included, delivery or acceptance conditions and payment date before starting. If payment follows acceptance, define how the customer will confirm acceptance and who is responsible for it. You need to know how long you may be funding the work yourself, especially when wages or supplier bills fall due before the customer pays. For work already delivered, the [invoice-collection guide](/en/b2b/invoice-collection) helps you follow up from the agreement and delivery record.

For the scheduling sale, your next move now depends on what you have learned. If only the administrator has seen the product, arrange the manager discussion. If the manager is ready to buy, resolve the scope and purchasing requirements. If the invoice is overdue, another demonstration is unlikely to be the useful next step.
