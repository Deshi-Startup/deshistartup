# From Corporate Pilot to Paid Contract

> **In short:** Run a pilot when a buyer needs to test something before buying. Agree what to test, who will decide, what it costs and when it ends. The product may work well and still win no contract. Someone must have the money and power to buy it. Fill in the brief below with the buyer before you start.

## Copy the pilot brief

Use this when a firm wants to try your product before a larger purchase. This page helps you plan the work. Have a qualified adviser check the actual contract, including data use and who bears each risk.

```text
Customer and business problem:
Question this pilot must answer:
Why a demo or small standard purchase is not enough:

Person supporting the test inside the buyer's firm, and daily user:
Budget owner and purchase approver:
Contact in the buying or accounts team:

Included site, people, workflow and data:
Excluded features and work:
Customer inputs and dates:
Our work, owner and effort limit:

Starting results, how we will check them and over what period:
Results the pilot must reach:
What would make the old and new results unfair to compare:

Pilot fee, tax treatment and payment schedule:
Estimated delivery cost and learning budget:
Plan for wider use: included work, price and steps to approve it

Start date, midpoint review and final decision meeting:
Decision: buy / stop / one defined additional test
Data return/deletion, access removal and support after the pilot:
Written approval/reference to the agreement:
```

Ask what the buyer needs to learn and who can approve the purchase. If neither answer is clear, pause. Adding more features will not give your contact the power to buy.

## Decide whether a pilot is needed

A **pilot** is a small test with real users. It checks whether your product does an agreed job for the buyer. A demo shows how the product works. A proof of concept checks whether a technical idea can work at all. Choose the test that answers the buyer's question.

Choose the smallest step that resolves the uncertainty.

| Customer's uncertainty | Useful next step |
|---|---|
| “I do not understand what it does.” | A task-focused demonstration |
| “Will it connect to our system?” | A bounded technical test |
| “Will our team use it in daily work?” | A pilot with real users and a measurement plan |
| “We are ready, but accounts needs documents.” | Complete purchasing requirements |

The last row is not a product problem. A free trial can delay a purchase that should already be moving through approval.

In a First Round interview, Emery Rosansky explains that a pilot should address the buyer's concern. It also needs someone inside the buyer's firm to support it and check progress.[^first-round-pilots] The brief above helps you agree those things.

## Agree what success means before starting

Choose one main result. Then name what must not get worse. For example, the tool should help staff match payments faster without losing any records.

Record the **baseline**: how the task works before the test. Use the same checks during the pilot. Compare similar work. A day with 20 orders is not the same as a day with 200. You may need to check time per order, missing records and mistakes, rather than total time alone.

“The client is satisfied” is too vague. Write what the buyer will check, who will check it and how to handle missing data. Set a decision date. A small test cannot prove that your product caused every gain. An easier week or extra help for users may also improve the result.

## Price the learning you are choosing to fund

A paid pilot can still lose money. Work out your delivery cost before naming a fee: staff time, setup, travel, hosting or equipment, support and exit work. State what taxes or third-party expenses are additional after checking their treatment.

If you charge less than the work costs, write down the gap. You are choosing to pay that amount to learn. The pilot fee is not all profit.

Field Buzz's co-founder said its first client did not pay at first but helped the team learn. It took a year to get the second client.[^field-buzz-pilot] This older account shows both the learning and the long wait. It does not mean your startup should offer free pilots.

## Worked example: a distributor's reconciliation pilot

Imagine a Dhaka software startup testing a daily reconciliation tool at one distributor. All amounts, timings and results are illustrative.

The buyer agrees to share daily records with private details removed. One user will try the tool. An accounts manager can approve the next purchase. The pilot lasts four working weeks. It tests whether staff can finish faster without missing records. Work on stock tracking or a second branch is outside this test.

Before starting, both sides choose these measures.

| Measure | Baseline | Agreed pilot condition |
|---|---|---|
| Daily matching time at comparable volume | 90 minutes | 60 minutes or less |
| Transactions accounted for | All in the daily export | Every input has a matched or explained status |
| Errors in a weekly check of 100 records | 8 | 3 or fewer, with the same sampling and check method |

The buyer chose this time target for this test. It is not a standard for the industry. Every record must have a status. Some may still need checking. Do not force a match to make the results look good.

Say the pilot fee is BDT 25,000 before any applicable tax. Delivery uses 40 team hours at an internal cost of BDT 700 per hour, plus BDT 4,000 in travel and hosting. Cost is 28,000 + 4,000 = **BDT 32,000**. The startup has chosen to spend **BDT 7,000 on learning**. It caps extra work at that agreed scope.

At the review, the observed matching time is 50 minutes and the same 100-record check finds three errors. The team also verifies that every input transaction has a status. The pilot meets the agreed measures, but the rollout decision still needs economics and approval.

The time saved is 90 − 50 = 40 minutes a day. Across 22 working days, that is 880 minutes, or about 14.7 hours. At a chosen BDT 300 per hour, the time value is about **BDT 4,400 a month**. It is not automatically a cash saving: the employee may remain on the same salary. A proposed BDT 6,000 monthly subscription cannot be justified by that time calculation alone. The buyer must judge other evidenced benefits, a different scope or a different price.

That is a useful result. The pilot has produced a purchase decision grounded in evidence rather than a celebratory slide.

## Run the midpoint and final meetings

Halfway through, check whether the buyer sent the promised records and the users tried the tool. If access is missing, you do not yet know whether the tool works. Agree how to fix the gap and how much extra work is allowed. Then set a fair period to test again, or stop.

At the final meeting, show the starting results, test results and what you still do not know. List any open problems. Bring the price and steps for a wider purchase. Ask the person who can approve it for a decision.

```text
We agreed to test [question] by [date].
The result was [measured result], with [limitation].
The remaining issue is [issue or none].
The proposed rollout is [scope] for [price and billing period].
Can you confirm the approver, documents required and decision date?
If you will not proceed, we will close access and handle data as agreed.
```

If an extension is necessary, write the new question, extra cost, evidence needed and final date. An extension with no new question is usually more unpaid service.

## Close the loop

- [ ] A named approver understands the proposed purchase.
- [ ] The pilot resolves an actual uncertainty.
- [ ] Scope, customer inputs and effort limits are agreed.
- [ ] Measures have a baseline and consistent definitions.
- [ ] The fee and any learning subsidy are visible.
- [ ] A final decision meeting is booked.
- [ ] A rollout, stop or bounded extension is recorded.
- [ ] Data, access and support are closed or transferred as agreed.

Plan a few hours to prepare the brief and budget; the actual pilot length depends on the customer's task cycle and setup. Your next action is to agree the brief before work starts. For a rollout, confirm the [invoice and collection process](/en/b2b/invoice-collection) before treating the contract as near-term cash.
