# Selling to Small Businesses: From First Visit to Paid Use

> **In short:** Choose businesses with one costly, repeated problem. Find the person who uses the product, the person who approves it and the person who pays. Demonstrate the job they need done, agree a small next commitment, and track actual payment and use. More demos will not fix an offer that nobody has a reason or authority to buy.

## Who this is for

Use this if your startup sells software, tools or a service to other firms. Buyers might be shops, clinics or firms that supply stores. <Term name="b2b-b2c">B2B</Term> means business-to-business: one business sells to another. SME means a small or medium-sized business. These buyers do not all have the same needs.

You will leave with a prospect list, a conversation structure and a short sales plan. If you have only an idea, first investigate the problem without pitching. If a corporate customer already wants a trial, the [pilot-to-contract playbook](/en/b2b/pilot-to-contract) covers that decision in more detail.

## Pick a workflow and a reachable group

Choose one group and one repeated task. For example, find firms in one area that check the money their sales staff collect each day. This is much clearer than “all SMEs.” A workflow is a set of steps people follow to get work done. Pick one that you can watch, show and price.

Accelerating Bangladesh's GTM course links the buyer, the offer, sales channels and day-to-day work.[^ab-gtm] GTM means go-to-market: how you will reach buyers and win sales. Plan these choices together. If you promise to set up the tool in person, include the visit in your costs.

Start with 15–20 firms you can reach. This is a small work plan, not a sample of the whole market. Use public listings, local visits and people who agree to introduce you. Write down why each firm might need your offer. A bought list tells you little if you do not know who is on it.

PriyoShop's Dipty Mandal described showing shopkeepers how the service works and staying in touch.[^fs-priyoshop-sales] Try the idea with your buyers: does seeing the task done help them trust the offer? This does not prove that shop visits always work better than online sales.

## Find the three people, even if they are one person

A cashier may love your product while the owner controls the budget. A manager may approve the idea while an accounts officer needs documents before payment.

Map these roles before preparing a proposal.

| Role | What you need to learn | Useful question |
|---|---|---|
| User | Task, frequency and difficulty | “Show me the last time you did this.” |
| Approver | Priority, budget and decision process | “Who needs to agree before you start?” |
| Payer | Billing details and payment timing | “What must reach accounts before they can pay?” |

In a small shop, one owner may do all three. In a larger business, ask to include the relevant people rather than relying on one contact to explain your offer for you.

## Run a useful first conversation

Ask for a short, agreed time outside the customer's busiest period. Use their preferred language and terms. The following is a structure to adapt, not a speech to read at someone.

```text
Opening:
We help [specific businesses] with [task]. Is now a suitable time
for a short conversation about how you handle it?

Discovery:
When did this last happen?
Can you walk me through what you did?
Where did it take extra time or require a correction?
What did that delay or error affect?
What have you already tried?

Buying process:
Is changing this a priority now? Why now?
Who else would need to use or approve a change?
How would a small purchase like this be approved and paid?

Close:
It sounds as though [specific problem] matters most.
Would [specific next action] help you judge whether we can solve it?
Who should join, and what date works?
```

Do not interrogate the buyer for a budget before understanding the problem. Do not request private customer records to make a demo convincing. Redacted examples or fictional data are usually enough at this stage.

If the buyer says there is no priority, ask what would make it one, record the answer and leave respectfully. A clear “not now” saves more time than repeated vague follow-ups.

## Demonstrate one complete task

Show the starting information, the action and the finished result. For a collection tool: a list of expected payments goes in, the missing payments come out, and the user sees who needs follow-up. Skip a tour of every feature.

Then let the intended user try it. Watch where they need your help. Setup that depends on the founder sitting beside every user may be acceptable for learning, but include that effort in the economics before scaling.

Field Buzz's co-founder described a difficult early B2B sales process and a year between its first and second clients.[^fs-field-buzz-sales] That is one company's experience from 2018, not a normal sales-cycle benchmark. It illustrates why you should track buyer actions and cash needs while learning to sell.

## Make a small, priced offer

Write a short offer. State what the buyer gets, who can use it and what setup includes. Explain what the buyer must do and when help is available. Add the price, billing period, due dates and how to stop. Check the tax treatment, then state whether the price includes tax.

An offer can be a paid month, a limited implementation or a small order. Do not add a pilot automatically if the customer is ready to buy. If they need proof first, identify exactly what they are uncertain about and agree how the trial will answer it.

## Worked example: does this sale pay for the effort?

Imagine a startup in Bogura selling stock-ordering software to small distributors. These figures are invented planning inputs, not prices or benchmarks.

In a two-week learning round, the founder contacts 20 suitable businesses. Twelve have a conversation, six try a demo, three accept the offer and two pay and start using it. Keep the unpaid acceptance separate.

Here is the <Term name="funnel">funnel</Term>: the number of buyers who reach each step in that same batch.

| Step | Businesses | What proves it |
|---|---:|---|
| Contacted | 20 | Dated contact record |
| Conversation completed | 12 | Notes about the actual task |
| Demo completed | 6 | Intended user tried it |
| Offer accepted | 3 | Written agreement to the offer |
| Paid and started | 2 | Matched payment and first completed task |

Two out of twenty is a 10% contact-to-paid-start rate for this batch. It is too little evidence to promise that the next 200 contacts will produce 20 customers.

Suppose travel and materials cost BDT 4,000. Sales and first setup also take 12 founder hours. Value that time at BDT 500 an hour for this example: 12 × 500 = BDT 6,000. The total effort costs 4,000 + 6,000 = BDT 10,000. Divide by two new paying buyers: **BDT 5,000 per buyer**. The time has a cost even if the founder takes no extra pay.

Each buyer pays BDT 3,000 a month. It costs BDT 1,000 a month to serve them. That leaves 3,000 − 1,000 = **BDT 2,000** to help cover sales costs and fixed bills, such as office rent. This amount is called contribution. It is not net profit.

At that rate, earning back BDT 5,000 takes 5,000 / 2,000 = **2.5 paid months**. Costs and payments must stay the same for that estimate to hold. <Term name="retention">Retention</Term> means keeping customers over time. One paid month does not show that they will stay. Extra setup work can also push back the payback date.

The next move is to learn why one accepted offer remains unpaid and whether the two customers continue using the product. Buying more leads before understanding those two questions would hide the bottleneck.

## Keep a small weekly sales sheet

Copy one row per business. Use stages only when the evidence has happened.

```text
Business | Fit reason | User | Approver | Payer
Recent problem | Current alternative | Offer and price
Stage | Evidence/date | Main objection
Next action | Owner | Agreed date | Cash expected date
First payment matched | First useful task completed | Follow-up result

Costs for this group of buyers:
Cash spent + value of sales and setup time = cost to win these buyers
That cost / new paying customers = cost per new customer
Revenue - direct delivery/support cost = contribution
Contribution still has to cover sales costs and fixed bills.
```

Set aside a regular half-hour to review stalled decisions and customer use. This is a suggested routine. Do not mark a deal lost just because a buyer needs time; do remove it from near-term cash expectations when there is no dated commitment.

If several teammates handle the same customer, use the [customer CRM tracker](/en/customers/customer-crm) to keep conversation notes, promises and next actions in one shared record.

## Before increasing sales activity

- [ ] One customer group and workflow are clear.
- [ ] The user, approver and payer are known.
- [ ] The demo completes the customer's task.
- [ ] Price, scope and payment expectations are written down.
- [ ] Each live opportunity has an agreed next step.
- [ ] Acquisition effort includes founder time and setup.
- [ ] Paid use and repeat use are tracked separately.

Start with the first five suitable conversations. Once an offer is accepted, use the [invoice and collection workflow](/en/b2b/invoice-collection) to make the payment path as clear as the product promise.

## Your first ten B2B customers: from contact to collected cash

Use the following worksheet when several deals are moving at once. Ten is a planning target, not a promised outcome or the right target for every product. One account can involve a user, an approver and a payer; count the account once. Keep its internal ID stable from first conversation through invoice collection.

Prepare the offer version, agreed work, any pilot exit criteria, invoice reference, due date and matched payment record. Give each account one next action and owner. Reserve a short weekly review; the sales cycle and collection timing depend on the actual buyer. Include visits, setup and founder time in the acquisition-cost calculation above.

Imagine a separate batch of ten accounts, each considering a BDT 3,000 first invoice. These are fictional counts from one review date, not conversion benchmarks:

| Evidence at the review date | Accounts | What it means |
|---|---:|---|
| Agreement reached | 8 | Scope accepted; payment is not established |
| First invoice issued | 6 | Total billed: BDT 18,000 |
| First invoice fully paid | 4 | BDT 12,000 matched to those invoices |
| First invoice partly paid | 1 | Another BDT 2,000 matched; BDT 1,000 remains |

The sixth invoiced account has paid nothing. Total matched cash is **12,000 + 2,000 = BDT 14,000**; the invoiced balance is **18,000 − 14,000 = BDT 4,000**. Only four accounts have fully paid their first invoice. Agreements, invoicing, cash and useful product use stay separate. These rows overlap and must not be added as different accounts.

If a buyer needs a pilot, write its scope, duration, success evidence, decision-maker and next contract decision. A pilot is optional; do not delay a buyer ready to purchase. Check the agreed documents and payment conditions before sending an invoice. Payment screenshots are leads to investigate, not a substitute for matching the actual receipt. Use the [pilot playbook](/en/b2b/pilot-to-contract) and [collection guide](/en/b2b/invoice-collection) for the detailed procedures.

### Inspect a completed sales cohort

The interactive example below uses a different, fully observed batch: 50 contacted, 30 discovery conversations, 20 scoped offers, 12 agreements, 10 invoices and 8 fully paid first invoices. All later stages are subsets of earlier ones. The observed contact-to-paid rate is **8 ÷ 50 = 16%**. At that same assumed rate, ten paid accounts would require **10 ÷ 0.16 = 62.5**, rounded up to **63 total contacts**. This is capacity-planning arithmetic, not a forecast; it does not specify when money will arrive.

If buyers skip stages or use a different route, use a separate pipeline instead of forcing the counts to fit. A cohort still waiting for its normal collection period is not a completed conversion sample. With no paid accounts, there is no observed paid conversion rate to extrapolate.

Copy this record if JavaScript is unavailable:

```text
Account ID | user / approver / payer roles
Stage evidence / date | offer version | optional pilot exit criteria
Invoice reference / due date | cash matched | remaining balance
First useful product task | next action | owner | follow-up date
```

Review one blockage at a time: unclear fit, absent decision-maker, unapproved scope, missing invoice documents or overdue collection. Do not solve an invoice problem by buying more leads. You are ready to increase activity when the team can explain why deals stall, match receipts, and deliver the work already sold.
