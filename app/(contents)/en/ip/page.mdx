# Intellectual Property: Know Who Owns the Work You Use

> **In short:** Before investing in a name or using someone else's work, find out what rights your business has. Keep the creator, agreement and permitted use together for each important asset. Resolve unclear ownership before a launch or a deal makes it expensive to change.

You have paid for a logo, bought a domain and started building a product with code from several sources. It is tempting to treat everything in the project folder as the company's property. But each item came to you in a different way, and each may carry different conditions.

This matters when you want to change the design, give the code to another developer, license the product or bring in an investor. Having the file answers where the work is stored. It does not necessarily answer who owns it or what you may do with it. A little checking while the work is being commissioned is easier than reconstructing the arrangement after the creator has moved on.

## Start with the work your business depends on

Make a short list of the names, logos, software, designs, writing and other material you would struggle to replace. Beside each, record who created it and how the business obtained it. Find the relevant agreement, licence or written permission and keep a link to it with the record.

Three familiar situations show why this is worth doing. A domain receipt shows that you obtained the domain, but leaves the brand-rights question open. A contractor's invoice records payment, but you still need to read the agreed ownership and usage terms. A code library may be available to download while its licence imposes conditions on use or distribution. The next action differs in each case: check the name, clarify the commissioned work, or review the software licence.

Do this before sending new work to a contractor as well. Explain the intended use and ask for the rights and handover terms to be written into the agreement. Include what files, source material or access you expect to receive, so delivery of a finished image or working website is not mistaken for delivery of everything you need to maintain it.

## Different kinds of work raise different questions

**Intellectual property**, often shortened to IP, concerns rights in creations such as inventions, creative work and commercial signs. A **trademark** distinguishes the source of goods or services. **Copyright** concerns original expression, including writing, artwork and code. A **patent** concerns an invention, while **trade secrets** concern qualifying confidential information. There is no single registration that answers all of those questions.[^ip-basics]

You do not need to choose a legal category for every file yourself. Use the distinction to ask a more useful question. For a product name, ask about clearance and brand protection. For commissioned code, ask who owns the work and what the business may do with it. For confidential know-how, ask who needs access and how it will remain confidential. Eligibility, ownership and enforcement need to be assessed under the applicable law.

Company-name clearance is a separate process from a complete brand-rights review. If you are choosing an entity name, the [name-clearance guide](/en/registration/name-clearance) explains that task. Do not treat its result, or an available domain, as permission to use a brand in every market.

## Resolve the gaps while the work is still easy to change

Mark a missing agreement as a question to resolve, rather than calling the asset “ours” because somebody paid for it. Contact the creator or supplier, explain the use you need and ask for the relevant terms. If the wording is unclear, the asset is central to the business or another party disputes it, take the documents to a qualified IP adviser before relying on the rights.

For an adviser, prepare the actual name or work, the creator's details, existing agreements and the proposed use. Say where you plan to sell or publish it and what decision is waiting. “Can we distribute this library inside our paid product under this licence?” is a question they can assess; “please protect our IP” leaves the important facts unspecified. The [legal and accounting directory](/en/directory/legal-accounting) can help you begin a shortlist.

Founder-created work needs attention too. Discuss what each founder brings into the company and record the agreed rights alongside the [founder agreement](/en/cofounders/founder-agreement). Keep confidential material accessible only to people who need it, and remove access when their work ends.

The specialist IP articles below are planned. For now, start with the assets you are about to use or pay for. Gather their terms and settle the unresolved questions before making the next commitment.
