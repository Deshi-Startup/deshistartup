# Manufacturing: From a Product Idea to a First Batch

> **In short:** A good sample shows that a product can be made. Your first production order tests whether a supplier can make it consistently, at a cost and pace your business can afford. Start with a clear product description, approve a sample, and agree how you will check the finished goods before committing to a larger order.

You have an idea for a physical product: perhaps a bag, a household item or a component another business needs. You may even have a sample that people like. The next problem is getting enough of it made to sell, without discovering too late that the quality varies, delivery is delayed or the full cost leaves no margin.

This section is for that stage. It is especially useful if another workshop or factory will make the product for you. You do not need to own a factory to run a product business, but you do need to be clear about what the supplier is responsible for and what you will check yourself.

If you buy finished goods and resell them unchanged, the supplier checks and cost comparison below still apply. You can usually skip the product-development work. If you are setting up your own factory, this is only an introduction to buying and production decisions; machinery, premises, worker safety and activity-specific approvals need separate planning.

## Start by describing what you want made

A supplier cannot price “a good-quality bag” accurately. They need to know what kind of bag, which material, how many, and what would make it unacceptable. That written description is the **product specification**. It gives you and the supplier something more reliable than a conversation to refer back to.

Suppose you want to sell fabric pouches to offices that put together welcome packs for new employees. For your first order, you are considering 100 pouches. A photograph explains the appearance, but it does not settle the fabric, size, zip, stitching or packaging. Two workshops could follow the same photograph and deliver different products.

Write a short brief covering those details. Include the intended use as well: a pouch meant to hold a notebook must actually fit that notebook. If small differences in size are acceptable, state how much. This permitted variation is called a **tolerance**. Agree it with the maker rather than assuming every unit will be identical.

You can begin with this request and fill in the brackets:

> We need [quantity] of [product] for [use]. The attached drawing or reference shows [appearance]. Please quote for [material, dimensions, finish and packaging], delivered to [location]. State the sample cost, total production cost, payment stages and delivery time. List anything you have excluded or would need to change.

Leave a detail marked “to agree” if you do not know it yet. Ask the supplier to explain the choices and their consequences. A visible question is easier to resolve than two different assumptions hidden inside a quote.

## Use a sample to resolve the important questions

For a supplier you are seriously considering, the next step is a **sample**: a unit you inspect before approving the larger order. For the pouches, use it as the customer would: put the intended items inside, open and close the zip, and examine the seams. A neat photograph would not reveal a zip that catches on the lining.

Describe any problem precisely. “The zip catches at this corner” gives the maker something to correct; “the quality is bad” does not. Ask for a revised sample when the problem affects use, safety or the appearance you have promised customers. Keep the agreed sample, its photographs and the written specification together so that later changes are obvious.

The sample also gives you a better basis for checking the supplier. Ask whether the same materials and production method will be used for the order, which work is subcontracted, and who will answer if something goes wrong. A supplier may make a strong sample yet lack the capacity to deliver your quantity on time. The [partner due-diligence guide](/en/manufacturing/partner-due-diligence) explains how to investigate that capability and check supporting documents.

For food, cosmetics, electrical goods or other products where a failure can harm someone, an informal sample check is not enough to establish safety or compliance. Find out which tests and approvals apply to the actual product and activity before arranging production. Start with the [licenses section](/en/licenses), then confirm the requirements with the relevant authority or a qualified specialist.

## Compare the whole order, not just the unit price

Once both suppliers are quoting for the same product, you can compare the money. In this illustrative pouch example, one workshop quotes BDT 80 per pouch and another quotes BDT 85. The first looks cheaper until you include its separate sample and setup charge.

Here is the comparison for the proposed 100-unit order. These are teaching figures, not current market quotes.

| Quoted cost | Supplier A | Supplier B |
|---|---:|---:|
| 100 pouches | BDT 8,000 | BDT 8,500 |
| Sample and setup | BDT 1,500 | Included |
| Delivery | BDT 500 | BDT 500 |
| **Total quoted** | **BDT 10,000** | **BDT 9,000** |

Supplier B's quoted total is BDT 1,000 lower despite its higher unit price. Check whether tax, packaging, inspection or any other charge is excluded before treating either total as complete. Price still does not settle the decision: a cheaper order that arrives too late for your customer's event is not useful.

Also ask when each payment is due. If the BDT 9,000 order requires half in advance, BDT 4,500 leaves your account before you have goods to sell. You still need enough cash for the remaining payment and other bills. A larger order may offer a lower unit price while putting more of your cash into unsold stock.

## Agree what happens between approval and delivery

“Delivery in two weeks” is incomplete unless both sides know when the clock starts. Does it start when you pay the deposit, approve the sample or supply the final artwork? **Lead time** means the time needed between agreed starting and finishing points. Record those points, any decisions you must make along the way, and the delivery date that follows from them.

Before placing the order, agree the quantity, payment stages and inspection arrangements in writing. State what happens if goods fail the agreed checks: for example, whether the supplier repairs or replaces them, who pays the transport, and when the corrected goods are due. Do not assume sample approval gives you a particular refund or replacement right; the terms need to be agreed. Obtain legal advice for a substantial or complex commitment.

Choose a first-order quantity small enough that a delay or failed batch would be manageable. The supplier's minimum order may be larger than you can responsibly buy. If so, ask about a smaller trial, a simpler design or another production method before accepting the larger commitment.

## Check the batch before treating it as stock you can sell

When the pouches arrive, compare them with the approved sample and specification. Count them, record defects and separate questionable units from goods ready for sale. The inspection method should match the product's risks and the agreement with the supplier; do not invent an acceptance percentage after finding a problem.

The number of usable units matters to your cost. If the BDT 9,000 order leaves only 90 saleable pouches and you recover none of the cost of the other ten, the batch cost is **9,000 ÷ 90 = BDT 100 per usable pouch**, before your selling costs. It is not BDT 90 simply because 100 units arrived. Resolve the defective units with the supplier and update the calculation when you know the actual repair, replacement or credit. Before reordering, compare that cost plus any packaging, delivery, payment fees and likely return costs not already included with the price customers will actually pay. The [unit-economics guide](/en/metrics/unit-economics) shows how to work out what each sale leaves to cover the rest of the business.

For your next step, choose the guide that matches where the work has stopped:

- **You cannot get comparable quotes:** use [supplier sourcing](/en/manufacturing/supplier-sourcing) to prepare the request and compare offers.
- **You have a possible supplier but need to check them:** use [partner due diligence](/en/manufacturing/partner-due-diligence).
- **You are about to approve or receive goods:** use the [first-batch quality checklist](/en/manufacturing/quality-control) to record the checks and results.

If you are starting today, write the product brief and take it to a potential supplier. The first useful result is knowing what can be made, what remains undecided and what a sample will cost. That gives you a basis for the production decision that comes next.
