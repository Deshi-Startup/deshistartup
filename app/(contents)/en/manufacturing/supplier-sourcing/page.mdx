# Local Supplier Sourcing Checklist

> **In short:** Write down exactly what you need before asking for prices. Send the same brief to each supplier. Check samples, who will make the goods and the full order cost. Start with an order you can afford to test. A low price per item can still tie up too much cash or leave you with goods you cannot use.

## Copy the supplier request

Use this if your startup needs locally made parts, packaging or other physical goods. It helps you compare suppliers for an early batch. Food, medicine, electrical items and other products with safety rules need the right specialist checks as well.

A **specification**, or spec, is a written description of what the item must be. Give each version a date or number so everyone works from the same one.

```text
Our business and contact:
Item name and code:
Spec version and date:
What the item will be used for:

Material and any required proof:
Size and allowed variation:
Colour, finish and packaging:
Sample, drawing or photo reference:
Checks the finished item must pass:
Changes that need our written approval:

Quantity needed now:
Possible later orders, clearly not a promise:
Delivery location and required date:

Please quote:
- Price per item and minimum order
- Sample and setup costs
- Delivery, tax and other charges
- When the delivery clock starts
- Payment stages
- How defects, rework and shortages are handled
- Who will make the goods and any subcontracted work
- How long this quote remains valid
```

**Minimum order quantity (MOQ)** means the smallest number of units the supplier will accept in an order. It can matter more to your cash plan than the unit price.

## Start with a clear item, not a photo alone

A photo may show colour and shape but not material, thickness, strength or an exact size. “Same as this picture” gives both sides room to make different assumptions.

In a 2024 Future Startup interview, Ohmtech founder Wasiul Haque Bhuiyan described difficulty finding parts and getting their specifications.[^ohmtech-sourcing] That is a practical reason to ask what can be proved about an input before designing a product around it.

Separate what must be exact from what can vary. For a simple cloth storage pouch, the size and zip may be essential while a small shade difference is acceptable. Put the allowed difference in writing. Do not use the same informal check for a safety-critical part.

## Find candidates, then check the actual work

Ask makers, industry contacts and businesses using similar inputs for introductions. Look at relevant business directories, supplier markets, associations and trade events. These routes produce names to check, not automatic endorsements.

Find out whether you are dealing with the producer, a reseller or an agent. Each may be useful, but the promises differ. Ask who controls production, who handles defects and who will receive payment.

Where practical, visit the actual site or arrange a live view of the work. Ask permission before taking photos. Look for work similar to your order, how goods are kept separate and who checks quality. A neat office or a sample in a showroom does not prove that the next batch will match it.

Check the supplier's business identity, invoice details and authorized contact. Confirm new bank details through a contact you already know. Ask for references with permission, then discuss a relevant delivery, quality issue and how it was resolved.

## Send the same request to each supplier

Compare quotes using the same item, quantity basis, quality rules and delivery point. If a supplier proposes a different material or size, mark it as a different option. Do not quietly place it in the same price column.

Ask whether the delivery estimate starts after the quote, deposit, approved sample or arrival of materials. “Ten days” is unclear without a start event and a definition of working days.

Keep unanswered points visible. Do not mark “good quality” just because the salesperson says so. Record the sample, test or reference behind the view.

## Worked example: cheap units, a larger cash promise

Imagine a Dhaka startup needs 500 cloth storage pouches for a first batch. It has a BDT 40,000 total order budget. The quotes below are made up and assume all applicable charges are included.

Both suppliers quote the same written spec. Their samples still need to pass your checks.

| Quote item | Supplier A | Supplier B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit price | BDT 60 | BDT 68 |
| Minimum order | 1,000 | 500 |
| Goods cost | BDT 60,000 | BDT 34,000 |
| Setup | BDT 2,000 | BDT 0 |
| Delivery | BDT 1,000 | BDT 1,000 |
| Total order cost | **BDT 63,000** | **BDT 35,000** |
| Cost per ordered unit | BDT 63 | BDT 70 |

A is cheaper per unit, but requires twice the quantity you need. The order commits **BDT 28,000 more cash** than B. It also leaves you with 500 extra units to store and sell.

Suppose both ask for 50% upfront. A's deposit is BDT 31,500 and B's is BDT 17,500. Both deposits fit inside BDT 40,000. But A's full bill does not. Do not judge affordability from the first payment alone.

B fits the order budget if its sample, delivery date and terms also fit. The remaining BDT 5,000 must cover any extra checks and costs not included in the assumed quote. If those costs exceed it, reduce the order or find another workable plan before committing.

## Approve a sample and keep it

Ask for a sample made using the material and process proposed for your order. Mark the sample and spec version both sides approved. Keep photos and a record of the checks, but retain the actual sample where useful.

One good sample does not prove a whole batch will be good. Ask how the supplier keeps later units consistent and what happens if a material changes.

Khaas Food's founder described trying suppliers before longer agreements and checking incoming goods in a 2017 interview.[^khaas-sourcing] The useful lesson is to test the relationship and deliveries. The food claims in that interview are not a substitute for product-safety tests.

For a small first order, agree the quantity, price, payment stages, delivery date, defect handling and who approves changes. Get suitable help for contract terms, ownership of tooling or designs, and larger commitments.

## Inspect before mixing the batch into stock

Label the delivery so you can trace it to the supplier, order and date. Count it. Check it against the approved spec and sample. Keep unapproved or failed goods separate from stock you can sell.

Record shortages and defects with the supplier's reference. Follow the agreed process for a correction, replacement, credit or return. Do not assume all costs will be refunded just because you found a problem.

Use the [first-batch quality checklist](/en/manufacturing/quality-control) for the inspection record and release decision.

## Keep a backup without doubling stock

Record another possible supplier for key inputs, but test their version before treating it as a direct replacement. Two items with the same name may use different material or fit differently.

You do not need to order a large stockpile from every backup. Keep contact details, checked capabilities, sample status and the time needed to place a real order.

Allow time for the brief, quotes, sample checks and the supplier's actual production cycle. Budget samples, visits and any specialist tests. Skipping these steps can move cost into rejected stock later.

## Before placing the order

- [ ] Every quote uses the same spec or clearly states its differences.
- [ ] We know who produces, approves changes and handles defects.
- [ ] The sample and required tests are approved.
- [ ] MOQ, full cost and later payments fit the cash plan.
- [ ] Delivery dates have a clear starting event.
- [ ] Defect and change terms are written down.
- [ ] Incoming goods will be checked and kept traceable.

Start by finishing the spec for one important item. Then request comparable quotes rather than asking everyone only for their lowest price.
