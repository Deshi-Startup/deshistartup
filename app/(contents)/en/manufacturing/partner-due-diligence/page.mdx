# Manufacturing Partner Due Diligence: Checks Before You Sign

> **In short:** Before you sign with a factory, verify four things in this order — that the business legally exists and may operate, that it can actually make your volume, that it can prove quality, and that the contract puts your tooling, your brand and your specification on your side of the table. Two of the most important checks are free and take ten minutes: Bangladesh's factory-inspection department and environment department both publish licence verification services, so you never have to take a photocopied licence on trust.[^dife-lima] [^doe] The most expensive mistake is paying an advance against a minimum order quantity you have not sized against real demand. Start with the ten-document table below.

Say Rafi has a small packaged-food brand in Dhaka. His kitchen-scale production stopped working at about 3,000 packs a month, and a contract manufacturer in Gazipur has offered to take over — good sample, warm meeting, a quoted price BDT 6 lower per pack than he expected, and a request for 40% advance against a first order of 20,000 packs.

Everything about that offer is normal. Two things in it can still cost him his year: the factory's paperwork, which he has not seen, and the order quantity, which he has not compared to his own demand. This guide is the check he should run first.

## Who needs this, and when

- **You have shortlisted one to three manufacturers and are close to committing.** This guide is for you.
- **You are still looking for candidates.** Go to [local supplier sourcing](/manufacturing/supplier-sourcing) first; come back when you have names.
- **Your counterparty is abroad and you are importing.** Different risk, different checks — see [supplier verification](/trade/supplier-verification), which covers foreign-counterparty and sourcing fraud.
- **You have already signed and want a specific shipment checked.** That is [pre-shipment inspection](/trade/pre-shipment-inspection).
- **You have signed and are planning the first production run.** That is [prototype to first batch](/manufacturing/first-batch).
- **You are having a one-off sample made.** Skip most of this. Run it when the relationship becomes repeat orders or an advance payment.

This page owns one decision: **do we appoint this manufacturer, and on what terms.** It ends the moment the contract is signed.

**What you will have at the end:** verified licence numbers, a capacity calculation in your own numbers, a scored comparison of your candidates, and a term list to negotiate.

## What you are actually choosing

Three arrangements get called "manufacturing partner" and they carry different risks.

| Arrangement | What it means | Your main risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Contract manufacturing** | They make *your* recipe or design, to your specification, under your brand | Specification drift and quality control; the paperwork may be in their name, not yours |
| **White label** | They make an existing product of theirs and put your label on it | You do not control the formula, and neither does your customer's trust in you |
| **Job work / toll processing** | You supply the inputs, they run one process step | Yield loss, wastage accounting, and material substitution |

If you are building a brand, contract manufacturing is usually the right answer and white label is the tempting one. White label gets you to market fastest and gives you the least to defend later — the same product is normally available to anyone else who asks that factory.

Whichever you pick, treat the choice as an operating decision rather than a purchasing one. Practitioners who run supply chains at scale in Bangladesh make the same point about early-stage companies: procurement and supplier selection sit alongside production and delivery as the places where a young product business actually fails.[^ab-supply-chain]

## The ten documents, and how to check them yourself

Ask for all of these as a set, in one email, before the second meeting. A serious factory sends them in a day; a factory that takes three weeks to find its own fire licence is telling you something.

| # | Document | Issued by | How you verify it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trade licence, current year | City corporation, municipality or union parishad | Check the expiry date and that the address matches the factory you visited |
| 2 | Certificate of incorporation and <Term name="form-xii">Form XII</Term> | RJSC | Confirms the entity and who its directors are; see [private limited registration](/registration/private-limited) for what these look like |
| 3 | <Term name="e-tin">e-TIN</Term> certificate | NBR | Basic tax identity — see [e-TIN](/tax/e-tin) |
| 4 | BIN / VAT registration | NBR | You will need it to claim input VAT on their invoices — see [VAT and BIN](/tax/vat-bin) |
| 5 | **Factory licence** | Department of Inspection for Factories and Establishments (DIFE) | **Verify the number yourself** at the DIFE licence-verification service[^dife-lima] |
| 6 | Fire licence | Fire Service and Civil Defence | Check the expiry and the covered premises |
| 7 | **Environmental clearance**, if the category needs one | Department of Environment (DoE) | Category and validity under the Environment Conservation Rules 2023; DoE publishes a certificate verification service[^doe] [^ecr-2023] |
| 8 | **BSTI CM licence**, if your product is on the mandatory list | BSTI | Check the current mandatory list, then the office-wise list of licensed firms[^bsti-mandatory] [^bsti-licensed] |
| 9 | Packaged-commodity registration, if you sell pre-packed goods | BSTI metrology wing | Ask for the certificate; it is separate from the CM licence[^bsti-licensed] |
| 10 | Sector licence, if your category has its own regulator | For food, the Bangladesh Food Safety Authority; otherwise the authority named in your product's own rules | Confirm the licence covers your exact product category, not a neighbouring one[^bfsa] |

Rows 5, 7 and 8 are the ones people skip, and they are the three that can stop your product from being sold. Everything else on that list mainly affects whether your invoices work.

### The two free verifications

**Factory licence.** DIFE runs LIMA, its labour inspection management application. It publishes a public page where you enter a licence or registration number and see whether it is real.[^dife-lima] Applications for a factory licence are made on Form-77 and layout-plan approvals on Form-76, so a factory that says "our licence is in process" should be able to show you a filed Form-77 with a date on it.[^dife-lima]

**Environmental clearance.** Under the Environment Conservation Rules 2023, industrial units are classified Green, Yellow, Orange or Red by environmental impact, and a clearance certificate is required accordingly.[^ecr-2023] The Department of Environment links a certificate-verification service from its own site.[^doe] When I checked on 14 August 2026 that verification service did not respond, so treat it as best-effort: if it is down, ask for the certificate and confirm it with the divisional DoE office before you rely on it.

**BSTI.** Two separate things matter, and founders confuse them. First, whether *your product* is on the mandatory certification list — that list is set and extended by gazette, most recently on 23 June 2026, so a blog's list from last year is not evidence.[^bsti-mandatory] Second, whether *this factory* holds a current CM licence for that product. BSTI publishes office-wise lists of licensed firms and issues licences carrying a QR code, so both are checkable.[^bsti-licensed]

> **Warning:** If your product is on the mandatory list, settle *whose name the CM licence sits in* before you sign, not after your first batch is packed. A licence held by the factory for its own brand is not obviously a licence for your brand coming off the same line. Put the question to BSTI's CM wing in writing and keep the answer with the contract.[^bsti-licensed]

## Capacity: the arithmetic they will not volunteer

"We can make 60,000 packs a month" is an installed-capacity number. What you need to know is the *free* capacity in the months you need it.

Ask three questions and write down the answers:

1. What was your actual monthly output for the last six months?
2. What share of that is committed to existing customers?
3. What is your changeover time between products, and how many changeovers a week do you run?

Rafi's factory answers: installed 60,000 packs a month, actual output averaging 51,000, of which about 44,000 is committed.

```
Installed capacity                       60,000 packs/month
Actual recent output                     51,000
Committed to existing customers          44,000
Genuinely free capacity                  51,000 - 44,000  =  7,000 packs/month
Rafi's month-3 requirement                                   12,000 packs/month
```

The factory is not lying, and it also cannot serve him by month three. That gap is the single most useful number due diligence produces, and it is invisible from the capacity figure on the brochure. His options are to stage the ramp, get a written capacity reservation, or keep looking — and all three are cheaper than discovering it in month three.

**Keep in mind:** ask which of their customers is largest as a share of their output. A factory where one client is 60% of volume will always serve that client first when a raw material runs short. You will be the flexible one.

## Quality: what "good" has to mean in writing

Quality disputes are nearly always specification disputes wearing a different hat. Before signing, agree in writing:

- **The specification sheet.** Ingredients or materials with grades and sources, dimensions with tolerances, weight with allowed variance, packaging material, print colours, shelf life.
- **The retained sample.** One approved physical sample, signed and dated by both sides, held by each. Every later argument is settled against it.
- **The acceptance standard.** How many defective units in a batch is acceptable, how a defect is defined, and who counts. Agree the sampling method, not just the number.
- **Who decides.** Name the person on each side who may accept or reject a batch, and how long they have to do it.
- **The remedy.** Rework, replacement, credit note, or refund — and the deadline for each.

The mechanics of running that check on the actual production run belong to [quality control for the first run](/manufacturing/quality-control) and [prototype to first batch](/manufacturing/first-batch). What belongs *here* is confirming, before you sign, that the factory can produce the evidence at all: batch records, in-process checks, calibrated scales, a test report for each production lot. A factory with no batch numbering system cannot give you traceability later, however sincerely it promises to.

## The terms that decide who owns what

Price is the term founders negotiate hardest and the one that matters least in year two. These matter more:

| Term | Why it bites later | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| **Tooling, mould and die ownership** | You pay for the die; they keep it and you cannot leave | "Tooling paid for by the buyer is the buyer's property, released on written request" — with the amount and item listed |
| **Formula and design ownership** | Your recipe becomes their next product | Assign IP in the specification to you; add confidentiality and non-use |
| **Minimum order quantity** | Ties up cash in stock you cannot sell yet | Negotiate the first three orders explicitly, not a standing MOQ |
| **Price validity** | Quoted price expires the moment inputs move | Fix a validity period and name the input index that may reopen it |
| **Lead time and late delivery** | A missed campaign costs more than the batch | State working days from purchase order and confirmed input supply, and the remedy for a miss |
| **Payment terms** | Large advances transfer all risk to you | Split advance / on-approval / on-delivery, tied to the quality gate |
| **Exclusivity** | They make an identical product for a competitor next month | If you cannot get category exclusivity, get non-use of *your* specification and packaging |
| **Termination and exit** | You are locked in with your mould behind their gate | Notice period, return of tooling and materials, and a final-order right |

The pattern is that every row is about what happens when the relationship ends. Negotiate the exit while everyone is happy; nobody negotiates it well later.

The amber band is the discipline the whole guide exists to protect: a paid trial run is a cost, and an advance against 20,000 units is a bet.

**Visit the factory yourself, at least once, unannounced-ish** — confirm the day, not the hour. Look for: the licences framed on the wall and their dates, whether the production line matches the capacity claimed, raw-material storage conditions, whether workers are wearing what the process requires, whether there is a functioning safety committee, and whether the place is running today. Photograph the line with permission. Ask for two customer references in your category and call them.

## What the numbers do to Rafi

His demand plan needs 12,000 packs a month by month three. The factory proposes an MOQ of 20,000 at BDT 42 a pack.

```
MOQ order value            20,000 x 42  =  BDT 8,40,000
Month-3 requirement        12,000 x 42  =  BDT 5,04,000
Cash tied up in stock he does not yet need  =  BDT 3,36,000
40% advance requested on the MOQ            =  BDT 3,36,000
```

He is being asked to pay, as an advance, exactly the amount of surplus stock the MOQ creates — and to do it before a trial run. The negotiation therefore has nothing to do with the BDT 42. It is: a first order of 12,000, a paid trial run before that, and the advance split so that a meaningful part is due only after the batch passes the agreed acceptance standard.

The second number that changes his behaviour is what the paperwork is worth. If the factory turns out to lack the licence his product needs, the batch sitting in his warehouse is BDT 8.4 lakh of stock he cannot legally sell — which is why checks 5 to 10 come before any money moves.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Safe action |
|---|---|---|
| **Taking photocopied licences on trust** | Expired or borrowed documents surface during a market inspection | Verify the factory licence number online, and check every expiry date yourself[^dife-lima] |
| **Reading installed capacity as available capacity** | Your orders quietly queue behind bigger customers | Do the free-capacity subtraction above, and get a written capacity reservation |
| **Paying a large advance before a trial run** | Your money is inside a relationship you have not tested | Pay for a trial run; tie the advance to the contract and the quality gate |
| **Accepting the factory's MOQ as fixed** | Cash converted into stock months before demand | Negotiate the first three orders separately |
| **Letting the factory own the mould you paid for** | Switching supplier means paying for tooling twice | Write tooling ownership and release into the contract |
| **Assuming the factory's BSTI licence covers your brand** | Product cannot legally be sold under your label | Get the position in writing from BSTI's CM wing before signing[^bsti-licensed] |
| **No signed specification or retained sample** | Every quality dispute becomes one person's word | Sign and date two physical samples on the day you agree the spec |

Five of these seven are things you can fix for free, before any money moves. That is the whole argument for spending three weeks on due diligence instead of three days.
