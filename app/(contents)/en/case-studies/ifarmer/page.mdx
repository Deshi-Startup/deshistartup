# iFarmer

<CaseCompany slug="ifarmer" locale="en" />

> **In short:** iFarmer did not start in muddy village rice fields. It started on concrete rooftops in Dhaka. In early 2018, Fahad Ifaz and Jamil Akbar launched an "Uber for Urban Farming." But within six months, they realized rooftop gardening was just an expensive hobby for city dwellers. In August 2018, they made a bold change (<Term name="pivot">pivot</Term>): they turned to Bangladesh's real farming heartland, helping the 16 million smallholder farmers trapped in poverty.[^pivot] Starting with simple crowd-funded farm loans, iFarmer tracked farmer crop cycles on smartphones, eventually convincing big commercial banks to lend money to village farmers.[^banks-2025] Today, the startup helps farmers get bank loans, supplies certified seeds and fertilizer, and sells harvested crops directly to food factories across 42 districts.[^current-site] The key lesson: solve one urgent bottleneck first, earn customer trust, and use that data to unlock adjacent services.

## Problem

Smallholder farmers in Bangladesh face a tangled web of problems. Just getting a loan is not enough if you can only buy fake seeds, receive no advice when pests strike, or get cheated by dishonest middlemen at harvest time. When iFarmer changed course, it realized that you cannot fix farm credit without fixing the whole supply chain.[^agfunder-2022]

Traditional banks avoid village farmers because farming feels too risky. Without official tax records, land deeds, or credit scores, banks have no way to know if a farmer will harvest a good crop or pay back a loan. iFarmer built a system to give banks the digital data they needed to lend safely.[^banks-2025]

## Why the problem was Bangladesh-specific

Agriculture is the backbone of Bangladesh. It employs over 40% of the workforce across 16 million smallholder families. Yet commercial bank branches stop at district towns. Smallholders farming less than one acre have no formal business accounts or land titles to put up as collateral, leaving them shut out of bank loans.[^banks-2025]

Instead, farmers historically relied on local village moneylenders (*mohajons*) who charged punishing interest rates of 60% to 100% per year. Or they borrowed from local fertilizer dealers who demanded to buy the harvested crop at rock-bottom prices. Because village life runs on cash and personal relationships, a Silicon Valley-style app alone could never work. iFarmer had to combine mobile software with real field officers, village shopkeepers, and local grain collection centers.[^sofol-2021]

## Founding insight

The key insight arrived only after the founders' first idea failed. Fahad Ifaz had spent ten years working on agricultural market programs for Swisscontact, CARE, and the World Bank. When their rooftop gardening project hit a wall, the founders realized their real advantage lay in solving the rural farming finance problems Fahad had studied for a decade.[^founder-2020]

<CaseDetail title="The pivot changed the customer, not just the feature set">

The 2018 pivot was a complete restart. The original customer was an affluent Dhaka apartment owner with an empty rooftop. The new customer was a village farmer needing money for fertilizer, authentic seeds, and fair crop buyers, alongside banks looking for reliable farm data.[^pivot]

For founders, this is a vital lesson: a true <Term name="pivot">pivot</Term> does not mean changing button colors on a website. It means having the courage to walk away from a tiny market and focus on a huge, painful problem where you have real expertise.

</CaseDetail>

## First wedge

iFarmer's starting wedge was funding farm projects. By connecting financing directly to seasonal crops or cattle-fattening projects, iFarmer gave farmers immediate cash while gathering valuable data on soil quality, crop cycles, and farm revenues.[^founder-2020]

Financing was the key that unlocked everything else. Once iFarmer helped a farmer get funding for a crop, the company had a strong reason to make sure the crop grew well. That meant delivering authentic fertilizer, monitoring plant diseases, and finding food companies to buy the harvest. Financing pulled the startup naturally into selling farm supplies, giving farming advice, and buying wholesale crops.[^agfunder-2022]

## Early customers

Signing up early users meant overcoming deep suspicion on both sides. Village farmers did not trust city tech workers promising money, and city investors were nervous about funding distant farms they had never visited.[^founder-2023]

iFarmer solved farmer sign-ups by hiring local field facilitators. Field officers visited villages, met reliable farmers, checked national IDs, and measured farm plots. This hands-on field process was later built into the Sofol smartphone app for agents.[^sofol-2021]

To find money for early loans, iFarmer turned to city professionals in Dhaka through a website. It let everyday urban investors sponsor specific farm projects (like raising a cow or growing chili peppers) in return for a share of the harvest profits.[^founder-2020]

Sofol was originally planned as an app for farmers themselves. But because many farmers did not own smartphones or know how to use apps, iFarmer switched to an "assisted digital" model: trained field agents and village shopkeepers used the app on behalf of the farmers.[^sofol-2021]

Years later, as cheap 4G smartphones spread to rural areas, iFarmer launched the Folon app in November 2024, letting farmers check weather alerts, get farming tips, and track their loans directly.[^folon-2024]

## Trust building

Trust in farming comes from seeing things with your own eyes. By profiling smallholders, mapping farm boundaries on GPS, and recording fertilizer use, iFarmer showed banks that rural farmers were reliable borrowers.[^banks-2025]

This proof led to a major pilot with Mutual Trust Bank in 2021. iFarmer reported that nearly 100% of farmers in the pilot paid back their loans on time. While pilot results involve carefully selected farmers, it proved to bank managers that lending to village smallholders could work when paired with careful tracking.[^banks-2025]

Big institutional partnerships reinforced this trust. Partnering with Visa and United Commercial Bank (UCB) to issue digital farmer debit cards, securing international loans, and winning the OPEC Fund Innovation Award showed banks and suppliers that iFarmer met strict professional standards.[^farmer-card-2024][^opec-2026]

## Local lessons

**Drop trendy ideas fast.** Rooftop farming was fashionable and got nice news coverage, but it was too small to become a real business. The founders walked away and tackled a massive, complicated rural industry instead.[^pivot]

**Build where you have deep experience.** Fahad’s years working in agriculture helped iFarmer understand complicated village supply chains that software engineers from Dhaka could not figure out.[^founder-2020]

**Help users use the technology before expecting them to do it alone.** Building an app for an illiterate farmer fails; giving digital tools to trusted local shopkeepers and field staff creates instant distribution.[^sofol-2021]

**Do the work that makes a partnership useful.** Banks wanted to lend to farmers but lacked the field network to verify and monitor many small loans. iFarmer brought that network, helping banks reach borrowers they struggled to serve.[^banks-2025]

## Constraints, mistakes and complexity

**The unscalable first attempt.** The original rooftop farming startup closed within six months after the founders realized that managing landlords and small rooftop gardens could never make real money.[^pivot]

**The weather insurance mistake.** In an honest interview, Fahad called weather micro-insurance one of iFarmer's biggest missteps. Index-based insurance was confusing for farmers, paying out claims took too long, and running the policies lost money without government subsidies.[^founder-2023]

**Managing physical goods takes serious cash.** Delivering truckloads of fertilizer and buying hundreds of tons of corn takes warehouses, trucks, and lots of cash (<Term name="working-capital">working capital</Term>). The loans from Pioneer Facility ($500k) and Symbiotics ($1.5M) show that inventory-heavy agriculture requires low-interest bank loans rather than selling company equity.[^pioneer-2025][^symbiotics-2026]

**Different ways of reporting numbers.** Founder interviews in late 2023 cited roughly 6 billion taka in total loans facilitated, while a 2024 retrospective cited 2.45 billion taka, and the website today cites over 11.5 billion taka.[^founder-2023][^pivot][^current-metrics] The reports do not establish a shared reporting period or definition, so these figures cannot be read as a year-by-year trend.

Founders should always distinguish total sign-ups from active seasonal borrowers, and cumulative loan totals from money currently out in the field.

**The danger of doing too much at once.** Managing loans, selling fertilizer, buying crops, writing software, and exporting food creates immense operational strain. Fahad admitted that during rapid growth, the company risked chasing total sales volume and venture valuations instead of watching profit on each farm.[^founder-2023]

## How the model fits together

By 2026, iFarmer had grown into an end-to-end agriculture platform with four main pillars:

1. **Farm Financing:** Connects commercial banks to village farmers, handling digital ID checks (<Term name="kyc">KYC</Term>) and crop monitoring so banks can lend without building rural branches.[^current-site]
2. **Farm Supplies (KriShop):** Sells authentic seeds, fertilizers, and pest treatments directly to over 30,000 village retail shops, cutting out fake products and high markups.[^current-site]
3. **Crop Buying and Wholesale:** Buys harvested corn, potatoes, and chili at local collection hubs and sells them in bulk to flour mills and animal feed factories.
4. **Farming Advice and Apps:** Gives localized weather alerts, planting advice, and pest help through the Folon app, gathering farmer data that helps with credit scoring.[^folon-2024]

This connected loop protects the business: good seeds and farming advice lead to bigger harvests, lower loan defaults, and guaranteed buyers so farmers have cash to repay their bank loans on time.

## Business model

iFarmer earns loan commissions and fees on agricultural input and crop sales:[^agfunder-2022]

1. **Financing facilitation fees:** iFarmer earns a commission for arranging loans. Its fieldwork includes finding farmers, verifying their identities (<Term name="kyc">KYC</Term>), and monitoring loans where banks lack a local network.
2. **Agricultural input sales:** iFarmer distributes authentic seeds, fertilizer, and crop protection from major manufacturers to its network of rural retail shops (KriShop), earning wholesale distribution margins (<Term name="gross-margin">gross margin</Term>).
3. **Output market linkage:** Buying harvested crops like maize, potatoes, and chili from farmers and supplying institutional buyers, food processors, and feed mills in bulk, earning a trading margin on wholesale volumes.

**Advisory and data support these services.** Farm monitoring and advice help farmers manage their crops and give lending partners information about how farms are doing.

## Capital

iFarmer moved from raising venture equity to borrowing debt as it grew. In May 2022, it raised $2.1 million in pre-Series A funding led by IDLC Venture Capital Fund, with Millville Opportunities and Startup Bangladesh.[^agfunder-2022]

As business volume grew, the founders began borrowing debt capital to finance inventory. In 2025, Pioneer Facility provided a $500,000 three-year loan, followed in April 2026 by a $1.5 million facility from international impact fund Symbiotics.[^pioneer-2025][^symbiotics-2026]

In June 2026, the OPEC Fund awarded iFarmer $250,000 through its Innovation for Development Award, giving it grant funding for new farming technology tests.[^opec-2026]

This funding path is a smart blueprint: use venture capital equity to build software and hire teams, and use low-cost debt loans to buy fertilizer inventory and grain shipments.

## Where iFarmer stands now

iFarmer reports over 310,000 registered farmers, 30,000 registered input shops, 11.5 billion+ taka in facilitated loans, 256,000+ tons of crops sold, and operations in 42 districts.[^current-metrics] The company has also started exporting produce to regional markets and packaging staple foods under its own label.[^current-site]

These are company-reported numbers. Audited financial statements showing annual net profits, unpaid loan defaults, and profit margins for each division remain private.

Recent financing deals prove strong outside trust: the $1.5 million Symbiotics loan in April 2026 and the OPEC Fund award in June 2026 show continued support from international development institutions.[^symbiotics-2026][^opec-2026]

## What to copy

- Kill an unviable first idea quickly when you realize the market is too small.
- Pivot to a problem where your team has genuine, real-world industry experience.
- Start with one painful bottleneck (farm financing) instead of trying to build an all-in-one app on day one.
- Use daily business transactions to gather proprietary data that makes big banks want to partner with you.
- Use field agents and local shopkeepers to bridge the gap for customers who do not own smartphones or read easily.
- Use debt loans instead of giving away company shares once you need cash to buy physical inventory.
- Make money at several points along the supply chain to build a resilient business.

## What not to copy

- Do not stick with a failing business idea just because you already spent time writing code.
- Do not assume every related idea (like weather micro-insurance) will work without educating customers first.
- Do not assume high repayment rates in a small pilot test will automatically hold true across thousands of new borrowers.
- Do not confuse registered farmer counts with active, paying users.
- Do not share total transaction numbers without showing the exact time period they cover.
- Do not let a story about software hide the massive cash needed to store real fertilizer and grain.
- Do not mistake winning international awards or securing debt loans for running a profitable company.

## Still unknown

Audited company profits, actual loan default rates across bank lending programs, and crop losses from spoilage are not public.

Public reports do not show daily active user numbers for the Folon app or total payment card spending on the Visa/UCB farmer cards.[^folon-2024][^farmer-card-2024]

While iFarmer has announced produce exports abroad, specific export destinations, shipment volumes, and profits are not publicly audited.[^current-site]

These gaps are normal for private companies, but they are important details for institutional lenders and investors looking closely at the business.
