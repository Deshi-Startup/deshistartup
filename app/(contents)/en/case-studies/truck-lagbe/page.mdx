# Truck Lagbe

<CaseCompany slug="truck-lagbe" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Truck Lagbe began with a price puzzle: the cost of moving goods depended partly on where the truck needed to go next.[^early] Matching loads more carefully could reduce wasted travel. Building a business around that insight required much more: trust on both sides, useful booking details, dependable payment and support when a trip went wrong.

A truck that has finished a delivery still has to go somewhere. If it returns empty, that travel earns nothing. If another load fits the route, timing and vehicle, the same journey can become paid work.

Truck Lagbe connects shippers - the people or businesses sending goods - with truck owners and drivers. It also arranges transport for larger businesses.[^ifc-review] A suitable truck is the start of the job. The goods still have to arrive, and the people doing the work need to get paid.

## A price puzzle led to the company

Anayet Rashid had seen transport costs affect an existing family business. In an IDLC interview, he described how truck hire could change the profit on the goods being moved.[^idlc]

He recalled two shipments to Jessore a week apart. One truck expected to return empty after the delivery; the other was already heading home to Jessore. The same destination produced very different quotes.[^early]

Finding more of these matches could help both sides. The shipper could pay less, while the owner earned money from a journey the truck needed to make anyway.

An empty truck is only useful if it can carry the load and reach the pickup on time. Waiting for cargo may cost the owner more than taking another job. Truck Lagbe had to account for those details before a potential match could become a worthwhile trip.

## Early interest was easier to find than a working service

While the founders were still turning their market research into a service, winning a startup competition brought calls from shippers and truck owners. The team was not yet ready to handle them.[^early]

The attention put pressure on the team to get the service working. People calling about a shipment needed a truck they could book.

By March 2021, iFarmer was describing its use of Truck Lagbe as it built an agricultural supply business.[^ifarmer] Using an outside transport network let a growing business arrange deliveries without first assembling a fleet.

For a business like this, arranging a truck is one part of fulfilling a customer's order. Time saved calling drivers can go back into running the business.

## The trust problem existed on both sides

The founders expected shippers to worry about the safety of their goods. What surprised them, Anayet told IDLC, was that truck owners feared losing their vehicles. Truck Lagbe began verifying both sides and teaching people to use the app so they could help others learn it too.[^idlc]

Truck Lagbe's September 2020 explanation of shipper verification makes the owner's concern concrete. An unknown customer could post a false trip, waste the driver's journey or ask for illegal goods to be moved. The company described checking contact details and making separate agreements with some business shippers.[^verification]

The customer is not the only party taking a risk. The owner puts a valuable asset on the road; the driver gives time and takes responsibility for the journey. If either side distrusts the transaction, the match will not happen.

For a marketplace founder, this changes onboarding. Making it effortless for buyers to post requests may look good in a conversion chart. If those requests waste providers' time, the supply side becomes less willing to respond. A little justified friction can protect the quality of the network.

## Useful details beat a generic booking form

The current booking FAQ asks for the load and unload points, vehicle category, date, time and product type. Owners then bid, and the shipper chooses a suitable offer.[^booking]

Those fields do different jobs. Location narrows the route. Timing checks whether both sides are ready. Product details help prevent a vehicle arriving for cargo it cannot carry.

Two choices Anayet described in 2021 brought the app closer to how customers already worked: familiar local place names and the option to rehire a driver.[^evolution] A landmark can be easier to find than an official address, and a customer may prefer someone who handled the last delivery well.

The rehire feature also exposes a marketplace risk. Once two people meet, they may deal directly next time. Helping them work together again means competing on continuing usefulness, rather than hoping they never exchange phone numbers.

A platform earns its place when the booking record, support, payment process or access to other jobs makes staying worthwhile. Charging for the introduction alone becomes harder after the introduction has happened.

## Different trips need different pricing

Truck Lagbe's May 2021 product explanation described both bidding and fixed pricing for one-ton pickups within Dhaka.[^rates]

The combination makes sense. Bidding can reflect the changing circumstances of a longer or less standard trip. A fixed price can make a familiar, repeated job quicker to book. The choice is not simply between “manual” and “automated.” It depends on how much uncertainty the business can responsibly remove.

| A trip needs an answer to… | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Can this vehicle carry this load? | Size alone may not establish safe carrying capacity. |
| Can it reach the pickup at the agreed time? | A delayed truck or delayed cargo ties up the other side. |
| What work does the price include? | Waiting, extra stops and loading arrangements can change the job. |
| What can the vehicle do afterward? | The next useful trip affects the owner's overall earnings. |

This is our explanation of the matching problem, based on the service's booking and pricing model.[^booking][^rates]

To the shipper, the size of the network matters only if a suitable vehicle is available for this load, on this route, at the required time. Registration counts alone say little about that experience.

## Enterprise customers brought a second business

In 2021, Anayet separated the open marketplace from an enterprise service he called brokerage. Large customers could need a dedicated account manager, agreed routes and prices, monthly bills and invoices in their own format.[^evolution]

An enterprise is buying relief from coordination work as well as transport. One person may have to organise vehicles across several sites, track deliveries and get bills approved. A dependable contact and a correct invoice can matter as much as the booking screen.

That service has different economics. It requires staff time. It may also require cash before payment arrives. Anayet told IDLC that some larger customers received time to pay while owners were paid promptly.[^idlc]

<CaseDetail title="No owned trucks does not mean no cash tied up">

Imagine a transport platform pays owners BDT 10,000 for each of 20 completed jobs while it waits for a business customer's monthly payment.

**20 × BDT 10,000 = BDT 200,000 paid out before collection.**

That is an illustrative cash gap, not Truck Lagbe's actual fares or payment terms. The fee it expects to earn does not supply the cash needed today. Late or disputed invoices can extend the gap.

</CaseDetail>

This is working capital: money needed to keep everyday operations moving while payments are still on the way. A business can avoid buying trucks and still need substantial working capital.

It also explains why a marketplace and an enterprise service should not be judged with identical measures. One may need fast, reliable matches. The other also needs accurate billing, collection and a cost-effective account team. See [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit) for the underlying money flow.

[ShopUp's early sellers waited for couriers to pass on money from completed sales](/en/case-studies/shopup-silq#the-next-product-was-hiding-inside-a-delayed-payment). Faster settlement can shorten that wait, but it cannot remove the customer credit period that Truck Lagbe chose to offer.

## The role of brokers became more complicated

A simple marketplace pitch often promises to remove middlemen. By 2021, Anayet was explaining why Truck Lagbe wanted to work with brokers too: they could reach truck owners who were not yet using digital tools.[^evolution]

A broker's fee can look like an avoidable cost until the platform needs that person's local knowledge or access to an owner who does not use the app.

Removing the person does not automatically remove the work. A platform must either do that work better or find a fair way to include the people already doing it.

The current owner page still promotes direct trips without a middleman.[^owners] That gives owners one way to find work, while Anayet's account explains why brokers could still help Truck Lagbe reach others.

## Some digital progress still needed paper

Anayet told IDLC in October 2021 that digital challans were not widely accepted, so the company still supplied physical copies.[^idlc] A challan is the document accompanying a delivery and recording the goods being sent.

This is a familiar constraint in Bangladesh: a process can be digital at one end and paper-based at the next. The customer still needs the goods accepted. Refusing to support the paper step would make the software cleaner while making the service less useful.

The same period produced a different surprise. Anayet said owners adopted an automated collection process within a month, faster than the team expected.[^evolution] People could accept one digital change while still needing paper elsewhere.

“Not ready for technology” is too broad a label. Readiness depends on the task, the benefit and who else has to accept the change.

## Growth has to work for drivers too

IFC's 2021 review is more useful than a funding headline alone. Based on remote interviews and document review, it noted gaps in monitoring driver hours, accident statistics and vehicle condition. Its action plan called for stronger procedures, training and oversight.[^ifc-review]

The same disclosure later marked the code of conduct and operations manual completed in January 2022.[^ifc-review] That records a response to the review. It does not, by itself, establish current driver earnings or safety.

A separate Fairwork study supplies a later, independent check. Its 2023 report awarded Truck Lagbe no points under its evidence-based labour standards. It raised concerns about accident liability and the availability of Bangla contracts. It also reported that the Truck Lagbe workers interviewed said they received full payment on time.[^fairwork]

These are dated findings, not a claim about every driver or today's conditions. They show why lower fares and more bookings are not enough to establish a good outcome for the people doing the work.

Truck Lagbe has also advertised a paid life-insurance option with Guardian Life.[^insurance] The existence of an offer is different from knowing how many drivers hold a policy, understand its exclusions or receive help after an accident.

For a durable marketplace, the supplier's economics belong in the product review. Fuel, maintenance, waiting time, vehicle payments and risk determine whether another trip is worth taking. A platform that makes the buyer's price attractive while leaving the driver worse off may weaken the very supply it needs.

## What the investors added

IFC announced US$2.5 million in equity, meaning investment in exchange for ownership, in September 2021. It also offered logistics experience and prospective customer connections.[^ifc] This was part of the US$4 million round later reported in the funding history.[^later-funding]

In 2023, PSA International led a further round. PSA described the fit with its ports and supply-chain work, including the potential to reduce empty trips and connect with other logistics services.[^psa] The amount was not disclosed in TBS's report.[^later-funding]

For a port operator, a trucking network offers a way to help goods continue their journey inland. The announcement explains PSA's interest, but gives no measured reduction in empty travel or evidence of profit on particular routes.

## What founders can take from Truck Lagbe

**Follow unused capacity to its cause.** An empty truck may reflect a missing match, but the route, load and timing still have to fit.

**Earn the provider's trust as carefully as the buyer's.** A false request or unclear responsibility can drive good providers away.

**Keep improving the transaction after the first match.** Rehiring, billing, support and collection give people a reason to keep using the service.

**Separate customer types when the work differs.** An occasional booking and an enterprise transport account can share a network while needing different people, processes and cash.

Do not copy the “asset-light” label without mapping who gets paid when. Do not judge driver outcomes from trip volume. And do not discard familiar practices merely because they do not fit an all-digital story.

The return-trip insight gave Truck Lagbe a way to make better use of trucks already on the road. Keeping owners and shippers coming back required the less visible work too: useful booking details, dependable payment and help when a trip went wrong.

The founder interviews explain the decisions; IFC and Fairwork provide different forms of outside scrutiny. Product pages describe the offer, while the iFarmer account shows use by another business. None provides a complete view of current operations.
