# Agroshift

<CaseCompany slug="agroshift" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Small grocery orders leave little room to pay for home delivery. Sending a motorbike courier to each doorstep can use up the margin. Agroshift found a clever alternative: targeting the country’s 4 million garment workers by delivering straight to factory gates.[^operations] Instead of hundreds of individual motorbike trips, a single truck drops off hundreds of orders in one go. But the model asks a lot from workers: they have to order days in advance and carry their groceries home after work. The big question is whether the savings are worth that extra effort.

Can an online grocery startup make money while serving Bangladesh's working class? In May 2022, venture capital fund Anchorless Bangladesh wrestled with this exact question in an internal investment memo before backing Agroshift.[^investor-memo] The memo highlighted two big risks: keeping fresh vegetables from spoiling, and testing whether garment workers would accept the hassle of carrying groceries home to save money.

## The founders had seen both ends of the problem

After years working abroad in tech consulting, Qazi Bouland returned to Bangladesh to grow vegetables on family land in Bhaluka, Mymensingh. Once his harvest grew larger than his family needed, he hit the harsh reality of farming: getting fair prices meant fighting aggressive middlemen and volatile wholesale markets. Looking for a partner who understood local distribution, he connected with Diptha Saha on LinkedIn, and Rameez Hoque joined soon after.[^bouland]

Diptha brought deep experience from Bangladesh's industrial heartland. After saving money from an engineering job in garment factory automation, he had founded Khamar-e, a startup helping small dairy farmers. Agroshift brought their two worlds together: Bouland's farming experience and Diptha's knowledge of factory workers.[^saha]

By November 2022, Khamar-e had shut down after running out of funding, Diptha told The Business Standard.[^operations]

Together, the founders understood both sides of the food chain: village farmers who could not get fair crop prices, and garment workers struggling to afford groceries on tight factory wages.

## Finding enough buyers was harder than finding food

Bouland first tried selling fresh vegetables in bulk to corporate cafeterias, but irregular orders and sudden price swings hurt profits.[^bouland] Buying fresh vegetables was easy; finding large, steady groups of everyday buyers who paid on time was the real challenge.

Garment factories offered a unique solution: huge crowds of shoppers in one place. Over 4 million garment workers in Bangladesh walk into factories every single morning. Instead of spending venture capital sending individual motorbikes to scattered homes, Agroshift could send one truck with hundreds of grocery bags straight to the factory gate.

While delivering to a factory saved on transport costs, it brought a new challenge: convincing workers to buy. Having 5,000 workers inside a factory means nothing if only five of them buy groceries on delivery day.

## The first prize supplied more than money

In February 2022, the H&M Foundation awarded Agroshift a top prize in the STITCH for RMG contest. Along with a $30,000 grant, the program provided something even more valuable: permission to run a real test inside Echotex, one of Bangladesh's top garment export factories.[^factory-pilot]

The Echotex test gave Agroshift a real testing ground to figure out logistics: where to set up order desks, how to hand out grocery bags without blocking shift exits, and how to check orders quickly without creating security lines at the factory gate.

Later, Agroshift partnered with Comfit Composite Knit, taking over an existing factory grocery shop and upgrading it into a digital ordering hub.[^comfit]

In June 2023, Diptha noted that being named to the Forbes 30 Under 30 Asia list helped him get meetings with factory owners.[^distribution-interview] Good press opened factory doors, but keeping workers happy required fresh food and low prices every week.

## How the order reaches the worker

In a June 2023 interview, Diptha explained how Agroshift filled orders in 12 to 18 hours. The company bought rice and lentils from mills, fresh vegetables from contract farmers, and packaged soap and oil from big manufacturers.[^distribution-interview]

Workers placed orders on their smartphones or walked up to an Agroshift booth inside the factory where staff placed orders for them on a tablet.[^factory-pilot] These physical booths were essential for workers who did not own smartphones or were hesitant about ordering online.

A single grocery order brought together several different suppliers. In November 2022, Agroshift explained that it bought fresh vegetables straight from farmers, rice from mills, and branded goods from consumer companies, using hired trucks to move the cargo.[^operations]

The hardest daily job was sorting: packing hundreds of custom grocery bags with the right mix of rice, potatoes, and cooking oil before the morning shift ended.

## Why orders per stop matter

A shared delivery truck is cheaper than a corner grocery store only when lots of people order at the same stop. Hiring a truck, paying the driver, buying diesel, and paying highway tolls costs about the same whether the truck carries 10 bags or 200 bags.

This shows why signing an agreement with a factory is only the first step. A large workforce is little help if too few people order to cover delivery costs. A smaller factory with regular, concentrated orders may be a better stop.

Repeat orders also let the company group more deliveries together. When workers order matters as well as how often they return.

## The decision: pre-orders or an always-stocked shop?

Anchorless Bangladesh's May 2022 investment memo pointed out a key dilemma: pre-ordered groceries took over 24 hours to arrive. Garment workers had to plan their cooking days ahead. Even more challenging, after standing on their feet for a long 10-hour shift, carrying a heavy 10 kg sack of rice and vegetables onto crowded public buses was exhausting compared to buying from the little corner shop right outside their home.[^investor-memo]

The obvious alternative was opening a fully stocked grocery store inside the factory. But the memo noted that keeping shelves full of inventory required expensive retail rent, lots of cash tied up in food, and complex management approvals.[^investor-memo]

| Choice | What the customer gains | What the business must carry |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-order and collect | A planned purchase at a shared point. | The need to deliver the promised items on time. |
| Keep groceries in stock | The option to buy immediately. | Cash tied up in goods, storage and the risk of unsold food. |

Pre-orders helped Agroshift plan purchases, but put the planning burden on the worker. Keeping inventory on-site made shopping easier for workers, but exposed the company to unsold or spoiled food.

For a factory worker, saving 50 to 100 taka on groceries had to be worth the physical sweat of hauling heavy bags home.

Different startups solved this in different ways: [Arogga chose to hold central medicine inventory](/en/case-studies/arogga#the-first-model-passed-an-order-into-a-stock-problem) after corner pharmacies ran out of stock, while Agroshift used pre-orders to avoid perishable grocery waste.

## Price and trust had to be visible in the purchase

In a Prothom Alo interview, Diptha emphasized that garment workers watch every taka. They demand clear savings and need friendly help when using digital apps.[^saha]

Trust is tested at the factory gate every time a worker opens their bag: are the potatoes firm, is the rice weight exact, and did they save real money compared to the local bazaar?

To encourage workers to try the service, Agroshift let workers on the same sewing line combine orders to unlock group bulk discounts.[^operations]

While group discounts encourage people to try the app, relying on constant discounts can hide whether workers truly want the service. Discount volume must not be confused with long-term customer loyalty.

While Comfit reported strong initial interest among workers,[^comfit] public data tracking how many workers kept ordering months later remains limited.

## A shorter chain still needs quality control

Cutting out traditional middlemen means doing their work yourself: sorting vegetables, grading sizes, inspecting freshness, and throwing away bad food. Bypassing middlemen only saves money if the startup manages spoilage better than traditional wholesalers.

When asked in 2022 about food safety, Diptha clarified that the early goal was delivering affordable, visibly fresh produce rather than paying for expensive organic farming certificates.[^operations]

Focusing on visual freshness made practical sense for budget-conscious families, but it showed the difference between marketing claims and real farming checks: fresh-looking vegetables do not prove they are free of chemical pesticides.

Anchorless Bangladesh's memo raised a similar concern, noting that early pilot tests had not yet systematically measured food waste, customer returns, or user drop-off rates.[^investor-memo]

Later updates from ADB Ventures claimed vegetable waste was below 1%.[^adb] If truly achieved from farm to factory gate, sub-1% waste is impressive, but it is unclear whether that counts spoilage in the field, in the warehouse, or at the factory drop.

## What the growth figures do - and do not - show

In February 2024, ADB Ventures reported that Agroshift delivered over 250,000 orders across 170,000 unique users during its first year.[^adb]

Dividing those totals gives roughly 1.5 orders per registered user. To understand repeat buying, though, Agroshift would need to track people who joined around the same time and see how many ordered again. A total that mixes new sign-ups with long-time users cannot answer that question.

A similar pattern appears in Agroshift's deal with Unifill Group, which brought service to six factories with 11,000 workers.[^unifill] Having access to 11,000 factory employees is not the same as having 11,000 active monthly shoppers.

The $1.8 million pre-seed round in October 2022 and later funding from ADB prove investor excitement.[^preseed][^adb] But raising venture capital does not prove that a grocery startup is profitable.

Checking whether a grocery business works requires tracking weekly repeat orders, average order values (<Term name="aov">AOV</Term>), profit margins after food waste, and real delivery costs per bag. ## The next business may use the same relationships differently

Agroshift's recent company updates show it moving into B2B wholesale supply, private-label packaging, and exporting produce abroad.[^company] These commercial avenues seek higher profit margins than delivering low-cost groceries to factory workers.

In June 2026, Agroshift announced a partnership with City Bank and dairy cooperative Milk Vita to help dairy farmers get micro-loans. Under the deal, Milk Vita provides the farmer network, Agroshift tracks digital farmer data, and City Bank provides the loan money.[^dairy]

This partnership uses farm data to lower lending risk. But managing farm credit and bad loan defaults is a completely different business than delivering sacks of potatoes to factory gates.

## What founders can take from this

- **Look for natural gathering spots.** Delivering to places where thousands of people already gather (like factories or office parks) turns expensive individual home deliveries into cheap bulk drop-offs.
- **Think about the customer's physical effort.** If ordering from you requires waiting 24 hours and carrying heavy bags home, your price savings must beat the convenience of the corner shop right outside their house.
- **Track drop density closely.** More orders at one stop spread the delivery cost. Check what remains after buying, packing, spoilage, and transport before calling that stop profitable.
- **Do not confuse potential factory headcount with active buyers.** Having access to 10,000 factory workers on paper is just an introduction; real success is measured by unprompted weekly reorders.
- **Cutting out middlemen means doing their work.** If you bypass traditional food wholesalers, you must sort, grade, and transport perishable produce without losing money to food waste.

## What remains unknown

Audited company financial statements, long-term customer repeat rates across factory sites, profit margins for each grocery category, and verified company-wide food waste rates are not public.

Agroshift's factory-gate delivery model solved the brutal delivery cost that doomed earlier grocery apps in Bangladesh. Whether the model can win over everyday food spending long-term depends on beating the simple, physical convenience of the local corner grocery store.
