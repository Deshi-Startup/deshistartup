# Agroshift

<CaseCompany slug="agroshift" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Agroshift found that buying a farmer's produce could be easier than finding enough people to sell it to.[^operations] Garment factories offered a place to gather those buyers. Grouping orders and delivering at work could lower costs. The catch: workers had to plan ahead and carry the groceries home. The business depends on making that trade worthwhile.

Would workers accept that arrangement? An investor recorded its questions in May 2022, before Agroshift's later funding and growth announcements, and eventually published the memo.[^investor-memo] It gives us a way to compare the original expectations with what happened afterward.

## At a glance

| Item | Detail |

| Early focus | Groceries for garment workers, ordered and collected at factories.[^factory-pilot] |
| Suppliers | A mix of farmers, traders and processors.[^adb] |
| Business model | Buy and sell essentials, with the price covering goods and the work of supplying them.[^operations] |
| Current scope | Workplace and reseller distribution, services for food companies, and cross-border trade.[^company] |

The early factory model is the focus here. The company's later activities are broader, and their economics should not be assumed to match it.

## The founders had seen both ends of the problem

After years abroad in technology and consulting, Qazi Bouland returned to Bangladesh and began growing vegetables on family land in Bhaluka. Once he was producing more than his family needed, he had to find a way to reach buyers. He met Diptha Saha through LinkedIn, and Rameez Hoque later joined the founding team.[^bouland]

Diptha's experience came from a different direction. In a 2023 interview, he described saving money from an engineering job linked to garment factories, then starting Khamar-e. That venture worked on dairy technology and market access. Agroshift combined his earlier experience in factories and agriculture.[^saha]

By November 2022, Khamar-e had closed for lack of investment, Diptha told The Business Standard.[^operations]

Between them, the founders knew farming and factory work. That experience helped connect a problem at one end, selling food, with a potential group of buyers at the other.

## Finding enough buyers was harder than finding food

Qazi Bouland's account describes an early plan to serve businesses, followed by difficulty with sales channels and changing prices.[^bouland] One large purchase could leave Agroshift needing many smaller sales.

A garment factory offered a different arrangement. Orders could be gathered at one place, then fulfilled together. Rather than send a separate delivery to each home, the company could supply a shared collection point.

The factory could make deliveries cheaper by bringing buyers together. But Agroshift still needed workers to order regularly. A large workforce would be little help if only a few people bought groceries on each delivery day.

## The first prize supplied more than money

In February 2022, H&M Foundation named Agroshift among six STITCH for RMG winners. Each received a US$30,000 grant and access to a partner factory for a pilot. Agroshift was paired with Echotex.[^factory-pilot]

The factory partnership let Agroshift test the details that would decide whether the service worked: where workers could order, when they could collect groceries and how the operation fitted around their jobs.

A later company update describes converting Comfit Composite's existing “fair shop” into Agroshift's ordering and supply service.[^comfit] Agroshift could use a place workers already had, with its own ordering and supply process behind it.

In June 2023, Diptha also said his Forbes recognition had helped with factory introductions and partnership discussions.[^distribution-interview] That explains how publicity could help the business: it opened doors to decision-makers. It did not automatically sell groceries to workers.

An introduction could win management's permission to operate. Agroshift then had to win workers' grocery spending, one purchase at a time.

## How the order reaches the worker

Diptha's June 2023 description combined online ordering with an offline transaction, usually delivered in 12-18 hours. The supplier network included farmers, traders and processors.[^distribution-interview]

Workers could order digitally or through factory kiosks.[^factory-pilot] Having somewhere to get help could make the service easier to try than an unfamiliar app alone.

A basket also contains different supply chains. The November 2022 account distinguishes fresh produce from farmers, grains from mills, and packaged goods from companies. Agroshift used outside transport providers.[^operations]

Agroshift had to bring all those suppliers' goods together into the basket a worker had ordered. Buying produce from farmers was one part of that job.

This helps explain why a factory agreement is only a starting point. A location with many employees but few orders can still be expensive to serve. A smaller site with regular orders may work better.

Repeat purchases help fill the next delivery as well as the first. For this model, when people order matters alongside how often they return.

## The decision: pre-orders or an always-stocked shop?

Anchorless Bangladesh's memo was written in May 2022 and published, with some figures hidden, in 2024. It recorded an uncomfortable trade: some groceries took more than a day to arrive. Workers would need to plan ahead. Carrying heavy items after work could also make a nearby shop more attractive.[^investor-memo]

The alternative was to hold stock for immediate purchases inside the factory. The memo noted that this would need more money, space and permission.[^investor-memo]

| Choice | What the customer gains | What the business must carry |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-order and collect | A planned purchase at a shared point. | The need to deliver the promised items on time. |
| Keep groceries in stock | The option to buy immediately. | Cash tied up in goods, storage and the risk of unsold food. |

Pre-orders gave Agroshift a clearer idea of what to buy, but asked workers to plan ahead. Keeping stock would make shopping more flexible while costing the company more. Neither choice suited every purchase.

From a worker's point of view, the price saving had to be worth the wait, the collection and the walk home with the bags.

[Arogga began buying and holding medicine stock after pharmacies could not fill its orders](/en/case-studies/arogga#the-first-model-passed-an-order-into-a-stock-problem). The trade is between paying for goods before they sell and asking customers to wait while orders are gathered; which works depends on the delivery promise.

## Price and trust had to be visible in the purchase

Diptha's Prothom Alo interview describes a price-sensitive audience that values an immediate, clear benefit and may need help when starting to use a digital service.[^saha]

Workers could judge the service with each bag: was the quantity right, was the quality good and had they saved money? Those were reasons to order again.

Agroshift also used group discounts linked to orders from a factory production line, according to its November 2022 account.[^operations] This linked the buying incentive to the shared delivery model.

That can encourage people to order together. It also means promotional volume needs careful reading. A busy discount day does not show what people will buy at the usual price.

The Comfit update reported substantial worker use soon after launch, but it was a company claim rather than an independent customer study.[^comfit] We did not find enough public worker interviews or repeat-purchase data to judge how consistently the service met its promise across sites.

## A shorter chain still needs quality control

Agroshift takes on work that other traders would otherwise perform: finding supply, checking it, sorting orders and arranging delivery. Removing stages is valuable only if the remaining process does that work well.

Asked in 2022 about the website's food-safety promise, Diptha said the early focus was lower prices and visibly acceptable goods, rather than intervention in production.[^operations]

That describes the early priorities, not today's food safety. It also leaves a gap between the promise and the checks described: **looking fresh is not the same as verified safety.**

The investor memo raised a related measurement gap. At the time it was written, data on waste, returns and returning customers had not yet been collected.[^investor-memo]

ADB Ventures later reported waste below 1%.[^adb] That is progress as described by an investor, but the announcement does not provide the full measurement method or define which stages of the chain it covers. It should not become a claim of zero loss from farm to household.

## What the growth figures do - and do not - show

ADB Ventures' February 2024 announcement reports more than 250,000 fulfilled orders for more than 170,000 unique users in Agroshift's first operating year.[^adb]

Those are useful scale indicators, with a clear period. They do not tell us how often a typical worker bought groceries or what share returned the next month. Dividing total orders by users cannot reveal the distribution of repeat purchases.

The distinction between reach and use appears in a later Unifill Group announcement too. Agroshift said it had expanded from one factory to six, giving it access to a workforce of 11,000.[^unifill] That is potential reach at partner sites, not 11,000 active customers.

The October 2022 pre-seed round and later ADB backing show that investors funded the model.[^preseed][^adb] They do not establish current audited net profit or the cost of adding the next factory.

For that decision, a founder needs the site's repeat orders, spending per basket, product margin, handling and delivery costs, and losses. The time between paying suppliers and collecting from buyers matters too. See [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics) and [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit).

## The next business may use the same relationships differently

Agroshift's current website describes workplace distribution and reseller networks, food processing, and cross-border trade. It also offers private-label services: helping another company sell goods under its own brand.[^company] Those offers extend beyond the early factory-grocery model.

In June 2026, the company announced a dairy-financing agreement with City Bank and Milk Vita. Milk Vita would provide access to its cooperative network. Agroshift would support farmer identification, digital onboarding and monitoring. City Bank would provide financing.[^dairy]

The roles are distinct. Agroshift is the technology and operating partner, not the bank. The announcement describes intended work; it does not establish completed loans through that agreement.

There is a plausible connection to the earlier business: repeated agricultural trade can create useful relationships and records. Whether those records make lending safer, or whether the new services improve the company's returns, requires later evidence.

The relationships may carry over, but financing introduces different responsibilities from supplying groceries. The new business needs to be judged on its own results.

## What founders can take from this

- **Find the gathering point.** Where customers already spend time can change the cost of serving them.
- **Use earlier experience to choose a narrow starting market.** The founders' farming and factory knowledge helped shape the first offer.
- **Count orders at a location, not just people with access.** A partnership and a repeat customer are different achievements.
- **Include the customer's extra effort in the price comparison.** Waiting and carrying groceries are part of the deal.
- **Name the work behind a shorter supply chain.** Sourcing, handling, quality checks and loss control still need an owner.

Do not copy a “no middlemen” slogan as a business plan. Do not treat a grant or award as proof of demand. And do not turn a reported margin into a whole-company profit claim without knowing what costs it includes.

## What remains unknown

Public evidence is still limited on repeat purchases by factory, consistent basket-price savings, current waste measurements and independently verified net profit. The early memo intentionally hides several figures; this case does not try to reconstruct them.

The factory gate gives Agroshift a way to serve many households through one delivery. Whether workers keep using it comes down to the whole purchase: the price, the food they receive and the effort it takes to get it home.
