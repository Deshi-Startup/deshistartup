# Arogga

<CaseCompany slug="arogga" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Arogga's first model broke down because the pharmacies it relied on could not supply the stock it needed. The team restarted with more control over buying, storage and fulfilment.[^origin] That made the customer promise more credible - and the business harder to run. The story is about choosing which work to own, earning repeat orders, and expanding without losing control of service quality.

The order is easy to describe: get the correct medicine to the correct person. Making that happen reliably is the difficult part.

Arogga is an online pharmacy and healthcare platform in Bangladesh. It began with medicine delivery, finding regular customers among people managing long-term conditions. It later added home lab testing and delivery of products that need careful temperature control.[^early-market][^labs][^novo]

Keeping those promises takes buying, checking, storage, delivery and help when an order goes wrong. Those are the business choices examined here; this study does not assess treatments or recommend medicines.

## The founding team brought different kinds of experience

Before Arogga, Rosina Mazumder had worked in investment banking in London and founded Cake Masters, a publishing business. During the pandemic, she began looking at healthcare businesses in emerging markets. Fahad Hossain and Shamim Hasan were already studying the supply and delivery problems facing pharmacies in Bangladesh.[^origin]

Yawar Mehboob also came from investment banking and became Arogga's finance lead. In a September 2021 Asia Tech Podcast interview, he and Rosina described combining that business experience with their co-founders' knowledge of the local market.[^podcast]

By the 2023 funding announcement, the roles were clear: Rosina led the company, Fahad ran operations, Shamim led technology and Yawar led finance.[^seed] The mix suited a business that had to do more than build an app. It needed supplier relationships, a physical operation and enough cash to carry stock.

Lockdowns gave people a reason to want medicine delivered. Arogga still had to make sure it could find the medicines they ordered.

## The first model passed an order into a stock problem

Even large Dhaka pharmacies could not reliably supply the medicines Arogga needed, according to Future Startup's June 2021 account. After Rosina joined in November 2020, the team changed how it bought stock.[^origin]

UNB's contemporaneous report describes the response: buying in bulk from manufacturers, setting up climate-controlled storage and operating with a small team that included a pharmacist.[^early-market]

The change is easiest to see by following an order.

Buying directly could improve access to stock and purchasing terms. But it also meant paying for products that might not sell quickly. A broad catalogue brings many separate decisions: what to stock, how much to buy, what can expire and how often to reorder.

This is vertical integration: a business taking control of more stages in its own supply chain. Arogga took on that work because searching other shops for stock could not reliably complete an order.

## The neighbourhood pharmacy is a serious competitor

A neighbourhood pharmacy gives Arogga plenty to compete with: it is close, familiar and can hand over an item immediately. A 2023 paper from ULAB's Center for Enterprise and Society and Future Startup describes these advantages alongside the delivery costs and waiting time of online services. Even next-day delivery can be too slow when someone needs an item now.[^sector]

Home delivery needed to offer customers enough convenience to make that wait worthwhile.

Early reporting points to people with long-term conditions who buy medicines regularly.[^early-market] A planned refill is a different shopping situation from needing something immediately. The customer can value avoiding another trip, finding several items together and placing the next order with less effort.

For other purchases, the familiar pharmacy nearby may still be the better choice. Arogga did not need to replace every pharmacy visit to give regular customers a reason to order.

The distinction also changes the product. A one-off order benefits from easy search. A repeat customer may care more about finding the previous order, checking availability and knowing when the parcel will arrive. A large total market says little about which of those jobs should come first.

## Trust began with the service, not the word “healthtech”

In a May 2022 IDLC interview, Rosina described the offer in everyday terms: genuine medicine, an affordable price and home delivery. She said customers who tried the service helped spread it through word of mouth. The company also reported reaching its 100,000th order.[^idlc]

The offer was easy to understand and to judge after delivery. If the right medicines arrived at a fair price, the customer had a reason to order again and recommend the service.

Arogga's ordering options still reflect that practical approach. Its website offers prescription upload as well as ordering through a call, Messenger or WhatsApp.[^site] A customer does not have to type every unfamiliar product name into a form.

Less work for the customer can mean more work behind the screen. Someone must read the request, confirm what can be supplied and resolve uncertainty. Removing friction from the interface should not remove a necessary check from the service.

Rosina's account credits referrals with helping Arogga grow. We do not know how many buyers came that way, how many came for a discount, or which group kept ordering.

## A closer look at one decision: keep stock, or coordinate suppliers?

Owning inventory is expensive. Leaving all inventory with others is risky when their stock information is weak. Neither model is automatically better.

Arogga's early failure makes the choice concrete. Its customer was already placing an order, but the supply arrangement could not reliably complete it. Better advertising would have sent more orders into the same problem.

<CaseDetail title="What the restart fixed - and what it created">

**It could fix:** dependence on whatever a retail pharmacy happened to have when an order arrived.

**It created:** responsibility for stock levels, expiry, storage, picking and the cash tied up in unsold products.

**The useful test:** the share of orders filled correctly and on time, weighed against stock losses and the money needed to run the warehouse.

</CaseDetail>

Those measures belong together. A warehouse can improve availability by carrying too much stock, while weakening the business through slow sales and expiry. A lean warehouse can look efficient while repeatedly disappointing customers.

The goal is not maximum stock. It is enough of the right stock to keep a specific customer promise. That lesson travels well to food, spare parts and other businesses where a missing item can break the whole order.

[Agroshift's early factory model asked workers to order groceries ahead](/en/case-studies/agroshift#the-decision-pre-orders-or-an-always-stocked-shop). That made purchases easier to plan but asked customers to wait, while Arogga's restart put cash into stock to make medicine orders more dependable.

## The return policy shows another side of trust

Trust is also tested when a parcel goes wrong.

Arogga's policy, checked on 13 September 2026, allows eligible returns or replacements for damaged, incorrect or missing items, with conditions. It excludes heat-sensitive and refrigerated products from returns. It also asks customers to provide an unboxing video when making a return, replacement or refund claim. Refunds go to an Arogga wallet, with withdrawal options; cash-on-delivery refunds can be withdrawn through bKash.[^returns]

These are concrete service choices. An unboxing video can help establish what arrived, but it asks the customer to know the rule before opening the parcel. A wallet can help process a refund, but it adds a step if the customer needs the money elsewhere.

For a business in a trust-sensitive category, the recovery process deserves as much design attention as the checkout. A customer who already received the wrong item should not have to discover the evidence rules afterward. Clear instructions before delivery, a reachable support person and a fair way to handle exceptions all matter.

We have not tested a return. The policy shows the rules customers face; it does not establish how well individual claims are resolved.

## Funding bought room to build a larger operation

Arogga's early capital came in stages. UNB reported US$200,000 in 2021.[^early-market] In 2022, a founder-shared announcement described a US$1.3 million round with Hyper and other investors.[^hyper]

The October 2023 announcement put the seed funding, an early stage of investment, at US$5.5 million: US$4 million in new money plus US$1.5 million raised earlier. It described plans to improve technology, logistics and customer care, deepen Dhaka coverage and add hubs in other cities.[^seed]

The US$5.5 million total includes the earlier investment. The plans also show what opening in another city would take: Arogga needed stock, delivery capacity and customer support there before it could make the same offer to new customers.

The sources do not provide audited profit or enough detail to calculate the cost of a typical order. A sale has to help pay for the product, warehouse, checking, delivery, support, discounts and losses. Order growth can spread some fixed costs over more sales. It can also increase the cash needed to buy and hold stock.

See [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit) for why a growing inventory business can need cash even while recording sales.

## Expansion brings partners back into the model

With home lab testing, Arogga returned to coordinating outside providers. Customers can choose among partner labs, compare prices and report times, and book home sample collection where available.[^labs]

The company does not need to own every laboratory to make that service useful. It does need the booking, collection, handover and report delivery to work as one experience.

| Service | The customer is relying on | The operating question |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine order | The correct item arriving as agreed | Can stock, checking and delivery keep the promise? |
| Home lab booking | A suitable appointment and a usable report | Who controls collection, transport and the laboratory handoff? |
| Temperature-sensitive delivery | Correct handling throughout the journey | How is the required temperature maintained and recorded? |

This is our comparison of the service tasks, based on the published offers.[^site][^labs][^novo]

The December 2025 Novo Nordisk agreement is a specific example of the third task. It covers online ordering and delivery within Dhaka, cold-chain handling and QR-linked instructions.[^novo] Cold chain means maintaining the required temperature through storage and transport. A fast delivery alone does not prove that this happened.

In March 2026, an announcement of Verge HealthTech Fund's investment described a broader primary healthcare platform, including diagnostics and future consultations.[^verge] On our September check, the doctor-consultation page still said “Coming Soon.”[^consultations] A service appearing in a menu is not enough to count it as launched.

A customer who trusts Arogga with medicine delivery may consider booking a lab test there too. But that trust now depends on different people and a different process, from collecting the sample to delivering the report.

## What founders can take from Arogga

**Find the customer situation in which you are clearly useful.** A planned repeat order and an immediate purchase are different needs, even when they involve the same product.

**Fix the weak part of fulfilment before driving more demand.** If an order fails because stock cannot be found, a smoother checkout will not solve it.

**Make the promise simple and the process dependable.** Prescription uploads and phone ordering lower the customer's effort. They still need careful checking behind them.

**Design for mistakes as well as successful orders.** Evidence rules, replacements and refunds are part of the product. They should be understandable before a customer needs them.

Do not copy a wide catalogue or a discount without counting what it costs to fulfil. Do not assume direct sourcing proves every later handoff is safe. And do not treat a new service announcement as evidence that the service already works at scale.

Arogga took control of medicine stock when relying on pharmacies failed, then chose partners for lab testing. The question in each case is where it needs control to keep the promise it makes to a customer.

## Evidence and open questions

The sources establish the restart, funding announcements, stated operating model and current service offers. Early growth and user counts are company-reported. Interviews help explain intent; the ULAB paper supplies a wider view of the market.

We do not have independent product or cold-chain audits, order error rates, expiry losses, customer retention by cohort, or audited profit. We also lack a detailed account of the first paid customers and their acquisition cost. This case leaves those gaps open rather than filling them with catalogue size, download counts or promotional claims.
