# Arogga

<CaseCompany slug="arogga" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Arogga started as a simple app that sent delivery riders to buy medicine from local pharmacies whenever a customer placed an order. But local pharmacies constantly ran out of stock and could rarely fill an entire doctor's prescription. To survive, the founders made a bold move: they stopped relying on retail shops, built their own temperature-controlled warehouse, and started buying medicine directly from manufacturers in bulk.[^origin] Holding inventory tied up a lot of cash, especially since government rules strictly cap medicine profit margins at 12% to 14%. But having guaranteed stock let Arogga win loyal customers who need monthly refills for chronic illnesses like diabetes and high blood pressure. Once those families trusted Arogga for monthly medicine, the company added higher-margin home blood tests and lab work.

Delivering medicine sounds deceptively simple: take an order, pick up the box, and drop it off at a home. In Bangladesh, making that reliable is tricky. The market is full of fake drugs, messy shop inventories, and strict government price limits.

Arogga launched as an online pharmacy to solve these exact problems. It built its business around chronic patients who need regular monthly refills.[^early-market][^labs][^novo] To keep customers happy, it grew from delivering pills into keeping insulin cold and sending nurses to collect blood samples at home.

Keeping these promises requires buying stock, checking prescriptions, safe storage, and handling returns when an order goes wrong. Those business trade-offs are what we study here; this case study does not give medical advice.

## The founding team brought different kinds of experience

Before starting Arogga, Rosina Mazumder worked in investment banking in London and built Cake Masters, a global publishing brand. During the pandemic, she looked at healthcare problems in developing countries. Meanwhile in Dhaka, Fahad Hossain and Shamim Hasan were already studying why Bangladeshi pharmacies ran out of medicine so often.[^origin]

Yawar Mehboob also brought a background in global investment banking, stepping in to run company finances. In a September 2021 podcast interview, he and Rosina talked about combining international finance discipline with their co-founders' street-smart knowledge of local distribution in Bangladesh.[^podcast]

By their 2023 funding round, leadership roles were clearly divided: Rosina served as CEO handling strategy and investor relations, Fahad ran delivery operations and warehouse hubs, Shamim led software and tech, and Yawar managed company money.[^seed] That mix of skills proved essential. An online pharmacy cannot survive on code alone; it needs strong relationships with drug companies, clean warehouses, and tight cash control.

While COVID-19 lockdowns encouraged people to order medicine online, Arogga still had to master the hard daily work of delivering real packages on time.

## The first model passed an order into a stock problem

In early 2020, Arogga tried an "asset-light" approach: when a customer placed an order, a delivery rider ran to a neighborhood pharmacy to buy the pills. But local pharmacies made poor partners for an online store. According to Future Startup, local shops were constantly out of stock, kept messy records on paper, and rarely had all the medicines listed on a single prescription. When Rosina joined full-time in late 2020, the founders saw that this marketplace model would fail and decided to overhaul how they bought medicine.[^origin]

A report by UNB documented the major turnaround: Arogga stopped buying from corner shops. Instead, they bought medicine in bulk straight from licensed manufacturers, opened a central temperature-controlled warehouse, and hired certified pharmacists to inspect packages and verify prescriptions before shipping.[^early-market]

Buying directly from manufacturers ensured authentic medicine and better wholesale prices. But it also meant Arogga took on inventory risk: spending its own cash to buy thousands of medicine boxes before any customer clicked "buy." Running a big medicine catalog takes careful cash management: keeping popular medicines in stock while making sure slow-selling boxes do not sit on shelves until they expire.

Arogga took on warehouse costs because outside shops could not deliver the reliable service customers expected.

## The neighbourhood pharmacy is a serious competitor

Across Bangladesh, over 150,000 neighborhood pharmacies offer serious competition. Customers may know their local chemist, buy on credit, and collect medicine without waiting for delivery. A 2023 study by ULAB and Future Startup highlighted these local advantages compared to the waiting times and delivery fees of online apps.[^sector]

An online pharmacy also has to make its delivery costs fit within its <Term name="gross-margin">gross margin</Term>: what remains from a sale after paying for the medicine. Discounts leave less money to cover the rest of the work.

For an illustrative 300-taka order, assume a 3% margin after discounts and the cost of the medicine. That leaves 9 taka for packaging, pharmacist checks, and delivery. If those costs exceed 9 taka, the order needs another source of income, such as a delivery charge, to cover them. These are example figures, not Arogga's reported margins.

Arogga's early focus included **chronic-care patients**, who need medicine regularly for conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure.[^early-market] Larger medicine baskets can raise <Term name="aov">average order value</Term> and spread delivery costs across more items, improving <Term name="unit-economics">unit economics</Term>. A recurring need also gives the company a reason to earn the next order. It does not guarantee that customers return: <Term name="retention">retention</Term> still depends on price, availability, and service.

## Trust began with the service, not the word “healthtech”

In a May 2022 interview, Rosina explained Arogga's promise simply: 100% genuine medicine, clear prices, and reliable delivery right to your door. In a country where people worry about counterfeit or poorly stored drugs, delivering real medicine on time created strong word-of-mouth recommendations. By mid-2022, the company celebrated its 100,000th order.[^idlc]

Arogga’s app made ordering easy. Instead of typing out long, complicated chemical names, customers could simply snap a photo of their doctor's handwritten prescription, or order via phone call, WhatsApp, or Facebook Messenger.[^site]

Making things easy for the customer created extra work behind the scenes. Arogga’s licensed pharmacists had to decipher messy doctor handwriting, match generic drug names, verify dosages, and call customers to double-check details. Making an app easy to use must never come at the expense of safety.

While word of mouth brought in early users, venture capital also funded discount offers to encourage people to try the app.

## A closer look at one decision: keep stock, or coordinate suppliers?

Owning inventory ties up a lot of cash. Relying on outside retail shops leads to frequent out-of-stock orders. Neither way is perfect; each comes with trade-offs.

Arogga's early marketplace failure showed that spending money on marketing cannot fix a broken supply chain. If an app cannot deliver what a customer ordered, running ads just wastes money and upsets people.

<CaseDetail title="The pivot: what it fixed, and what it created">

**What it fixed:** Arogga no longer depended on whether a corner chemist had a pill in stock at that exact moment.

**What it created:** The team took on the burden of ordering stock, checking expiry dates, paying warehouse rent, and tying up cash in unsold bottles sitting on shelves.

**How to judge it:** Compare the higher percentage of successful deliveries against the money lost on expired stock and warehouse operations.

</CaseDetail>

These trade-offs must be balanced carefully. Keeping too much stock in the warehouse might guarantee that 99% of orders are delivered, but throws away money if unused bottles expire. On the other hand, keeping too little stock means chronic patients get frustrated when essential medicines run out.

The goal is not to build the biggest warehouse. It is to stock high-demand, chronic-care medicines in sensible quantities that keep patients happy while protecting your cash.

This trade-off appears across industries: while [Agroshift used pre-orders to avoid stocking perishable groceries](/en/case-studies/agroshift#the-decision-pre-orders-or-an-always-stocked-shop), Arogga chose central inventory to improve medicine availability.

## Customer trust is tested when orders go wrong

Customer trust is tested most when an order arrives damaged, incomplete, or wrong.

Arogga's return policy allows returns or replacements for damaged or incorrect deliveries, but with strict safety rules. It does not accept returns on temperature-sensitive items like insulin, because returned medicine cannot safely be resold to someone else. It also asks customers to record a video when opening their delivery box to prove missing or damaged bottles. Approved refunds go into an in-app wallet, which customers can transfer to bKash if they paid with <Term name="cod">cash on delivery</Term>.[^returns]

These rules highlight the reality of online retail in Bangladesh. Requiring an unboxing video prevents fake claims and courier theft, but it adds extra hassle for honest patients. Similarly, refunding money to an in-app wallet makes accounting easier, but frustrates customers who just want their cash back immediately.

For a healthcare brand, fixing mistakes quickly is critical. Replacing a wrong package with friendly pharmacist support keeps regular, high-value patients loyal.

## Funding bought room to build a larger operation

Arogga raised money step by step. In 2021, UNB reported an early $200,000 pre-seed check.[^early-market] By 2022, the company confirmed a $1.3 million round led by Silicon Valley accelerator Hyper and angel investor groups.[^hyper]

In October 2023, Arogga announced a $5.5 million seed round, consisting of $4.0 million in new funding alongside $1.5 million raised earlier. The company planned to use the money to improve software, its own delivery operation, and customer care in Dhaka, with hubs in two other cities planned for the second quarter of 2024.[^seed]

Expanding a warehouse-heavy pharmacy outside Dhaka requires serious money. Opening in Chittagong or Sylhet means renting air-conditioned hubs, hiring local pharmacists, and spending large amounts of <Term name="working-capital">working capital</Term> on stock before steady sales begin.

Growing a medicine distribution company brings a classic cash squeeze: as orders grow fast, you must pay drug companies for inventory long before customers hand over cash. For a detailed look at this, see our guide on [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit).

## Expansion brings partners back into the model

After building its own medicine supply chain, Arogga chose a partnership model for its next service: home lab testing. Through the app, patients can browse accredited medical labs, check test prices, see how fast results arrive, and book a trained phlebotomist to collect blood samples at home.[^labs]

Lab partnerships let Arogga offer another service to existing patients without buying and storing more medicine. The costs shift to collecting samples, paying partner labs, and supporting patients. It is a way to serve the same customer more fully without building another medicine warehouse.

| Service | The customer is relying on | The operating question |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine order | The correct item arriving as agreed | Can stock, checking and delivery keep the promise? |
| Home lab booking | A suitable appointment and a usable report | Who controls collection, transport and the laboratory handoff? |
| Temperature-sensitive delivery | Correct handling throughout the journey | How is the required temperature maintained and recorded? |

This table contrasts Arogga's operational responsibilities across its expanding healthcare offerings.[^site][^labs][^novo]

In December 2025, Arogga signed a deal with global pharmaceutical maker Novo Nordisk. The partnership established certified cold-chain delivery across Dhaka for temperature-sensitive insulins, using special cooling packs and QR codes to track temperatures throughout the trip.[^novo]

In March 2026, Verge HealthTech Fund invested in Arogga to support its goal of becoming an all-in-one health app combining pharmacy, lab tests, and doctor visits.[^verge] As of September 2026, the doctor consultation feature is still listed as "Coming Soon," showing that telemedicine remains an upcoming project rather than a finished business.[^consultations]

Delivering medicine earned Arogga trust with families, but offering medical lab tests and doctor visits adds new responsibilities around test accuracy and medical liability.

## What founders can take from Arogga

- **Target customer groups that buy frequently with larger orders.** Delivering single emergency fever pills on demand loses money under tight price caps; building monthly delivery subscriptions for chronic patients creates a sustainable business.
- **Fix supply and fulfillment before spending heavily on ads.** Spending marketing money when your backend cannot fill orders reliably will disappoint customers and waste cash.
- **Make ordering simple, but protect safety.** Letting users upload prescription photos makes ordering effortless, but requires licensed pharmacists to verify doses behind the scenes.
- **Pair high-margin services with low-margin deliveries.** Use frequent, low-margin medicine deliveries to win customer loyalty, then offer high-margin lab test bookings to improve overall company profits.
- **Build fair, transparent return rules.** In sensitive businesses like healthcare, how kindly and quickly you resolve a mistake decides whether a customer stays or leaves.
