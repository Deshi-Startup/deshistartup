# Arogga

<CaseCompany slug="arogga" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Arogga first relied on local pharmacies to fill orders. Stock shortages led the team to restart with its own warehouse and buy directly from medicine manufacturers.[^origin] That gave it more control, but also tied up cash in stock and added storage costs. Many early regular customers needed medicine for long-term conditions. Arogga later added home lab testing through partners. The useful lesson is to check whether reliable supply and repeat orders can cover the extra costs. The sources reviewed here do not establish Arogga’s profitability.

An online pharmacy has to deliver the right medicine, stored properly, when the customer needs it. A useful app cannot make up for missing stock or a wrong delivery.

Arogga’s early regular customers included people who needed medicine each month for conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure.[^early-market][^labs][^novo] Its later services included home blood-sample collection and temperature-sensitive medicine delivery. Each service adds work the customer may never see.

Keeping these promises requires buying stock, checking prescriptions, safe storage, and handling returns when an order goes wrong. Those business trade-offs are what we study here; this case study does not give medical advice.

## The founding team brought different kinds of experience

Before starting Arogga, Rosina Mazumder worked in investment banking in London and built Cake Masters, a global publishing brand. During the pandemic, she looked at healthcare problems in developing countries. Meanwhile in Dhaka, Fahad Hossain and Shamim Hasan were already studying why Bangladeshi pharmacies ran out of medicine so often.[^origin]

Yawar Mehboob also brought a background in global investment banking, stepping in to run company finances. In a September 2021 podcast interview, he and Rosina talked about combining international finance discipline with their co-founders' street-smart knowledge of local distribution in Bangladesh.[^podcast]

The 2023 funding announcement names Rosina as CEO, Fahad as COO, Shamim as CTO and Yawar as CFO.[^seed] The team needs more than software skills: buying and storing medicine, delivering orders and managing cash are separate jobs.

While COVID-19 lockdowns encouraged people to order medicine online, Arogga still had to master the hard daily work of delivering real packages on time.

## The first model passed an order into a stock problem

Arogga’s early model relied on local pharmacies for medicine. Future Startup reported that repeated stock shortages forced it to pause. Rosina joined full-time in November 2020, and the team changed how it bought and stored medicine.[^origin]

UNB described the restart: Arogga bought medicine in bulk from manufacturers, set up a climate-controlled warehouse and employed a certified pharmacist.[^early-market]

Buying and holding stock moves the risk into the business. Arogga has to buy and store medicine before it is sold, keep popular items available and avoid buying so much that slow-selling stock expires. More control of supply also means more work and cash at risk.

Arogga took on warehouse costs because outside shops could not deliver the reliable service customers expected.

## The neighbourhood pharmacy is a serious competitor

A 2023 study by ULAB and Future Startup described a market with more than 150,000 pharmacies. Customers may know the local pharmacist, buy on credit and collect medicine without waiting for delivery. These are practical advantages an online service has to compete with.[^sector]

An online pharmacy also has to make its delivery costs fit within its <Term name="gross-margin">gross margin</Term>: what remains from a sale after paying for the medicine. Discounts leave less money to cover the rest of the work.

For an illustrative 300-taka order, assume a 3% margin after discounts and the cost of the medicine. That leaves 9 taka for packaging, pharmacist checks, and delivery. If those costs exceed 9 taka, the order needs another source of income, such as a delivery charge, to cover them. These are example figures, not Arogga's reported margins.

Arogga's early focus included **chronic-care patients**, who need medicine regularly for conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure.[^early-market] Larger medicine baskets can raise <Term name="aov">average order value</Term>, the average amount spent per order. They also spread delivery costs across more items. That may improve <Term name="unit-economics">unit economics</Term>: the income and costs of one order. A recurring need also gives the company a reason to earn the next order. It does not guarantee that customers return: <Term name="retention">retention</Term> still depends on price, availability, and service.

## Trust began with the service, not the word “healthtech”

In a May 2022 interview, Rosina described Arogga’s promise as genuine, affordable medicine and reliable home delivery. The interview also reported the company’s 100,000th order.[^idlc] These are company statements, not an independent check of every delivery.

Arogga’s app made ordering easy. Instead of typing out long, complicated chemical names, customers could simply snap a photo of their doctor's handwritten prescription, or order via phone call, WhatsApp, or Facebook Messenger.[^site]

Making ordering easy leaves important work for the team. A prescription photo still needs to be read and checked; unclear details need to be resolved before an order is sent. Arogga’s app cannot replace the work of qualified pharmacy staff.

Word of mouth can bring a first order, but the service still has to earn the next one. Discounts also reduce the money left to pay for delivery.

## A closer look at one decision: keep stock, or coordinate suppliers?

Owning inventory ties up a lot of cash. Relying on outside retail shops leads to frequent out-of-stock orders. Neither way is perfect; each comes with trade-offs.

Arogga's early marketplace failure showed that spending money on marketing cannot fix a broken supply chain. If an app cannot deliver what a customer ordered, running ads just wastes money and upsets people.

<CaseDetail title="The pivot: what it fixed, and what it created">

**What it fixed:** Arogga no longer depended on whether a corner chemist had a pill in stock at that exact moment.

**What it created:** The team took on the burden of ordering stock, checking expiry dates, paying warehouse rent, and tying up cash in unsold bottles sitting on shelves.

**How to judge it:** Compare the higher percentage of successful deliveries against the money lost on expired stock and warehouse operations.

</CaseDetail>

Too much stock can tie up cash and expire before it sells. Too little can leave a regular customer without the medicine they ordered. Compare orders filled, stock losses and storage costs before increasing inventory.

The goal is to keep medicines that sell regularly in sensible quantities, so patients can get what they need without tying up too much cash in unsold stock.

This trade-off appears across industries: while [Agroshift used pre-orders to avoid stocking perishable groceries](/en/case-studies/agroshift#the-decision-pre-orders-or-an-always-stocked-shop), Arogga chose central inventory to improve medicine availability.

## Customer trust is tested when orders go wrong

Customer trust is tested most when an order arrives damaged, incomplete, or wrong.

Arogga’s policy, checked on 4 October 2026, sets a seven-day contact window for eligible return or replacement requests involving damaged, defective, incorrect or missing items. Returns require unused, unopened goods with intact packaging and seals. Items marked non-returnable and temperature-sensitive goods such as insulin are excluded. An unboxing video is required for return, replacement or refund claims. Approved refunds first enter the wallet. For <Term name="cod">cash-on-delivery</Term> orders, withdrawals are limited to bKash.[^returns]

A video requirement adds work for customers who need help with a damaged or incomplete order. A wallet refund also adds a step before they get their money back. A founder should explain these conditions before purchase and check that support can handle problems fairly.

When an order goes wrong, customers need a clear way to contact support and find out what happens next. A return policy alone does not show how well those problems are resolved.

## Funding bought room to build a larger operation

Arogga raised money step by step. UNB reported an early $200,000 funding round in 2021.[^early-market] In 2022, the company announced a $1.3 million round led by Hyper and angel investors.[^hyper]

In October 2023, Arogga announced a $5.5 million seed round, consisting of $4.0 million in new funding alongside $1.5 million raised earlier. The company planned to use the money to improve software, its own delivery operation, and customer care in Dhaka, with hubs in two other cities planned for the second quarter of 2024.[^seed]

A new medicine hub needs suitable storage, pharmacy staff and money to buy stock before regular sales begin. These are costs to budget for, rather than proof that the planned hubs opened. <Term name="working-capital">Working capital</Term> is the money needed to keep this daily work running.

Buying more stock can leave less cash for other bills while the company waits for sales. The size of that gap depends on when suppliers must be paid and when customer payments arrive. The sources here do not give Arogga’s payment terms. See [cash flow versus profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit) for the difference between a sale and money available to spend.

## Expansion brings partners back into the model

Arogga later offered lab-test bookings through partner labs. Its website lists home sample collection for eligible tests and locations.[^labs] This adds a different handoff: from the person collecting the sample to the lab preparing the report.

Lab partnerships let Arogga offer another service to existing patients without buying and storing more medicine. The costs shift to collecting samples, paying partner labs, and supporting patients. It is a way to serve the same customer more fully without building another medicine warehouse.

| Service | The customer is relying on | The operating question |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine order | The correct item arriving as agreed | Can stock, checking and delivery keep the promise? |
| Home lab booking | A suitable appointment and a usable report | Who controls collection, transport and the laboratory handoff? |
| Temperature-sensitive delivery | Correct handling throughout the journey | How is the required temperature maintained and recorded? |

This table contrasts Arogga's operational responsibilities across its expanding healthcare offerings.[^site][^labs][^novo]

A December 2025 press release described an agreement for ordering and delivering Novo Nordisk products in Dhaka, with cold-chain requirements. It said QR codes provide product-use instructions. The announcement describes the agreement, rather than independently verifying each delivery.[^novo]

In March 2026, Verge HealthTech Fund invested in Arogga to support a wider health service.[^verge] On 4 October 2026, Arogga’s consultation page listed instant consultations with general doctors, while specialist bookings were marked “Coming Soon.” This is the advertised service, not a tested booking or a measure of medical quality.[^consultations]

Delivering medicine earned Arogga trust with families, but offering medical lab tests and doctor visits adds new responsibilities around test accuracy and medical liability.

## What founders can take from Arogga

- **Check the costs of repeat orders.** Regular medicine needs may help spread delivery costs across larger orders. Calculate the money left after medicine, discounts, checking, packaging and delivery; repeat demand does not guarantee profit.
- **Fix supply before spending heavily on ads.** More orders help only when the team can fill and deliver them reliably.
- **Make ordering simple and keep qualified checks.** A prescription photo saves typing, but pharmacy staff still need to check the order and resolve unclear details.
- **Cost each new service separately.** Lab testing adds sample collection, partner payments and support. Check those costs before assuming it earns more than medicine delivery.
- **Explain returns before purchase.** State which items are excluded, what evidence is required and how the customer can get a refund.
