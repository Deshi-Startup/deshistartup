# 10 Minute School

> **In short:** For its first several years 10 Minute School charged students nothing. The content was free and the money came from corporate sponsorship. When that funding dried up in 2020–21 the company nearly shut down. It then built a PDF book in two weeks and sold it, to find out whether people would pay at all. The useful lessons here are cheap demand testing and raising money without giving up ownership. But the model rested on the founder's own large audience, and that is not something you can copy.

## The problem

Good exam preparation in Bangladesh has long been concentrated in Dhaka. Before admission tests, students from district towns travel to the capital for coaching. That means two costs: the coaching fee, and living in Dhaka. Students who cannot afford both drop out of the race.

Ayman Sadiq watched this up close. He had been teaching at a Dhaka coaching centre since 2012. In a Future Startup interview he said students would turn back because they could not put together the admission money.[^fs-part-one]

A 2023 TechCrunch report put the number of K12 enrolled students in Bangladesh at roughly 42 million. Rural and semi-urban areas have fewer good teachers and preparation centres, which is what pushes families toward Dhaka.[^techcrunch]

## Why the problem is Bangladesh-specific

Coaching centres here are not just a business, they are a social arrangement. Once a family knows where a good teacher teaches, that is where the child is sent. Access to good teaching ends up tied to geography.

The second factor is language. For a Bangla-medium student, free foreign courses made in English are not enough. The NCTB syllabus, the question patterns and the mark distribution are all different.

The third factor is money. In 2023 the company told TechCrunch that 52 percent of its students come from rural and semi-urban areas outside Dhaka, and 34 percent are female.[^techcrunch] Those are company-reported figures, not an independent survey.

## The founding insight

Ayman Sadiq got stuck on accounting while studying at IBA. Coming from a Bangla-medium background made the subject harder. He watched Khan Academy, and found a YouTube channel that covered an entire textbook in just 17 videos. That is where the idea came from: put your own class on video and it can be watched from anywhere.[^fs-part-one]

The insight is simple, but the timing worked. In 2015 nobody had a library of free, syllabus-aligned video in Bangla. The real competition was coaching centres, not other apps.

## The first wedge

The first wedge was university admission tests. HSC came next, because the syllabus largely overlaps. Then SSC. Skill courses were added later.

In his Future Startup interview Ayman said they only moved to the next vertical once returns in the current one started falling.[^fs-part-one] They did not launch every class and every subject at once. The first product was not heavy either: a website with embedded YouTube videos, quizzes and notes.

## Early customers

The first users came from Facebook and YouTube, not from paid acquisition. Ayman was already known from his coaching work. When the website launched on 17 May 2015 the traffic from Facebook took it down within half an hour. Hosting was upgraded overnight and the site relaunched.[^fs-part-one]

The early team was close at hand too. Ayman told Future Startup that his first collaborators were his own former students, and that apart from one person they worked without pay at the start.[^fs-part-one]

One thing is worth noticing here. Getting a team to work for free is not a tactic, it was an advantage a popular teacher happened to hold. A new founder may not have it.

## Building trust

The main trust-building tool was free content. Students could judge the teaching quality before paying anything.

The money came from elsewhere. Talks with Robi's CSR department began in mid-2015. After eight months of negotiation a deal was signed in February 2016, with first-year sponsorship of BDT 70 lakh. That money let Ayman finish his degree and work on this full time. Sponsorship from IPDC, Unilever, Walton and IDLC followed.[^fs-part-one]

The deal had a second effect that mattered as much as the money. Becoming a vendor to a multinational meant sorting out company registration, TIN and the rest of the paperwork. In Ayman's words, without that he might not have taken on the hassle so early, and 10 Minute School might have stayed just a YouTube channel.[^fs-part-one]

## The local lesson

Generic startup advice says get paying users first and worry about a free tier later. 10 Minute School did the reverse. In a market where students had little money and online payment habits had not yet formed, billing a sponsor instead of the customer was a practical route.

The second lesson is about the medium. Facebook and YouTube were not marketing channels here, they were the distribution. The audience was built without ad spend, which kept customer acquisition costs low.

The third lesson is about paperwork. Registration, TIN and a bank account are unavoidable once you want big clients or sponsors. Many founders put this off. In this case the contract forced the job through.

## Constraints and limits

**Sponsorship drying up.** Sponsorship money stopped during the pandemic. Ayman later said he had a few thousand taka in the bank at that point and had to take money from his father. In the same interview he said that one more month would have forced the company to shut down or go into hibernation.[^fs-part-two] A revenue base that held up a seven-year model can disappear in one shock.

**What paying customers expect.** Once you take money, you have to provide support. As Ayman put it, when people pay for a product they expect service when something goes wrong. That required new teams and infrastructure, which became one reason to raise outside funding.[^fs-part-two]

**Finding people.** In the same interview he said tech and product managers are still hard to find in Bangladesh.[^fs-part-two]

**Political and reputational risk.** In July 2024, after Ayman posted in support of the quota reform movement, Startup Bangladesh Limited cancelled a proposed Tk 5 crore investment. The then state minister declined to give a reason, citing a non-disclosure agreement.[^ds-cancel] The investment was later restored.[^tbs-restore] Earlier, in 2022, Ayman had received death threats following a religious smear campaign.[^dt-threats]

## The business model

The model is freemium. Most K12 content is free, and revenue comes from paid courses, live classes and books. In his Future Startup interview Ayman said that 100 percent of the content used to be free and now 95 percent is, but that the number of learners on that 95 percent is far larger than before.[^fs-part-two]

The paid model started with a cheap experiment. In May 2021 a spoken English PDF book was built in two weeks and sold at BDT 75. It sold more than a hundred thousand copies and brought in about as much as a full year of sponsorship.[^fs-part-two] In other words, they learned that people would pay for something good before building the full platform.

Some pricing examples appear in that interview: live classes at BDT 750 per subject per month, and recorded premium courses at BDT 450.[^fs-part-two] Prices change, so current rates are best checked against the company's own pages.

He was equally open about the cost side: content production, sales and marketing, salaries, servers, office rent, and capital costs like cameras and laptops. Their yardstick before scaling into a new market was the ratio between what a customer brings in over their lifetime (LTV) and what it costs to acquire them (CAC). Ayman put the bar at 3 or higher.[^fs-part-two]

## Capital and funding

The company ran without outside investment for its first seven years. In early 2022 it raised $2M in seed funding from Sequoia Capital India's Surge programme. Getting to a term sheet took eight months.[^fs-part-two]

In October 2023 came a $5.5M pre-Series A led by Conjunction Capital, joined by Peak XV's Surge, Startup Bangladesh Limited, CRED founder Kunal Shah and MyAsiaVC's Sajid Rahman. Total funding then stood at $7.5M.[^techcrunch]

The figures the company reported at the announcement: 3.2 million monthly active learners, more than 650,000 paid users and 6.7 million app downloads.[^fs-funding] All of these are company-reported, not audited.

The sequence is what a new founder should notice. Audience first, then proven revenue, then venture money. The funding here was not money to start the business, it was money to grow it.

## What you can copy

- Build an audience and trust with free content before charging anything.
- Raise money without giving up ownership: sponsorship, CSR budgets or brand partnerships.
- Test demand cheaply. Even a BDT 75 PDF can tell you whether people will pay.
- Move to the next vertical only when returns in the current one start falling.
- Acquire customers through channels you own, so ad spend stays low.
- Let a big partner's requirements push you into sorting out paperwork early.

## What not to copy

- Do not build a business on the founder's personal fame. That was an advantage Ayman happened to have, and it brought reputational risk with it.
- Do not treat sponsorship as durable revenue. That income stopped during the pandemic.
- Do not plan to run a team without pay. What works in the first months does not work at scale.
- Do not treat a government or sovereign fund commitment as certain money. The 2024 episode shows it can change even after an announcement.
- Do not assume free-then-paid works on its own. It took 10 Minute School six years to get there, and sponsorship paid the bills in between.

## What remains unknown

Current revenue, profit and loss, paid-user retention, per-vertical unit economics, offline centre economics and the latest valuation are not available in audited public form. Most figures on this page come from 2022 and 2023 interviews and news reports, so the present position may differ.

The company's own site now claims more than 17 million students, alongside online batches, English courses, IELTS preparation and a handful of offline centres.[^tenms-site] Those are company claims, not independently verified. So treat this case study as something to learn from, not as a basis for investment or business decisions.
