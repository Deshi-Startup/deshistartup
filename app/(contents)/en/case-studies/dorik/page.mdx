# Dorik

<CaseCompany slug="dorik" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Dorik grew from a Sylhet side project into a website builder for customers abroad. Its early advantage was a simple way to publish a good-looking site. Later, agencies wanted to use it for their own clients. But a very low price began to work against the business. Dorik's story shows why the right product, the right buyer and the right price have to develop together.[^founder][^profile][^pricing-decision]

A person who needs a website rarely wants to learn a website builder. They want the finished page: a service explained, work displayed, or a way for customers to get in touch.

Dorik was built around that gap. It lets people make and publish websites without writing code. Its founders did not start with the agency tools and artificial intelligence (AI) features offered today. They began with a much smaller product, sold access while it was still in testing, and learned which parts people would pay for.[^early-review][^first-sales]

This case is useful for founders selling software from Bangladesh, especially those trying to turn a skilled service into a product. The decisions below come from launch records, founder accounts and product documentation. The business analysis is ours.

## The idea came from work the founders already understood

Mizanur had been building websites for clients before Dorik existed. In a 2021 interview, he described moving into the template business in 2014. Customers liked the templates but wanted to change them without working through the code. He and his colleagues tried to build an editor more than once. Earlier efforts ran into limits in their skills and resources.[^founder]

The 2019 attempt became Dorik. This time, the team had more experience with both the technical work and the people who needed it. Repeated customer requests gave the project a clear purpose.

That background also gave them a useful constraint. They were not trying to serve every possible web project. They knew a particular kind of buyer: someone who wanted a polished site but did not want web development to become another job.

The Daily Star's 2022 profile places most of Dorik's then 45-person team in Sylhet, with others working remotely from Dhaka and Chattogram. It describes the product as an attempt to balance freedom with ease of use.[^profile] Building for foreign customers did not require the whole team to move abroad. It did require a product that could be understood, tried and supported across distance.

## The first product left a lot out

A July 2020 review is a useful record of what Dorik actually offered before the public launch. It could build single-page sites, connect a custom web address and handle forms. Blogs and multi-page sites were still on the roadmap.[^early-review]

A single-page limit sounds severe beside today's website platforms. But it gave the first version a finish line. A freelancer could publish a portfolio. A small business could explain a service and collect an enquiry. The builder did not need every feature of a large content system to complete those jobs.

This is the difference between a small product and an unfinished one. A small product solves fewer problems. An unfinished product leaves its chosen problem half solved.

The same early review reported that Mizanur wanted feedback from people outside his existing audience. He had a sizeable email list from earlier work, but strangers would bring less goodwill and fewer assumptions about how the tool should work.[^early-review]

There is a practical distinction here. Existing followers can help a launch get noticed. New users can expose the explanation, setup or editing steps that the founder no longer notices. Both groups are useful; they answer different questions.

## The first buyers paid for access, not a promise of future revenue

Mizanur's July 2020 Indie Hackers post describes eight paying customers in the first week after payments were added. Early users then asked for a lifetime offer. The team offered one for US$49 and made beta access immediate. A customer's tweet brought new visitors, while an email reached people already registered. Mizanur reported US$1,054 in sales over the next 24 hours.[^first-sales]

Several things happened together. People could see the product, buy it and start using it. Someone outside the company helped others discover it. The offer rewarded users who were willing to try an early version.

The public Product Hunt launch followed on 10 August 2020.[^launch] The order matters: Dorik had already begun learning from paid use before the bigger launch. A launch day was another route to customers, not the moment the entire business was validated.

A lifetime purchase also creates a different obligation from a subscription. The company receives money once but may need to host the site and support the user for years. That cash can help a young team. It cannot be counted as monthly recurring revenue, which is the subscription revenue expected to repeat each month.

For Dorik, the early offer was evidence that some people would pay. It was not yet evidence that a large customer base would produce a durable software business.

## Agencies changed what the product needed to do

In October 2022, Dorik announced a white-label platform and disclosed pre-seed backing from US investors.[^funding] White label means another business can use the software while presenting it under its own name.

This changes the buying decision. Someone building a personal site needs one finished website. An agency needs a repeatable way to deliver many sites while keeping control of its client relationships.

Dorik's agency page now offers a branded client dashboard, client billing, team access and the ability to transfer a site. The agency can let a client work inside a service that carries the agency's identity.[^agency]

The agency is both a customer and a route to more users. If it chooses Dorik for several projects, one sale can lead to wider use. But that customer is also harder to replace: it may need a demo, a trial project and confidence that the tool will hold up across its work.

Dorik's published customer stories include Melissa Buening of Unicorns Agency describing her use of the platform for new client sites.[^customers] This is a company-selected testimonial, but it makes the use case concrete. The value is not only “I can build a page.” It is “I can deliver my next client project with this tool.”

That helps explain why features that look secondary to a solo user - branding, access rights and handover - can matter so much to an agency.

## When being cheap began to hurt

Mizanur said the team expected monthly paid-customer growth to fall by 50%. He reported a 15% drop instead. He also said that adding US$100 in monthly recurring revenue now took about four new paying customers, compared with 25 before.[^pricing-decision]

The decision tested whether price still matched value. Keeping old rates protected existing customers while the team learned how new buyers would respond.

It also changed which result mattered. A lower number of new buyers can be acceptable if the customers who remain bring in enough revenue to fund the service. Maximising sign-ups and building a sound business are not always the same task.

These figures come from the founder, without the underlying customer records or a controlled comparison. They support a reported outcome of this decision. They do not tell another founder how much demand their own price increase would lose.

## Look at what must remain true after a price rise

A higher price creates room to serve customers well. It also raises what they expect.

For a website builder, those expectations continue long after the first page is published. A form must keep working. A client's site must stay available. Someone must be able to change the page without breaking it. If a product costs more but makes these jobs harder, a stronger price can become a stronger reason to leave.

The first version reduced the work needed to publish. Agency features reduced the work needed to deliver repeatedly. Pricing then had to capture enough of that value to sustain the company.

A founder looking only at competitors' prices would miss most of that sequence. The starting question is what valuable work the product completes, and for whom. Price comparisons come after that.

## AI tackles the blank page - but the website still has to work

Dorik AI's February 2024 launch added another way to begin.[^ai-launch] Its current product page describes generating a website from a prompt, along with text and images that users can edit.[^ai]

This fits the original problem. Templates still ask a beginner to choose a layout and decide what belongs on it. A generated first draft can shorten that step. The user has something to react to instead of an empty canvas.

The stronger product question comes afterward. Can the business owner turn that draft into an accurate, useful site? Are the services, prices and contact details correct? Can the agency make it suit the client rather than accept a generic page?

Generating a draft and publishing a site are separate achievements. Publishing and winning a customer are separate again. A useful AI feature should be judged at the stage of the job it improves, rather than by how impressive the first screen looks.

Dorik's route into AI is therefore worth studying as an extension of its existing product. The team already had editing, publishing and customer workflows for a generated page to enter. A founder copying only the generation step would still have the rest of that work to build.

## How the business gets paid now

On 13 September 2026, Dorik's pricing page listed monthly, annual, two-year and lifetime options. Its agency subscription showed US$79 per month when billed as US$948 annually, or US$99 on a monthly billing plan.[^plans]

Those figures should not be mixed with the historical price experiment. Plans, bundles and payment timing have changed.

They also show why “price per month” is an incomplete description of a sale. An annual payment brings cash forward and asks for a larger commitment. A monthly plan lowers that initial commitment but lets the customer leave sooner. A lifetime offer brings cash forward further while leaving a long service obligation.

For the customer, the right comparison includes setup time, ongoing work and the cost of moving later. For Dorik, it includes hosting, support, product development and the pattern of future payments. The public sources do not provide enough detail to calculate its profit per plan.

## What is worth copying - and what is not

**Start close to a problem you have seen repeatedly.** Dorik's origin in client and template work gave the founders more than an idea. It gave them a view of where people got stuck.

**Finish one useful job before widening the product.** A publishable single-page site was a real outcome. Agency workflows and AI came later.

**Notice when the buyer changes.** Agencies needed a delivery system for their own business. Treating them as people who simply wanted more websites would have missed important parts of the product.

**Revisit price when you have evidence.** Dorik's pricing account describes a specific mismatch between product value, buyer perception and revenue. It is a reason to examine those relationships, not a licence to raise prices blindly.

The limits matter too. Do not copy lifetime deals without estimating long-term costs. Do not use a launch spike as proof of recurring demand. And do not assume a global market makes customer support or distribution easy.

Dorik's most useful achievement is the sequence: a small product became useful enough to sell, then useful enough to deliver other businesses' work. Pricing had to catch up with that change.
