# Chaldal

<CaseCompany slug="chaldal" locale="en" />

> **In short:** Chaldal shows how a useful service with real customers can still reach a serious crisis. Its history raises hard questions about expansion, side businesses and dependence on fresh investment. The cost reached employees waiting for their pay. For a founder, the warning is clear: test each expansion and protect the cash needed to keep existing promises.

## Start with the failure

In September 2026, Chaldal workers protested over unpaid salaries and alleged bounced cheques. CEO Waseem Alim acknowledged a severe cash shortage and employee hardship.[^wages]

On 30 September, The Financial Express reported that Chaldal had suspended operations. The report does not explain the full scope or duration of that suspension. It does not establish permanent closure.[^suspension]

This is a cautionary case about a promising business that reached that point. We examine documented setbacks, management's explanations and lessons for other Bangladeshi founders. The sources were checked on 3 October 2026. Our lessons are analysis; the sources do not let us calculate how much each decision contributed to the crisis.

## Why the promise was real

Chaldal began in 2013, founded by Waseem Alim, Tejas Viswanath and Zia Ashraf.[^ifc-disclosure] Its offer was easy to understand: order groceries without making the shopping trip yourself.[^about] In Dhaka, avoiding a trip through traffic gave that offer a clear use.

The Business Standard's account of the early days describes a Gulshan apartment operation. Friends and neighbours were early customers, the founders used Facebook to reach people, and Zia Ashraf handled buying and delivery himself.[^early-story] That small starting area let the team learn from actual orders.

The business also gained serious backing. Chaldal joined Y Combinator in 2015.[^yc-company] In September 2021, The Daily Star reported a US$10 million funding round, 27 warehouses across four cities, and plans for 15 more cities.[^expansion]

The service and the funding were real. Neither proved that a much larger business could keep paying its bills. That distinction matters throughout this case.

## A new city needed a new demand test

Waseem's July 2023 account links the retreat to an investor's pullback. He said demand outside Dhaka needed more marketing or product choice, while convenience mattered less and local shops had stronger relationships.[^retreat]

That is a much harder problem than opening another warehouse. If people already find nearby shopping easy, faster delivery alone may not persuade them to change. A city can have plenty of residents but too few repeat orders in a small delivery area to support the costs.

Our reading is that the expansion needed stronger proof of local demand before taking on more ongoing costs. That does not mean every city outside Dhaka is a bad market. It means the reason people pay in one place may not carry over to another.

Before entering a new city, test why customers would switch, whether they order again, and what serving that area costs. Set a spending limit and a decision date. A launch announcement cannot answer those questions.

## More businesses created more unfinished work

In a February 2021 interview, Waseem said the Cookups acquisition had been completed in 2020. He also discussed payments and expansion beyond the existing business.[^founder-twenty-one] BanglaMeds announced its acquisition by Chaldal in December 2021.[^banglameds]

By July 2023, Waseem said Cookups' customer experience was not yet fully joined up with Chaldal, and the service still needed a better match with customer needs. He spoke more positively about BanglaMeds.[^retreat]

There was also a separate venture that stopped. In September 2023, Chaldal deputy director Shahriar Rubayet told The Business Standard that its ridesharing service had run for about six months after registering in 2019. He blamed strong competitors and rides arranged outside the apps for making it unsustainable.[^ridesharing]

These are different outcomes, not proof that every side business failed. But they show why “we already have the technology” is too weak a reason to enter another market. Each service still needs paying customers, a team and work to fit it into the company.

The public sources do not show what each venture earned or lost. We cannot say an acquisition caused the wage crisis. The practical warning is to put a separate budget, responsible person and stop condition against every new business. Count the senior team's time as a cost too.

## What warehouse results left out

Waseem made an important distinction in February 2021: he described warehouse operations as positive, but said the overall business was not yet positive because it kept investing.[^founder-twenty-one] He did not define that financial measure. This was his account, not an audited group profit figure.

Chaldal's model involved holding groceries in small warehouses and using software to show available stock.[^yc-model] Customers pay for goods, and the company also lists delivery charges. Cash on delivery and a return policy offer ways to address customers' concerns.[^help] A policy alone does not prove that complaints were handled well. The money left after buying the goods still has to cover packing, delivery, spoilage, staff, rent and the wider company.

A founder can make two mistakes here. The first is treating sales as profit. The second is counting only the costs inside a warehouse or an order, while leaving out the team and projects above it.

Check both [what one order earns after its costs](/en/metrics/unit-economics) and what the whole business spends. Then check [when the cash arrives and leaves](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit). A positive result in one part does not settle the other questions.

## Expected funding was not dependable cash

The Daily Star reported in April 2026 that Chaldal had aimed to raise Tk100 crore in 2025 but received about half. Waseem confirmed the funding shortfall and said bank support had not come.[^funding-gap]

In March 2026, The Business Standard reported monthly sales of about Tk40 crore and a working-capital gap of the same amount. Chaldal sought emergency funding as wages and other bills mounted.[^march-crisis] These are reported figures, not audited accounts. A shortfall is not the same thing as total debt, and a funding request is not money received.

**Working capital** is the money needed to keep everyday trading going while payments and bills fall on different dates. But a shortage can also sit alongside ongoing losses. Calling a problem “temporary” does not show that a loan will solve it.

Build a cash plan that works if the next investment does not arrive. List the cash available, likely customer receipts and payment dates. Include the cost of reducing or closing an operation. Set the point at which you stop adding commitments while you can still meet existing ones.

Use the [working-capital calculator](/en/tools/working-capital-calculator) to understand cash tied up in trading. It cannot decide whether a whole company can survive; that needs the full costs, debts and cash plan.

## Employees carried the cost of the crisis

The March 2026 reporting described a wage-payment agreement after protests. December pay had reportedly been paid, with later dates promised for January and February.[^march-crisis]

September brought fresh protests. One employee told The Daily Star that December - February salaries had been cleared, but later wages remained unpaid. Workers described difficulty paying for accommodation and travelling home.[^september-workers] That evidence matters: we should not claim the March agreement was never honoured. We can say the wage-payment crisis recurred.

The 23 September report added allegations that settlement cheques had bounced. Waseem said some dues had been cleared and settlements were continuing.[^wages] That response is not proof that every worker received everything owed.

For an employee, wages cover this month's living costs. They cannot pay rent with a company's next funding target. Repeated unpaid salaries are a failure to meet basic commitments, whatever the explanation for the cash shortage.

The lesson for management is to act before payroll depends on a hoped-for rescue. Keep accurate records of dues, give staff clear information and make payment promises grounded in available money. Do not treat staff waiting without pay as another source of funding.

## Funding conditions mattered, but they are not the whole lesson

In the September Financial Express report, Waseem blamed a lack of funding for businesses at Chaldal's scale and falling foreign investment.[^suspension] That is management's explanation of the financing constraint. It does not answer every question about earlier spending or why employees were left waiting.

The record also contains a useful response to a different shock. During the 2024 internet blackout, reporting described Chaldal stopping fresh purchases of perishable goods, selling stock through local markets and arranging orders by phone to limit losses.[^blackout] It would be wrong to invent a story that all its stock was lost.

A capable response to one emergency can sit alongside serious failures elsewhere. Founders should study both. The actions worth copying are small initial tests and quick steps to protect stock when demand or access changes. The pattern to avoid is growing commitments without a plan it can rely on to pay them when funding falls short.

## What this case cannot settle

The sources reviewed do not provide audited company-wide accounts, losses by city or venture, board decisions, a full record of money owed or verified final wage settlements. The precise current scope of service also needs a fresh check before anyone relies on it.

So this case does not establish fraud, a legal finding that Chaldal cannot pay its debts, or a permanent end to the company. Those are separate claims requiring separate evidence. The documented retreats, stopped venture and repeated payroll failures already give founders enough reason to question the growth story.

Before your next expansion, write down what would make you stop it, how much that would cost, and whether existing staff and suppliers could still be paid. If the answer depends on investment that has not arrived, the plan needs changing now.
