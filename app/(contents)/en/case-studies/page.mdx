# Case Studies: Learn from Bangladeshi Startup Decisions

> **In short:** Read a case for the decision a team faced, the conditions around it and what happened afterwards. Before copying a tactic, check what differs in your own business. A founder's account can explain their experience without proving that the same action caused success or will work for you.

You may come to a startup story looking for an answer to a current problem: how to find the first customers, make delivery work or decide whether to expand. A story is useful when it shows the choices available at the time. Knowing only that a company eventually grew tells you much less about what to do next.

Local cases can make the practical details easier to examine. Payment habits, delivery arrangements, language, regulation and customer expectations affect the work differently across Bangladesh's sectors. Look for the part of the story that resembles your actual problem, rather than assuming that two companies in the same industry should follow the same plan.

## Read the decision in its original circumstances

Begin with what the team knew before taking the action. Who was the customer, what were they doing already and what made the existing arrangement unsatisfactory? Then follow what the team tried, the resources it used and the difficulties it encountered. Work done by founders behind the scenes can matter as much as the feature customers saw.

That sequence helps you separate a useful explanation from hindsight. A tactic that now looks obvious may have been one experiment among several. A decision that failed may still reveal a constraint you need to investigate. The collection therefore includes planned success and failure cases, not only surviving companies.

Our case-study format follows [fifteen questions](https://github.com/Deshi-Startup/deshistartup/blob/main/plan/case-study-format.md), covering the problem, first customers, business model, operations and limits. You do not need to memorise the format. Use it to notice what a story has not yet answered, especially when a confident conclusion rests on a thin account of events.

## Check what the source can actually establish

A founder interview can tell you why the founder says they made a decision. A company's announcement can report its own figures or plans. Neither automatically supplies an independent check of results. We label founder accounts, company claims and unknowns so that those differences remain visible.

When reading a reported result, check the period, the meaning of the number and where it came from. Customer registrations, repeat purchases and money earned describe different things. If a case does not disclose the acquisition cost or the attempts that failed, leave those questions open rather than assuming the tactic was cheap and consistently successful.

Looking only at companies that survived also leaves out teams that tried similar things without the same outcome. That is why one successful case cannot, by itself, establish a general rule for the market.

## Apply one lesson to the work in front of you

Choose one decision from the case and compare its conditions with yours. Did the team already know the buyers? Could it afford to do manual work for longer? Did a partner supply something you would have to build or pay for? These differences can change whether the same action is feasible.

Write down the question you would need to answer before adopting the tactic. If the story describes finding customers through introductions, your question might be whether you can reach the relevant buyer through your own network or an association. It is not yet a reason to abandon other ways of reaching customers. Use [idea validation](/en/validation) to turn that question into an appropriate interview or small test.
