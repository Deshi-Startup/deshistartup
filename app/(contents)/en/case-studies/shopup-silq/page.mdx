# ShopUp / SILQ

<CaseCompany slug="shopup-silq" locale="en" />

> **In short:** ShopUp discovered a painful truth: an online seller could have plenty of orders and still go broke because their cash was trapped with delivery couriers. In Bangladesh, almost everyone pays with <Term name="cod">cash on delivery (COD)</Term>, so money often takes days or weeks to get back to the seller. Solving that cash squeeze pushed ShopUp from building Facebook software into small business lending and its own delivery fleet, REDX.[^founder-discussion] Later, its wholesale business (Mokam) tried to help neighborhood *mudir dokans* (corner grocery shops) stay stocked with rice, oil, and soap. Large wholesale sales did not mean large profits. In 2025, ShopUp merged with Saudi Arabia's Sary to create SILQ, bringing their trade and delivery operations together with a new financial services arm serving both markets.[^merger]

## Start with the seller who already has a business

ShopUp's story begins with Afeef Zaman visiting his grandparents in Bauphal, Patuakhali. He watched local potters lose ground to bigger companies with better distribution. The problem was not making nice clay pots; it was finding supplies, delivering goods safely, and getting paid on time.[^about]

ShopUp started by building tools for Facebook merchants who were already selling clothes, crafts, and food online. These were real small businesses looking for an easier way to manage orders.[^history]

Working with active sellers showed ShopUp real everyday headaches: lost packages, late customer payments, and shopkeepers who did not have enough cash to buy new stock.

ShopUp also met sellers in person. In February 2018, it ran a SuperSellers workshop with The Daily Star. Small business owners gathered to learn about digital marketing, customer habits, and Facebook ads.[^seller-workshop]

The event gave ShopUp a chance to listen directly to merchant problems. Public reports do not say how many attendees ended up becoming paying software customers, but it gave the team front-row access to their market.

## The next product was hiding inside a delayed payment

Many Facebook sellers hit a painful cash wall. Customers bought their products, but the cash sat with delivery couriers for days or weeks. In a 2022 discussion with Bangladesh Angels, ShopUp's founders explained that this delayed cash created a severe <Term name="working-capital">working capital</Term> crunch. Sellers could not buy new inventory until old money arrived. To solve this, ShopUp launched a merchant loan service and built its own delivery network, REDX.[^founder-discussion]

These two solutions do very different jobs:
- A loan gives the seller emergency cash while they wait.
- A faster courier network gets cash back to the seller quickly so they do not need to borrow in the first place.

A loan can help a seller buy inventory today, but their next batch of sales will just get trapped again. Faster delivery and instant cash payouts fix the root cause.

For an online seller, a package showing as "delivered" does not finish the job. The sale is only truly complete when the money is back in their hands to buy new stock.

[Truck Lagbe ran into a similar cash delay](/en/case-studies/truck-lagbe#enterprise-customers-brought-a-second-business), paying truck drivers immediately while waiting months for corporate clients to pay invoices. Returning money that a courier has already collected requires fast daily settlements; letting business clients pay later takes serious cash in reserve.

## The first lending model used a partner's strengths

In its early lending pilot with BRAC, ShopUp reviewed a seller's online sales data and recommended loan amounts. A local BRAC branch processed the paperwork and sent the money to the merchant's bank or bKash account. As the merchant made sales, ShopUp collected loan repayments and forwarded them to BRAC.[^brac-pilot]

BRAC reported that the pilot served 380 small businesses by January 2019. It noted that many sellers asked for a second loan after paying off the first. While this was positive feedback from a partner, it was not an independent audit of loan defaults or long-term profit.[^brac-pilot]

ShopUp brought fresh data on online sellers, while BRAC brought decades of lending experience and local branches. This let the young startup test loans without taking on the whole banking burden alone.

It also shows an important local lesson: online apps in Bangladesh often rely on respected offline institutions that customers already trust.

## Why supply neighbourhood shops rather than replace them?

In December 2019, ShopUp launched Mokam to supply wholesale products to local corner grocery shops (*mudir dokans*). Mokam bought goods in bulk from food mills and big brands, while REDX moved the boxes to small retail stores.[^about]

Neighborhood corner shops already have loyal neighbors, great street locations, and steady daily sales. Helping them restock was much easier than trying to convince families to stop visiting their trusted local shopkeeper. In 2021, ShopUp stated clearly that it wanted to stay a business-to-business supplier rather than selling groceries directly to consumers.[^service-problems]

This is very different from trying to build a consumer grocery delivery app. The corner shopkeeper keeps their customer relationship. The wholesale supplier competes on low prices, reliable delivery, and simple restocking.

Yet wholesale logistics has sharp limits. An app makes it easy to order boxes on a screen. But someone still has to load heavy sacks of rice into trucks, navigate bad roads, and replace damaged goods. A slick mobile app does not make a broken bag of flour any cheaper to deliver.

Expanding into physical logistics forced ShopUp to take on heavy real-world costs.

## Buying a team solved a problem that hiring had not

Growing fast created another big headache: finding great software engineers. In their Bangladesh Angels interview, ShopUp's leaders explained that it was hard to hire experienced tech talent when the startup was still small and unknown. Indian e-commerce platform Voonik offered a seasoned engineering team and ready-made wholesale software.[^founder-discussion]

In February 2020, ShopUp acquired Voonik's business-to-business division. Voonik's tech team and founders, Sujayath Ali and Navaneetha Krishnan, joined ShopUp, while Voonik's consumer retail branch was spun off to another firm called Schoolay.[^voonik]

ShopUp bought the exact engineering team and software it needed to run wholesale operations, leaving the consumer business behind.

Buying a team can be much faster than hiring one engineer at a time. But it also creates work that normal hiring does not: blending two company cultures, merging old software systems, and sorting out leadership roles. Public reports tell us why they made the deal, but do not disclose how much time or money it ultimately saved.

## Credit records became a customer problem of their own

<CaseDetail title="The test: can a merchant see that a payment has cleared?">

In August 2021, The Business Standard reported that ShopUp's credit service, Baki, had launched before the software was fully automated. Shopkeeper repayments were not always updated quickly in the system, causing confusion. A local field agent said the service had been paused, while Afeef explained that it remained active for select shops with good payment track records.[^service-problems]

When a shopkeeper pays off their bill, seeing an outdated balance causes real worry. It leaves them unsure how much they owe and blocks them from ordering new stock.

**Make sure repayments and receipts work smoothly before scaling loans.** A shopkeeper must see their balance update immediately and know exactly whom to call if numbers do not match.

</CaseDetail>

The same 2021 report also noted delivery delays and missing packages. Afeef acknowledged these problems, stating that customer support needed improvement and that the team was working on fixes.[^service-problems] These reports show the growing pains of rapid scaling.

## Large sales came with large costs

In May 2024, The Business Standard reported on audited financial filings for ShopUp's parent company for the year ending June 2023. Annual revenue reached about $129 million, but net losses topped $49 million. Most of that revenue came from moving wholesale goods.[^group-accounts]

ShopUp responded that over $14 million of that loss came from an employee share plan (<Term name="esop">ESOP</Term>), which was a non-cash accounting expense. Excluding that expense, the company said its loss had shrunk. A share award is still a cost, but it does not require the same cash payment as a salary that year.[^group-accounts]

By July 2024, ShopUp announced that its packaged-goods wholesale division, Mokam CPG, had turned a profit. It shared these numbers at a press briefing.[^division-results]

<CaseDetail title="What remains from BDT 100 of sales?">

| Mokam CPG, financial year 2023-24 | Company-reported figure |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | More than BDT 2,000 crore |
| Operating profit | BDT 26 crore |
| Operating profit per BDT 100 of revenue | About BDT 1.30 or less |

The last row is our calculation: 26 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 1.30. Because revenue was **more than** 2,000 crore taka, the actual margin per 100 taka of sales was even lower. These are unaudited numbers shared by management.[^division-results]

Operating profit is what is left after operating costs, including goods, wages, warehouses, and delivery, but before interest and taxes. It is not the entire company's final net profit.

</CaseDetail>

Making only 1.30 taka on every 100 taka of sales leaves almost zero room for error. If a sack of flour breaks, a delivery truck sits in traffic for hours, diesel prices spike, or a shopkeeper fails to pay their bill on time, that tiny profit turns into a loss. This is the big dilemma for wholesale grocery startups across South Asia: managing trucks, warehouses, and tech teams is expensive, while basic groceries have paper-thin profit margins.

Both news reports make sense together. One part of a business can make a profit while the parent company takes an overall loss. Improving profits later does not wipe away the money spent in earlier years.

This is why founders must look at profit per order (<Term name="unit-economics">unit economics</Term>) alongside total sales, and watch real cash flow alongside reported profits.

## Capital changed form as the business changed

In its early years, ShopUp grew by raising money from venture capital investors in exchange for company shares.[^history] But buying inventory and funding merchant credit takes a different kind of money: cash that can be lent out, collected, and lent out again.

In August 2024, ShopUp announced $6.5 million in debt funding from Saison Investment Management, part of Japan's Credit Saison.[^saison] Debt is a loan that must be paid back with interest; it is completely different from selling company shares to venture investors.

The type of funding changed even more after teaming up with Sary. In April 2025, the two companies announced a $110 million financing package that combined equity and a large loan facility for SILQ Financial. ShopUp and Sary would keep operating in their home markets, while SILQ Financial would offer financing to businesses across the two networks.[^merger] Afeef became group CEO, while Sary's Mohammed Aldossary led the financial services division.

In July 2026, investment fund Fasanara announced a $75 million credit facility for SILQ's Saudi business, Fina.[^fasanara] The next month, SILQ reported a $100 million credit package from Fasanara, Gemcorp, and Amwal for its Saudi operations.[^fina]

These announcements describe credit facilities that can be drawn down as needed, not pure cash added to ShopUp's bank account in Bangladesh. Having a credit line does not guarantee that every dollar will be lent out or repaid successfully.

This international pivot shows how capital needs change with your business model: delivering groceries in Bangladesh required venture capital to build warehouses and buy delivery vans, while financing business trade in Saudi Arabia relies on large credit lines to earn steady interest margins.

## What founders can take from this

- **Look closely at where money gets stuck.** Online sellers often need their delivery cash collected quickly more than they need another software dashboard.
- **High sales volume cannot rescue paper-thin margins.** Generating massive wholesale revenue looks impressive, but an operating margin of around 1.3% leaves little room for unexpected spoilage, bad debts, or transport delays.
- **Build on top of existing local shops.** ShopUp supplied corner grocers who already had steady daily customers, rather than trying to change consumer shopping habits.
- **Partner with established players where it counts.** BRAC's branch network and lending experience powered ShopUp's early loan tests.
- **Make sure payments and receipts work every single time.** If an app fails to show that a debt was paid, customers lose trust immediately.
- **Always check which part of the company a profit number describes.** A single profitable division does not mean the entire parent company is making money.

Do not try to launch wholesale, logistics, and lending all at once. Doing all three adds huge operational risks. Founders can learn from ShopUp's path without trying to build a massive conglomerate on day one.

## What we still cannot tell

Public records do not show SILQ's current audited group profits, long-term customer <Term name="retention">retention</Term>, loan default rates across all orders, or the exact financial gains from the Sary merger. Early workshops and loan tests show how ShopUp met its first merchants, but public records do not detail its first ten paying software customers.

Following the online seller's delayed cash led ShopUp far beyond basic software. Solving that problem meant managing warehouses, running delivery fleets, and handling merchant money. ShopUp solved a huge problem for small businesses, but it also took on a much tougher, more capital-intensive business to run.
