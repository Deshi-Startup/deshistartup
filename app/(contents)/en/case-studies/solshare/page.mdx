# SOLshare

<CaseCompany slug="solshare" locale="en" />

> **In short:** SOLshare started in rural Bangladesh with a clever idea: letting neighbors trade extra solar power with each other.[^early-insight] Using smart digital meters called SOLboxes, village homes with rooftop solar panels could sell excess electricity to neighboring shops and families using mobile money. But by 2021, the government extended the national electrical grid to almost every village, making off-grid solar trading less urgent. Instead of giving up, SOLshare adapted: it connected its village networks into the national power grid and launched SOLmobility - leasing modern lithium-ion batteries to Bangladesh's huge fleet of two million electric auto-rickshaws. SOLshare's story shows how climate and hardware startups must adapt when national infrastructure changes, manage expensive physical equipment, and move from charity grants to real business profits.

Imagine a village family with a solar panel on their tin roof. By 2:00 PM on a sunny afternoon, their battery is completely full. Any extra sunshine hitting the panel goes to waste. Meanwhile, a neighbor running a small tailoring shop next door has no electricity to run a sewing machine or a light bulb.

SOLshare was created to fix this waste. Instead of building expensive power plants, the company built a smart local trading network: neighbors could buy and sell extra solar electricity to each other using bKash on their phones. But as the national power grid reached more villages, SOLshare had to make a big shift: connecting village solar to the national utility grid, putting solar on factory roofs, and financing batteries for electric auto-rickshaws.

## Bangladesh had already paid for much of the starting point

Bangladesh's Solar Home System program was one of the biggest off-grid solar projects in history. Managed by state-backed IDCOL, the program helped finance more than 4.1 million solar kits on village homes by 2018, using microfinance loans and international aid.[^solar-programme]

This gave Bangladesh a massive foundation: millions of village homes already owned solar panels, inverters, and batteries. But every home sat on its own, like an isolated island, with no way to share power with a neighbor.

Sebastian Groh, who had worked in investment banking and rural energy research, saw a huge opportunity: connecting these isolated solar islands together into a shared marketplace. He moved to Dhaka in 2014 to build the meters and software to connect them.[^founder-interview]

Connecting these home panels let families with extra electricity sell their power, while neighbors without solar panels could buy cheap, clean electricity without buying an entire solar kit.

## The founders found a behaviour they could build around

While visiting villages in 2014, the founders noticed that people were already sharing power informally. A villager would run a long wire over bamboo poles to let a neighbor charge a mobile phone or power an emergency bulb, settling up with small cash tips or favors.[^early-insight]

The human desire to share electricity was already there. What was missing was an accurate meter to measure kilowatt-hours, make sure wires were safe, and handle payments smoothly.

However, selling "spare power" takes real capital. Building smart digital meters, burying safe electrical cables in village dirt, putting up transformers, and paying mobile money transaction fees costs money that must be recovered through customer fees.

## What the box adds

At the heart of SOLshare’s network is the SOLbox: a two-way digital electricity meter with a mobile SIM card, automatic circuit breakers, and digital tracking.[^solgrid]

As shown by GSMA, the system connects smart meters with cloud software, field-agent apps, and bKash mobile wallets.[^gsma] When a home has extra solar electricity, the SOLbox sends it out to the village network. When a home needs power at night, it draws electricity back in, and the system settles the bill automatically.

Roles change throughout the day: a family acts as a mini-power plant on a sunny afternoon, and becomes an electricity buyer at night to run lights and fans.

Early village tests charged a small fee on every unit of power traded, as reported by IEEE Spectrum in 2018.[^field-report]

## Trust meant letting someone near an expensive asset

Convincing village homeowners to connect their solar panels to an unfamiliar network was not easy. For most families, their solar panel and battery represented years of hard-earned microfinance loan payments. They worried that connecting wires to a neighbor’s house would drain their battery or damage their equipment.

To build trust, SOLshare partnered with Grameen Shakti, Bangladesh's largest rural solar installer, using its trusted village technicians to set up wires, inspect equipment, and handle repairs.[^field-report]

In an interview with IIX, Sebastian explained that teaching people how the tech worked, paying for hardware, and overcoming community skepticism were their toughest early hurdles.[^founder-interview]

Winning over the community took showing that the system protected their batteries and delivered dependable earnings to their mobile phones.

## The decision: what happens when the grid arrives?

By 2021, the government of Bangladesh announced that the national electrical grid had reached almost every corner of the country, running power lines into remote villages.[^solar-programme]

The arrival of the national grid threatened SOLshare's original village business. When villagers could get cheap, subsidized electricity from government power lines, their interest in paying for private solar microgrids disappeared.

Sebastian had anticipated this shift years earlier, noting in 2016 that decentralized solar networks would eventually have to connect directly into the national power grid.[^early-insight]

In 2022, SOLshare launched its new solution: a grid-connected microgrid at Saddam Bazar, using a special transformer (Point of Common Coupling, or PCC) to link the village solar network directly to the national rural electricity grid (BREB).[^report-twenty-two][^pcc]

<CaseDetail title="A new buyer for the same solar power">

Before the national grid connection, a village network could only sell power to neighbors who happened to be home. If neighbors were away, extra solar power was wasted. The grid connection turned the village into a community mini-power plant, allowing villagers to sell extra solar electricity directly into the national power grid under official net-metering rules.[^pcc]

This changed SOLshare's business: instead of only helping off-grid homes, it helped provide stable clean energy to the national grid.

</CaseDetail>

## A grid connection did not solve every problem

Connecting to the national grid brought a new headache: rural power cuts (load-shedding). When the national grid goes dark, safety rules require solar systems to disconnect automatically so they do not shock utility repair crews working on power lines. That meant village solar could not sell power when the national grid was off.

To find a use for extra solar power during blackouts, SOLshare added a steady local customer: a commercial charging station for electric three-wheelers (*easy bikes* and auto-rickshaws), soaking up extra midday sunshine to charge vehicle batteries.[^report-twenty-four]

## A battery is useful long before its owner can afford it

Testing rickshaw charging points opened the door to a much bigger commercial opportunity. Over two million electric three-wheelers (easy bikes) operate across Bangladesh every day, predominantly powered by traditional lead-acid batteries. While lead-acid batteries are inexpensive upfront, they degrade quickly, require frequent replacements, and introduce significant environmental and operational inefficiencies.

Higher-performance lithium-ion battery technology offers greater energy density and longer cycle life, but the upfront capital cost is too high for individual operators to purchase outright without credit.

To address this financing bottleneck, SOLshare launched SOLmobility. The platform integrates IoT telemetry with charging-station infrastructure and battery hardware, monitoring real-time performance, location, and battery health. By providing transparent operating data and remote risk-mitigation tools, SOLmobility enables banks and non-bank financial institutions to finance charging-station operators and fleet managers with reduced credit risk.[^mobility]

| Participant | What a workable offer needs to establish |
|---|---|
| Driver / Operator | Predictable operating costs, daily driving range, and battery reliability. |
| Charging operator | Equipment financing, station throughput, maintenance, and power costs. |
| Financial institution | Verified usage telemetry, loan repayment visibility, and asset recovery tools. |

This alignment demonstrates what each partner requires to make commercial battery deployment viable.

Data visibility reduces perceived credit risk for lenders, while reliable charging infrastructure and modern battery packs help operators increase daily fleet uptime and earnings.

## The company had to learn how to sell the new offer

Selling batteries to rickshaw drivers required new operating skills. In a November 2024 interview, Sebastian talked about the challenge of finding good battery cell suppliers and learning how to pitch conservative garage owners who were used to traditional lead batteries.[^mobility-interview]

The company reported converting more than 200 electric rickshaws to smart lithium batteries, noting that drivers earned more money each day thanks to lighter vehicles and faster charging times.[^mobility-interview]

The business was first tested in Rajshahi in 2022, partnering with BAT Bangladesh, Btomorrow Ventures, and the Rajshahi City Corporation to build solar-powered charging garages.[^report-twenty-two]

Running physical charging garages taught the team how to manage battery swaps, work with local electricity grids, and collect daily payments from drivers.

## Who pays for the work?

SOLshare earns revenue in three main ways: fees on village solar trading, building solar installations on factory rooftops, and leasing smart batteries for electric rickshaws.[^report-twenty-two]

In its 2024 annual corporate disclosures, the company reported total annual revenues of over $670,000 across its divisions.[^report-twenty-four]

The company's funding history shows the careful balance needed for hardware and climate startups in emerging markets. Non-repayable grants, like GSMA's 2015 seed grant, funded the early hardware research and village tests when venture capital funds were unwilling to take the technical risk.[^gsma] Impact investment from IIX followed to expand early setups.[^founder-interview]

In June 2024, SOLshare raised $2.2 million toward a $4 million investment round led by SBK Tech Ventures, alongside an international group that won a €5.3 million EU Horizon research grant.[^funding]

The big challenge for climate hardware startups is moving from grant-funded pilot tests to self-sustaining business profits. While research grants fund early university trials, long-term survival requires commercial revenues from battery leases and factory solar roofs to cover equipment wear-and-tear, battery replacements, and field maintenance.

## What founders can take from this

- **Build software around habits people already have.** SOLshare did not invent sharing electricity; it built digital meters to measure and monetize informal sharing that villagers were already doing over bamboo poles.
- **Use existing physical infrastructure.** Building on top of Bangladesh's 4.1 million home solar systems gave the startup a ready-made network of solar panels to connect.
- **Adapt quickly when the market changes.** When national power lines reached rural villages, SOLshare pivoted to connecting with the national grid and financing electric vehicle batteries.
- **Pair advanced hardware with easy financing.** Better hardware (like lithium batteries) will not sell in low-income markets unless drivers can pay for it through small daily installments.
- **Use grant funding for research, but aim for commercial revenue.** Grants are great for early prototypes, but long-term survival requires commercial sales that pay for equipment depreciation and field repairs.

## What remains uncertain

Audited profit numbers for each division, hardware depreciation schedules, long-term battery lifespans, and bad debt rates in the vehicle leasing business are not public.

Early research reports and pilot milestones show that the technology works, but nationwide profit margins have not been verified by independent audits.

SOLshare's evolution from a village microgrid test to an electric rickshaw battery platform shows the resilience needed to build climate tech in emerging markets. Long-term commercial success depends on whether smart battery leasing can expand profitably across thousands of city garages without ongoing grant subsidies.
