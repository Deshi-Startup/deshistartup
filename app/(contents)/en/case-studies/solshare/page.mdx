# SOLshare

<CaseCompany slug="solshare" locale="en" />

> **In short:** SOLshare noticed two things happening side by side: some homes had spare solar power, while neighbours wanted more electricity. People were already making informal deals to share it.[^early-insight] The company built a way to measure the power and settle the payment. Its later grid and battery work tests the same idea in new settings: useful equipment can earn more when it has the right connection.

The company began with a local electricity trade. It later faced a harder question: what should that service become when the national grid reaches the village?

That change makes SOLshare more than a story about solar panels. It is a case about adapting a business as the infrastructure around it improves. The later move into electric-vehicle batteries adds another layer: equipment can work well and still be too costly for its user to buy.

## At a glance

| Part of the business | The job it does |
|---|---|
| SOLgrid | Connects homes and small businesses so they can trade electricity locally.[^solgrid] |
| SOLbox | Measures power flowing into and out of each connection.[^solgrid] |
| Grid connection | Gives a local network a shared link to the national grid.[^pcc] |
| SOLmobility | Adds monitoring and payment tools to vehicle batteries, including leasing.[^mobility] |

Co-founder Sebastian Groh moved to Dhaka in January 2014 to develop the idea.[^founder-interview] The early founding team also included Hannes Kirchhoff and Daniel Ciganovic.[^field-report] A first trial followed in 2015.[^pilot]

## Bangladesh had already paid for much of the starting point

Bangladesh's solar home systems programme began long before SOLshare. By 2018, it had sold more than 4.1 million systems, according to the World Bank. The programme brought together lenders, private suppliers and development organisations to help households buy and maintain them.[^solar-programme]

That matters to the business model. SOLshare did not have to persuade every first customer to buy a panel from scratch. There was already equipment on roofs, experience with solar power and a network of organisations serving those homes.

These national installations are the background to the opportunity. They are not SOLshare's customers.

Sebastian had worked in investment banking and later at MicroEnergy International, where he encountered both small-scale energy and microfinance, which provides small loans. During his research, he proposed connecting Bangladesh's home solar systems. He then moved to Dhaka to work on it.[^founder-interview]

The idea brought together two problems that are often studied separately: getting more use from equipment, and making that use affordable.

## The founders found a behaviour they could build around

In a 2016 interview, Sebastian said the team saw spare electricity going unused. It also saw neighbours negotiating access to one another's systems. People who could not afford their own equipment still wanted to buy some power.[^early-insight]

This was stronger evidence than a survey answer about liking renewable energy. There was a useful resource, a nearby need and an existing attempt to connect them.

The first opening was therefore narrow: make a small local trade work. A household did not have to buy an entire solar system just to buy some electricity.

But “spare power” does not mean free power. The equipment has an owner. The connection costs money. Someone must maintain it, measure use and settle the bill. Those costs determine whether sharing can become a service.

## What the box adds

A SOLbox is a meter that works in both directions. Connected homes can supply electricity or receive it through local wires. The company combines the meters with software for user records, payments and monitoring.[^solgrid]

GSMA describes the system as linking meters, field-agent tools and mobile money services.[^gsma] The physical and payment networks work together: a reading records the electricity used, while an account records what the user owes or earns.

That is the useful role of software here. It makes a real exchange easier to track. The electricity still travels through wires.

A household can also change roles. A seller with spare output at one time can need power later. The business must support both directions, rather than treating every user as a permanent producer or consumer.

The early model included a fee on trades, as described in IEEE Spectrum's 2018 reporting.[^field-report] A current standard fee schedule was not available in the sources reviewed, so that historical model should not be read as today's price.

## Trust meant letting someone near an expensive asset

The early adoption problem was unusually concrete. In the 2018 field report, Hannes said some villagers were reluctant to let outsiders access equipment they had spent years paying for. SOLshare worked with Grameen Shakti on marketing, installation and maintenance.[^field-report]

A homeowner could reasonably ask: will my family still have enough electricity, will the meter be fair, and who will fix the system?

Those questions help explain why a local service partner mattered. The company needed more than permission to install a device. It needed a credible relationship around the device after installation.

Sebastian's IIX interview also identifies high costs, unfamiliar technology and unclear business rules as obstacles.[^founder-interview] These are founder-reported challenges, not an independent ranking of the causes of slow adoption.

The practical lesson is to treat control and service as part of the offer. A technically sound network will struggle if joining it feels like surrendering something valuable.

The public pilot accounts establish that homes were connected. They do not reconstruct the first ten paying customers or what it cost to persuade each one.

## The decision: what happens when the grid arrives?

By 2021, the World Bank described Bangladesh as having near-universal electricity access.[^solar-programme] That changed the setting for a company built around homes outside the grid.

The team had considered this early. In the 2016 interview, Sebastian described a future in which grouped solar systems could connect to the national network.[^early-insight] The later work was therefore an old idea becoming practical, rather than a surprise pivot invented overnight.

In 2022, SOLshare and its partners launched a shared connection to the national grid.[^report-twenty-two] The company identifies the site as Saddam Bazar. Its “point of common coupling,” or PCC, links the local solar network to the Rural Electrification Board's network through one connection.[^pcc]

<CaseDetail title="A new buyer for the same solar power">

Before that connection, the local network needed a nearby user for spare electricity. The PCC added a route for selling it to the national grid.[^pcc]

The opportunity changed from supplying a place the grid had not reached to helping small generators take part in a larger system.

That is the central business decision: preserve what remains useful - the equipment, connections and operating knowledge - while changing the job it does.

It still needs workable technical and payment arrangements. One successful connection does not establish that every village can join on the same terms.

</CaseDetail>

## A grid connection did not solve every problem

The next constraint makes the story more revealing. SOLshare's 2024 report says outages prevented the Saddam Bazar community from selling spare power back to the national grid. The response was to add a local charging point for electric three-wheelers.[^report-twenty-four]

Having a potential buyer was not enough. That buyer's network also had to be available when the power was ready to sell.

The wider lesson is easy to miss: a market needs demand at the right time and place. Total demand for electricity can be high while a particular system still has nobody it can sell to that afternoon.

## A battery is useful long before its owner can afford it

Vehicle charging opened a related but different problem. A driver needs a working battery to earn money. Paying for it all at once can be harder than paying from daily income.

SOLshare's current mobility offer uses a small monitoring device attached to batteries. It records their condition, charging and use, and gives operators remote management tools. Those records can support financing through banks and microfinance institutions.[^mobility]

The roles are worth separating. A driver uses the battery. A charging-station operator manages equipment. A lender supplies money and expects repayment. A product that helps one of them must still work for the others.

| Participant | What a workable offer needs to establish |
|---|---|
| Driver | Total payments, useful driving time and what happens if the battery fails. |
| Charging operator | Equipment cost, charging capacity, maintenance and collection of payments. |
| Lender | Reliable records, expected repayments and what happens after a missed payment. |

This is our analysis of the responsibilities, not a summary of an undisclosed customer contract.

Monitoring can make an asset easier to finance. It cannot remove wear, late payments or the cost of replacement. Remote control also makes the rules for errors and missed payments important to the person who depends on the vehicle for income.

## The company had to learn how to sell the new offer

The move into batteries was not presented as effortless in Sebastian's November 2024 Climate Champions profile. The account says SOLshare initially lacked a clear plan for bringing SOLmobility to market. Reliable battery suppliers were another early challenge.[^mobility-interview]

The same profile reported 200 rickshaws converted to smart lithium-ion batteries. It described income gains reported by drivers, but did not publish a full comparison method or a representative long-term sample.[^mobility-interview]

That gives us evidence of deployment, with limits around the measured outcome. It does not justify multiplying a claimed income gain across Bangladesh's entire vehicle fleet.

The earlier Rajshahi pilot is also useful context. SOLshare's 2022 report describes working with BAT Bangladesh, Btomorrow Ventures and Rajshahi City Corporation on solar-equipped charging.[^report-twenty-two] A real site allowed the company to work on charging, batteries and the operator's business together.

The important distinction is between making a component work and making the whole service worth paying for. Both needed testing.

## Who pays for the work?

SOLshare's activities include energy trading, battery services and commercial rooftop solar.[^report-twenty-two] They do not all earn money in the same way or on the same schedule.

Its 2024 report states more than US$670,000 in revenue across all businesses that year.[^report-twenty-four] That company-reported total is neither the income earned by solar households nor the company's profit. The report does not give enough detail here to compare each product's long-term economics.

Capital and project support have played separate roles. GSMA's 2015 grant supported a trial.[^gsma] IIX later invested and helped with financial and impact assessment work.[^founder-interview]

In June 2024, a funding announcement reported US$2.2 million secured toward a US$4 million investment round led by SBK Tech Ventures. It also mentioned a €5.3 million EU Horizon grant for a consortium, meaning a group of organisations working together.[^funding]

The funding target was not the amount already secured. The consortium grant was not all SOLshare's money. These distinctions matter when judging what can pay for research, equipment and ongoing service.

For a founder in a similar field, there are two tests: does the service improve the customer's situation, and can its income cover delivery and upkeep? Passing one does not automatically pass the other.

## What founders can take from this

- **Look for a useful exchange people already attempt.** Informal power-sharing helped reveal both supply and demand.
- **Build on equipment and relationships already present.** Bangladesh's solar programme and local service organisations were part of the starting advantage.
- **Treat trust as a practical design problem.** People need to understand their balance, their control and their route to help.
- **Recheck the buyer when conditions change.** Grid access changed the role of a local network; outages then exposed another gap.
- **Test the payment model with the equipment.** A better battery matters only if users and operators can afford a workable arrangement.

Do not copy a national solar or vehicle count into a sales forecast. Do not assume a pilot proves long-term maintenance costs. And do not preserve a technology at the expense of the customer's changing needs.

## What remains uncertain

The sources do not establish current audited profit by business, repair costs over the full life of a connection, or standard battery lease terms. Much of the impact evidence comes from SOLshare and organisations that support it. The early IEEE field report documents working connections; it does not verify the company's later income or environmental claims.

SOLshare's clearest lesson is about finding a use for what already exists. A panel needs a buyer for its spare power. A battery needs an affordable way to reach its user. The invention is only part of that connection.
