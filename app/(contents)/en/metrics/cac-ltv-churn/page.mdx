# CAC, LTV, Retention, Churn, Payback

> **In short:** Before you spend more to win customers, work out what they leave after the cost of serving them, how much they cost to acquire, and when that cost comes back. Use the worksheet below to calculate CAC, LTV, payback and churn. Keep real results separate from guesses about future purchases; one good-looking ratio does not prove that the business makes a profit.

This page is for a business where customers buy again, such as subscription software or monthly coaching. It follows Sumaiya's fictional software business from the [Unit Economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics) guide. You do not need to read that page first: all the inputs are below. For a single sale, per-order costs may be enough; repeat buying also needs a view of the whole customer relationship.

## What is customer acquisition cost (CAC)?

**<Term name="cac">Customer acquisition cost (CAC)</Term>** is the average amount you spend to win one new paying customer. For a chosen period:

> **CAC = spending to acquire customers ÷ new paying customers acquired**

Include the sales and marketing costs used to win those customers: ads, content, sales tools, commissions, and the relevant share of team pay. Write down what you include. If you leave out the founder's unpaid time, show that separately rather than treating the work as free. This is different from ad spend alone.[^skok-metrics]

Say Sumaiya spends **10,000 taka** on acquisition in one month and wins **20 new paying customers**. Her CAC is **10,000 ÷ 20 = 500 taka**. This is an invented teaching example, not a market benchmark.

Count each new paying customer once. Repeat orders, app downloads and free sign-ups are not new paying customers. If none arrived, CAC cannot be calculated by division; record the spend and zero new customers rather than reporting a CAC of zero.

Match the time window and costs to the customers you count. A sale closed this month may come from work done months earlier. If that happens often, follow those leads over a longer period and label the timing gap. Do not present a single month's ratio as a precise cost for every customer.

## Getting LTV out of Sumaiya's numbers

In this example, a customer pays **500 taka a month**. After the direct costs of serving that customer, **100 taka** remains each month. That amount is **contribution**. It has not yet covered acquisition spending, fixed costs such as office rent, or tax. Include costs of keeping the customer, such as ongoing offers or support, in the service costs where they apply.

**<Term name="ltv">LTV (lifetime value)</Term>** here means the contribution a customer is expected to leave over the whole relationship, **before CAC**. Other reports may use revenue instead, so check the definition before comparing figures.

For a simple model with steady monthly contribution:

> **Estimated LTV = monthly contribution per customer × average paying lifetime in months**

Assume Sumaiya's customers stay **10 months** on average. Her estimated LTV is **100 × 10 = 1,000 taka**. Deduct CAC once: **1,000 − 500 = 500 taka** remains toward fixed costs and profit. That is not 500 taka of net profit.

The ten-month lifetime is an assumption in this example. In your own sheet, use what older customer groups have actually done, and mark any forecast clearly. Two months of sales do not prove ten months of lifetime. When prices, service costs or repeat buying change, use the amounts for each period instead of stretching one month's result across the whole future.[^skok-definitions]

## The LTV:CAC ratio, and three ways to move it

The **LTV:CAC ratio** is LTV divided by CAC. Sumaiya's **1,000 ÷ 500 = 2**, written **2:1**. The model leaves contribution after acquisition cost, but it does not show whether that covers the rest of the business.

You may hear **3:1** as a target. It is a rule of thumb for subscription software businesses, often called SaaS, not a pass-or-fail test for every startup. The source's guideline has its own margin and LTV assumptions. Check which costs are included before comparing it with your contribution-based figure.[^skok-definitions] A high ratio built on an untested lifetime can still be misleading.

There are three inputs to this simple model: contribution, lifetime and CAC. The table changes just one at a time, leaving the other two unchanged. These are possible calculations, not promised results:

| Change in the example | New LTV | New ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly contribution rises from 100 to 150 taka | 150 × 10 = 1,500 taka | 1,500 ÷ 500 = 3:1 |
| Average paying lifetime rises from 10 to 15 months | 100 × 15 = 1,500 taka | 1,500 ÷ 500 = 3:1 |
| CAC falls from 500 to 333 taka | Still 1,000 taka | 1,000 ÷ 333 ≈ 3:1 |

Each change improves the model if the other inputs hold. In practice, raising prices may affect retention, and a cheaper channel may bring different customers. Test one useful change, then recalculate all three inputs. Do not assume they improve together.

## What CAC payback actually tells you about cash risk

**CAC payback** is the time it takes for customer contribution to cover acquisition cost. With steady monthly contribution:

> **Estimated payback in months = CAC ÷ monthly contribution per customer**

For Sumaiya, **500 ÷ 100 = 5 months**. This estimate assumes the customer keeps paying and contribution stays at 100 taka. With zero or negative contribution, this model cannot recover a positive CAC. With changing contribution or customers leaving, follow the actual amounts over time.

Say Sumaiya pays **20 × 500 = 10,000 taka** upfront to acquire 20 customers. To show the timing, assume all 20 start together, stay for at least five months and pay on time. After their direct service costs are paid, they leave **20 × 100 = 2,000 taka** in cash each month. No new customers or other business costs are included in this table.

| Month | Contribution collected so far, taka | Amount still to recover, taka |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2,000 | 8,000 |
| 2 | 4,000 | 6,000 |
| 3 | 6,000 | 4,000 |
| 4 | 8,000 | 2,000 |
| 5 | 10,000 | 0 |

Sumaiya needs the 10,000 taka upfront; the unrecovered amount falls each month. The table does not say she needs the full 10,000 untouched for five months. Rent, salaries, tax, late payments and another round of acquisition can create further cash needs. Before growing, put those dates and amounts in a [cash calendar](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit). A contribution-based payback estimate is not a bank-balance forecast.

## How to calculate retention rate and churn rate

**<Term name="retention">Retention rate</Term>** is the share of a starting customer group that stays through a chosen period. **<Term name="churn">Customer churn rate</Term>** is the share of that same group that leaves. Decide what counts as staying: for example, keeping a paid subscription active. Use the same rule at both ends of the period.

For a separate fictional example, an online coaching platform starts a month with **100 paying students**. Of those same students, **85** still have an active subscription at the end and **15** have left.

- **Customer churn rate = 15 lost ÷ 100 at the start × 100 = 15%.**
- **Customer retention rate = 85 remaining ÷ 100 at the start × 100 = 85%.**

These two add up to 100% because they use the same group, period and definition. New students who joined during the month do not go into either count. Revenue churn and contract renewal rates use different measures or groups; do not mix them with this calculation.[^skok-definitions]

What happens if 15% of the remaining group leaves each month? With no new or returning students added, the model is **100 × 0.85 raised to the number of months**. The counts below are rounded to whole students:

| Month | Students remaining from the original group |
|---|---:|
| 0 | 100 |
| 1 | 85 |
| 2 | 72 |
| 3 | 61 |
| 4 | 52 |
| 5 | 44 |

By month five, the model has about 44 of the original 100 students left. It loses 15% of the remaining group each month, not 15 students every month. Your real rate may change. Track actual customer groups over time with the [Cohort Retention](/en/metrics/cohort-retention) guide; do not use this separate coaching example to infer Sumaiya's customer lifetime.

## What to try when customers leave

Start by asking why they left and looking at what happened before they stopped paying. Sumaiya could test clearer first-use help, faster support for a repeated problem, or a check-in to see whether the software solves the customer's task. Pick the action that matches the problem rather than offering a discount to everyone.

Future Startup's 2021 review of founder interviews highlighted product and service quality as a retention theme.[^future-startup-quality-retention] That is useful experience to learn from, not proof that one change will work for your business. Compare the next customer group's behavior and the extra cost of the change before deciding whether it helped.

Leave a result unavailable when its denominator is zero or its input is unknown. Do not quietly replace it with zero. For an uneven buying pattern, total the customer's contribution across actual purchases instead of assuming a monthly subscription. This simple worksheet does not account for the changing value of money over time.

## Checklist

- [ ] Counted new paying customers separately from repeat orders and free sign-ups
- [ ] Listed acquisition costs and any gap between spending and the sale
- [ ] Kept contribution, CAC and net profit separate, without counting a cost twice
- [ ] Marked customer lifetime as observed or assumed
- [ ] Checked actual payment dates as well as estimated payback
- [ ] Used the same starting group and period for retention and churn
- [ ] Chosen one change to test and a date to review it

Before increasing spend, make sure you can explain which numbers are measured, which are guesses, and how you will pay the bills while waiting for acquisition cost to come back.
