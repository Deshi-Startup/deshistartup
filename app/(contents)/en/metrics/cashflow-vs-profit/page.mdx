# Cashflow vs Profit: A Simple Explanation for Founders

> **In short:** Profit is what your accounting ledger says you made. Cash flow is what's actually moving in and out of your bank account. When the day a sale is booked and the day its money actually arrives don't match, a ledger can show profit while the bank shows nothing. Courier payouts on COD orders lag by days; B2B sales run on 30-60 day credit terms. Both create this same gap. So don't judge a month by the ledger; check the cash sitting in the bank, every week.

## Why profit and cash flow aren't the same thing

**Profit** is what's left on the ledger once costs are subtracted from a sale. **Cash flow** is the real money moving through your bank account or your hand. The two diverge for a simple reason: a sale gets booked the moment an order is placed or shipped. But the actual money from that sale can take days, sometimes months, to land in the bank. It's in that gap that businesses get into trouble.

For you this means: don't relax just because the ledger shows a profit at month's end. The real question is what's sitting in the bank right now, and what's due to leave next week.

## The first gap: COD orders and courier delay

Say Nasrin runs a cosmetics page on Facebook out of Sylhet, entirely cash-on-delivery (COD). On Monday she ships 30 orders worth 45,000 taka in total. The ledger books that 45,000 as revenue the same Monday. But the courier company only collects the cash from customers after delivery, and pays Nasrin out 5 to 7 days later, sometimes longer.

| Day | On the ledger | In the bank |
|---|---|---|
| Monday, orders shipped | 45,000 taka | 0 taka |
| The following Monday, courier pays out | 45,000 taka | 45,000 taka |

Now say a 20,000 taka supplier bill is due that Wednesday. Monday's sale sits on the ledger, but the money isn't in the bank yet; it's still with the courier. The ledger says the business is profitable, yet there's no cash in hand to pay the supplier. That's the cash flow gap: the sale happened, the profit is booked, but the money is still on its way.

## The second gap: B2B receivables

In B2B sales, this gap grows wider. Selling to a shop or an organisation on credit usually means offering 30 to 60 day payment terms. ShopUp's Mokam platform, for instance, lets grocery shops take stock on credit through its Baki feature, precisely because shop owners run short on cash even while selling steadily[^shopup-baki]. The fuller comparison of these two models is in [B2C vs B2B](/en/ideas/b2c-vs-b2b).

Sell this way, and the ledger books the full amount as revenue on the day of the sale. The actual money lands 30 or 60 days later. If rent, salaries, and supplier payments fall due before that cash arrives, a business that looks profitable on the ledger can still find itself unable to pay its bills.

## Build one lasting habit

You can't close the gap between profit and cash flow entirely; some credit or delay is part of running a business. But one habit manages the risk: on the same day every week, write down exactly how much cash is in the bank, whatever the sales ledger says. Alongside that, know your runway, how many months your cash in hand will last. The full method for working out runway is in the [Monthly Founder Dashboard](/en/metrics/founder-dashboard).

Keep in mind, how much profit one order or one customer actually leaves you is a separate question, covered in [Unit Economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics). This page's question is when that profit's cash actually lands in the bank.

## Checklist

- Write down the cash in the bank on the same day every week
- Know the gap between when a COD order is booked and when the courier pays out
- For B2B credit sales, know the credit term and when the cash actually lands
- Match supplier, rent, and salary payment dates against when cash actually arrives
- Know your runway, how many months your cash in hand will last

Rule of thumb: **Whatever the ledger says about profit, what matters before a bill is due is how much cash is actually in the bank.**
