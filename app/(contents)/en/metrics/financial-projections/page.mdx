# Bottom-Up Financial Projections: Revenue, Cost and Cash Runway

> **In short:** A projection does not predict the future. It writes down what you believe about a handful of numbers, in a way that lets you see within a month if you were wrong. Build it **bottom-up**, from customers you can count and prices you actually charge. Never top-down from a share of a market. The runway figure most founders quote is wrong in both directions, because dividing cash by this month's burn ignores that both sides keep moving. Your next step is the five-block worksheet at the end of this page, filled with your own numbers.

Say Sadia runs a six-person company selling scheduling software to tuition centres in Dhaka, at BDT 2,000 a month. Forty centres pay today. She has BDT 42 lakh in the bank.

Ask her how long that lasts and she divides 42 lakh by last month's <Term name="burn-rate">burn</Term> and says "about thirteen months". Once her growing revenue is in the model, the real answer is closer to seventeen. And the model tells her something far more useful than either number. On her current assumptions, she never reaches <Term name="break-even">break-even</Term> before the money runs out.

That is what a projection is for. Not to impress an investor. To find the sentence you did not want to hear, while there is still time to act on it.

## Who needs this, and when

- **You are raising, and someone asked for a three-year model.** This page builds it. Keep the valuation conversation separate. That is [valuation](/en/funding/valuation).
- **You are not raising, but you spend money every month.** This case matters more. A model nobody else ever sees is still the thing that tells you when to hire and when to stop.
- **You have revenue and no idea whether you are profitable.** Start with [cashflow vs profit](/en/metrics/cashflow-vs-profit), then come back.
- **You do not know what one sale earns you.** Start with [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics). This page assumes you know your contribution per customer.
- **You have no product and no customers.** A model would be fiction. Go to [demand without building](/en/validation/demand-without-building).

By the end you will have a monthly model in five blocks, three cases, a runway date, and a review you can run in twenty minutes a month.

## Bottom-up, and why top-down fails

There are two ways to project revenue, and only one of them is honest.

**Top-down** starts from a market. "The market is BDT 900 crore, we will take 0.5% in year two." It is fast, it sounds confident, and nothing in it can be checked next month.

**Bottom-up** starts from things you can count. How many customers you have, how many you add, how many leave, and what each one pays. Every line is a number somebody in the company is responsible for, and every one of them can be proved wrong within thirty days.

> **Rule:** If a line in your model cannot be checked against reality inside one month, it is not a projection. It is a wish with a decimal point.

Market sizing does have a real job. It tells you whether the opportunity is big enough to be worth years of your life. That job belongs to [market sizing](/en/ideas/market-sizing) and [TAM, SAM and SOM](/en/ideas/tam-sam-som). It does not belong in the revenue line of your model.

## The five blocks

Every bottom-up model has the same five blocks, in this order. Build them as five sections of one monthly spreadsheet, with months across the top.

### Block 1: revenue drivers

This is the only block where assumptions live. Everything after it is arithmetic.

A subscription business needs three drivers:

- **Customers at the start of the month**
- **New customers added**, worked out from your own funnel, not from ambition
- **Churn rate**, the percentage who leave each month

Sadia's drivers: 40 centres today, 8 new a month, 3% monthly churn. The 8 is not a hope. It comes from two salespeople closing four each, which she has measured. The 3% comes from her last six months.

```
Centres at month end = start - (start x churn) + new

Month 1:  40 - 1.2 + 8  =  46.8  ->  47
Month 2:  47 - 1.4 + 8  =  53.6  ->  54
Month 3:  54 - 1.6 + 8  =  60.4  ->  60
Month 6:  72 - 2.2 + 8  =  77.8  ->  78

Revenue month 6 = 78 x BDT 2,000 = BDT 1,56,000
```

If you sell products or transactions, your drivers are orders per month, average order value and repeat rate. If you sell B2B services, they are deals closed, average contract value and delivery capacity. The block changes shape. Its job does not.

### Block 2: cost of goods sold

The cost that moves with each customer. For software that means hosting, SMS, payment gateway fees and support tools. For physical products it means the product, the packaging and the delivery.

```
Sadia's COGS = BDT 180 per centre per month
Month 6 COGS = 78 x 180 = BDT 14,040

Contribution per centre = 2,000 - 180 = BDT 1,820
```

That BDT 1,820 is the number the whole model runs on. If you do not have its equivalent, stop and read [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics) first.

### Block 3: payroll

List every person and their gross monthly salary. Then add the line most Bangladeshi models forget.

```
2 engineers @ 70,000            140,000
1 designer  @ 55,000             55,000
2 sales     @ 35,000             70,000
1 founder-operations @ 40,000    40,000
                               --------
Monthly payroll             BDT 305,000

Festival bonus set aside:
one month's payroll per year = 305,000 / 12 = BDT 25,417 a month
```

**Set the festival bonus aside every month.** It is customary, it is often one or two months' salary a year, and treating it as a surprise in one month is how a company that looked fine in April cannot make payroll in May. Put a monthly amount aside and pay it when it falls due. Do the same with any statutory contributions your company owes, and check what applies to you rather than copying a template.

### Block 4: everything else

Fixed running costs that do not move with customer numbers.

```
Office rent                      45,000
Internet and utilities           12,000
Software subscriptions            8,000
Accounting and legal             10,000
Travel and miscellaneous          8,000
                               --------
Monthly operating cost      BDT  83,000
```

```
Total fixed cost per month
  = payroll 305,000 + bonus set-aside 25,417 + operating 83,000
  = BDT 413,417
```

### Block 5: cash and runway

Now the two numbers that decide things.

```
Monthly net burn = fixed cost - contribution

Month 1:  413,417 - (47 x 1,820)  =  413,417 -  85,540 = BDT 327,877
Month 6:  413,417 - (78 x 1,820)  =  413,417 - 141,960 = BDT 271,457
Month 12: 413,417 - (109 x 1,820) =  413,417 - 198,380 = BDT 215,037
```

Burn falls every month, because contribution rises. So the runway most founders quote is wrong.

```
Quick runway  = cash / this month's burn
              = 42,00,000 / 327,877  =  about 13 months

Modelled runway = the month your cash balance hits zero,
                  with revenue and costs both moving
                  =  about 17 months
```

| Month | Centres | Contribution | Net burn | Cash left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | 40 | — | — | 42,00,000 |
| 1 | 47 | 85,540 | 327,877 | 38,72,123 |
| 6 | 78 | 141,960 | 271,457 | 24,05,638 |
| 12 | 109 | 198,380 | 215,037 | 9,78,916 |
| 17 | 131 | 238,420 | 174,997 | 29,311 |

Figures in taka, using the base-case drivers above, with the customer count rounded to a whole number each month and carried forward. Rebuild it in a spreadsheet and you should get these same numbers. Watch the shape rather than the decimals. Cash falls fast early and slowly late, so a straight-line estimate is wrong at both ends.

**And here is the finding that matters.** Break-even needs contribution to cover fixed cost.

```
Break-even centres = 413,417 / 1,820 = 228 centres
```

Sadia reaches 109 by month 12 and 131 by month 17, when the cash runs out. **On these assumptions she never gets there.** Her model has done its job. In month one it has told her that adding eight centres a month does not end well, and that she has to raise money, raise the price, cut fixed cost, or find a faster way to get customers. For the last of those, see [first 100 customers](/en/customers/first-100-customers).

## Three cases, set honestly

One set of numbers is a guess. Three become a decision tool, but only if the cases differ in **drivers** and not in confidence.

| Case | New per month | Monthly churn | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Downside** | 5 | 5% | One salesperson leaves and is not replaced quickly |
| **Base** | 8 | 3% | The last six months carry on |
| **Upside** | 11 | 2% | The referral loop starts working |

Each case is a named story about the business, which is what makes it arguable. Run the same model on each set of drivers and Sadia's runway is about 13 months in the downside and about 17 in the base case. The upside is the only one that reaches 228 centres. It gets there in month 23, with 231 centres, before the cash runs out.

Two rules keep the cases honest.

- **Change drivers, not outcomes.** "Downside: revenue 30% lower" is a haircut, not a case. "Downside: five new a month because we lose a salesperson" is a case, because you can tell the day it starts happening.
- **Your base case is your last six months, not your best month.** If you cannot show six months, say so, and use the shortest honest history you have.

> **Warning:** Do not show the upside case as the plan and keep the downside in a private file. Investors read three cases as a sign that you understand your own business. They read one hockey stick as a sign that you do not. The [data room](/en/funding/data-room) is where this gets checked.

## Where AI helps, and where it invents your business

AI tools are really useful here. They also fail in one specific way that is easy to miss.

In a 2025 Accelerating Bangladesh session on financial projection and valuation using AI, Muhammad Arifur Rahman, National Program Coordinator at Accelerating Bangladesh and an independent economist and financial consultant, showed the audience how to prompt an AI tool for a startup forecast. It came back with a five-year customer projection climbing from a few hundred to a few thousand, and a five-year revenue forecast starting under a thousand dollars and rising steeply.[^ab-ai-projection]

Look at what happened. The tool was given a description of a business and produced growth rates. None of those rates came from the company's funnel, its churn, its sales capacity or its market. The AI supplied a **shape**, and the shape it supplies is the one that appears most often in its training data, which is up and to the right.

| Use AI for | Do not use AI for |
|---|---|
| Writing the spreadsheet formulas and checking them | Deciding how many customers you will add |
| Building the mechanics that switch between cases | Choosing your <Term name="churn">churn rate</Term> |
| Getting a plain-English explanation of a term you do not know | Deciding an accounting treatment. Ask your accountant |
| Checking arithmetic and finding circular references | Anything you will present as evidence |
| Turning your model into a readable summary | Filling a gap where you have no data |

The line is simple. **AI may touch anything downstream of your assumptions, and nothing upstream of them.** Every driver in block 1 has to come from something you counted, and you have to be able to say where it came from.

## Actual against plan: the only review that matters

A model written once and never opened again is decoration. The discipline is a monthly check, and it takes twenty minutes.

Each month, put your actual numbers next to the plan for four lines only.

| Line | Plan | Actual | Difference | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New customers | 8 | 5 | −3 | One salesperson on leave for two weeks |
| Churn | 3% | 4.1% | +1.1pp | Three centres closed for exam season |
| Contribution | 141,960 | 121,940 | −20,020 | Follows the two lines above |
| Cash at month end | 24,05,638 | 23,15,400 | −90,238 | Burn, plus a one-off legal fee |

Then answer one question in writing. **Is this noise, or is it the new normal?** One bad month on a driver is noise. Two months in the same direction means the driver has changed, and the model has to change with it.

Update the drivers, not the outcomes. If churn has really moved from 3% to 4%, change the churn cell. Do not adjust the revenue line to make the total look right. The whole value of a bottom-up model is that you always know which assumption moved. Your actual numbers come from your [founder dashboard](/en/metrics/founder-dashboard).

## The copy-ready worksheet

One tab. Months across the top, five blocks down the side.

```
BLOCK 1: DRIVERS            M1    M2    M3   ...
  Customers, opening        ....  ....  ....
  New customers added       ....  ....  ....
  Churn rate (%)            ....  ....  ....
  Customers lost            = opening x churn
  Customers, closing        = opening - lost + new
  Price per customer        ....
  REVENUE                   = closing x price

BLOCK 2: COGS
  Variable cost per customer ....
  TOTAL COGS                = closing x variable cost
  CONTRIBUTION              = revenue - COGS

BLOCK 3: PAYROLL
  (one row per person, gross monthly)
  Festival bonus set aside  = annual bonus / 12
  TOTAL PAYROLL

BLOCK 4: OPERATING COST
  Rent / utilities / software / professional / other
  TOTAL OPERATING COST

BLOCK 5: CASH
  FIXED COST                = payroll + operating
  NET BURN                  = fixed cost - contribution
  Cash, opening             ....
  Cash, closing             = opening - net burn
  BREAK-EVEN CUSTOMERS      = fixed cost / contribution per customer
```

Copy the tab three times, for downside, base and upside, and change only the driver rows. If you find yourself editing something outside block 1 to make a case work, it is not a case.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Top-down revenue from a share of a market | A number nobody can check, including you | Build from customers, price and churn |
| Cash divided by this month's burn | Runway wrong in both directions | Model the month your cash hits zero |
| Festival bonus treated as a surprise | A payroll month you cannot fund | Set money aside monthly, pay when due |
| Cases that differ by confidence, not drivers | Three versions of the same guess | Make each case a named story about the business |
| Adopting an AI-generated growth rate | A forecast that describes training data, not your company | Keep AI downstream of your assumptions |
| A model built once for a deck and never reopened | You learn you were wrong from your bank balance | Twenty-minute review, every month |
| Adjusting outcomes when actuals miss | You lose the ability to see which assumption moved | Update the driver cell, never the total |
| No break-even customer count | You cannot tell whether the plan ends anywhere good | Fixed cost divided by contribution per customer, on the front page |

The last row is the cheapest line in the model and the one most often missing. It turns a spreadsheet into an answer.

## Checklist

- [ ] Model is monthly, with months across the top, for at least 18 months
- [ ] Every revenue driver traces to something you counted, and you can say where
- [ ] Contribution per customer worked out and written down
- [ ] Every person on payroll listed at gross salary
- [ ] Festival bonus set aside monthly, and any statutory contributions checked
- [ ] Total fixed cost visible as one number
- [ ] Net burn worked out per month, not assumed flat
- [ ] Runway written as a month, not a ratio
- [ ] Break-even customer count on the front page
- [ ] Three cases that differ only in the driver rows
- [ ] Base case reflects the last six months, not the best month
- [ ] Monthly review scheduled, with the noise-or-new-normal question written down
- [ ] Nothing upstream of your assumptions produced by an AI tool

**You are done when:** you can name the month your cash hits zero under each of three cases, the number of customers that covers your fixed cost, and which single driver you would change first.
