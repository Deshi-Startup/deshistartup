# E-commerce Metrics

> **In short:** A rising monthly total doesn't tell you whether the business is moving forward. Selling direct through a Facebook page or your own site, track five numbers side by side every month: AOV (average order value), the conversion rate from reach or inquiries to confirmed orders, RTO% (the return rate), repeat purchase rate, and cost per delivered order. On Rahim's clothing page, booked sales climbed from 120,000 to 162,000 taka over three months, and it looked great. Over the same stretch, though, AOV fell, RTO rose, repeat customers thinned out, and the cash actually landing in his hand nearly stalled. Don't celebrate rising revenue alone; today, line up these five numbers from your last three months.

The [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics) page showed what one of Rahim's orders truly leaves him once returns are counted. But one order's maths can't tell you how the month went; what matters is which direction the numbers are moving. Orders were climbing on Rahim's page, so he was pleased, but without a few more numbers alongside it, the trouble stayed invisible.

## AOV: what's the average sale per order

AOV (average order value) is how much a single order is worth on average, in a given month. The maths is simple: AOV equals total sales divided by number of orders.

Why track it separately? A month's total sales can rise for two different reasons: more orders coming in, or each order being worth more. Those are not the same lever. Cut prices or push cheaper items to inflate the order count, and it looks like growth while the business is actually thinning out underneath.

## Conversion rate: from reach or inquiries to an order

For a website-run store, conversion rate means the share of visitors who buy. Most sellers in Bangladesh don't have that funnel at all. By e-CAB's count, roughly 50,000 Facebook pages sell products here, and the vast majority have no website, let alone anything that tracks "visit to purchase"[^ecab-fb-sellers]. For a page-seller like Rahim, the real funnel runs from a post or boost's reach, to how many people messaged the inbox or commented (an inquiry), to how many of those actually confirmed an order.

Conversion rate equals confirmed orders divided by inquiries, times 100. Say in Rahim's first month, 400 inquiries came in and 100 became orders, a 25% conversion rate. Three months on, with heavier boosting, inquiries reached 1,000 but only 180 became orders, down to 18%. More people were reaching the page, true, but a larger share had little real intent to buy, so the inquiry-to-order rate slid.

## RTO%: measuring the return rate properly

The unit economics page introduced RTO (return to origin), how one returned order can eat the profit of an entire batch. Here's how to actually measure RTO% month by month, and why this one number alone can flip the whole unit-economics picture.

RTO% equals returned orders divided by total orders shipped, times 100. Pull both numbers from the month's courier report and log them separately every month; "quite a lot come back" isn't a number you can act on.

Courier and packaging costs stay roughly fixed regardless of order price. So when AOV falls as RTO rises, contribution margin breaks down fast, that fixed cost now eats a bigger share of a smaller sale. Pathao's own blog, one of Bangladesh's major couriers, states that most sellers see a delivery success rate (DSR, the share of shipped orders delivered without issue) between 75% and 85%, with 80 to 85% considered standard, and anything under 75% signalling a real problem[^pathao-dsr-benchmark]. Flip that around and it puts RTO at 15% to 25% for most sellers; higher, and it's time to look hard at delivery area, product, or how orders get confirmed before shipping. Ways to bring RTO down are in the [Payments](/en/payments) section.

## Repeat purchase rate: how many old customers come back

Repeat purchase rate is the share of a month's (or a few months') customers who had already bought at least once before. The maths: orders from returning customers divided by total customer orders, times 100.

This matters more than a single month's AOV, because CAC (the cost of winning a customer, from the unit economics page) is paid only once. A returning customer's order needs no fresh ad spend, so nearly all its contribution is pure profit. A falling repeat rate means almost every order is bought with fresh advertising rather than earned from old customers, so the business stays dependent on ad spend even as it grows.

## Cost per delivered order

Add up the month's courier, packaging, and payment gateway or mobile-banking fees, then divide by orders actually delivered. The two-way courier cost of every returned order still belongs in that total, even though you divide by delivered orders alone. So when RTO% rises, this number rises with it, that's the direct link between RTO and delivery cost.

## Rahim's three months: sales rose, here's what the real picture said

Now put all five numbers together and see what actually happened on Rahim's page.

| Month | Orders (shipped) | AOV | RTO% | Repeat rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 1,200 | 15% | 12% |
| 2 | 140 | 1,050 | 20% | 9% |
| 3 | 180 | 900 | 25% | 6% |

Booked sales (orders times AOV) rose from 120,000 to 147,000 to 162,000 taka, climbing every month, so Rahim figured business was going well. But once RTO is stripped out, 85 orders were delivered in month one for 102,000 taka collected, 112 in month two for 117,600 taka, and 135 in month three for 121,500 taka. Real cash grew close to 15% in each of the first two steps, then slowed to barely 3% by the third, almost flat.

Cost per delivered order (courier plus packaging) climbed from 179 to 195 to 213 taka, as returned orders' two-way courier cost spread over more shipments. The repeat rate falling from 12% to 6% means almost every order is now bought on raw ad spend, not carried by old customers. Booked revenue kept climbing on the chart, exactly why Rahim was ready to push more into boosting, when these five numbers were actually saying: stop, find out why first.

## Build your own monthly tracking table

You don't need special software; copy the table above into your own sheet. At month's end, pull the numbers from the courier report and your order log into five columns: month, orders shipped, AOV, RTO%, and repeat rate. Three months in, you'll see at a glance which way each line is moving, no need to hold the numbers in your head.

## Checklist

- Logged the last three months' order count and AOV separately
- Worked out the conversion rate from inquiries or reach to confirmed orders
- Calculated monthly RTO% and compared it against the previous month
- Worked out repeat rate: how many customers had bought before
- Divided by delivered orders to get cost per delivered order
- Looked at real cash collected net of RTO, not just booked sales

Rule of thumb: **Don't celebrate rising booked sales on its own. When AOV, RTO%, and repeat rate all move the wrong way together, that's not growth, it's a warning sign.**
