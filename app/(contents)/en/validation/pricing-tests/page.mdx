# Pricing Tests: What to Charge in a Low-Trust Market

> **Key Takeaway:** A price is not found by asking, it is found by making an offer. Nobody can tell you what they would pay, but you can watch whether they send money once they hear a number. Two figures make the first number easier to set. Per Bangladesh Bank, an <Term name="mfs">MFS</Term> payment to a business averaged BDT 1,336 in May 2026[^bb-mfs-stats], while a card transaction at a shop counter averaged BDT 4,686.[^bb-card-stats] These averages are neither payment limits nor evidence of an individual’s habits. Larger payments may also use MFS, subject to the provider’s applicable limits. And in a low-trust market, cutting the price does not reduce suspicion — sometimes it raises it. Suspicion falls with a small first step, a written refund rule, and showing your face.

## "How Much Would You Pay" Does Not Work

People cannot forecast their own future behaviour, so a question about price gets one of two answers. Either they inflate it out of courtesy, or they lowball it out of bargaining habit. Both are useless.

Get at past spending instead. What does getting this job done cost them now, who does it go to, and exactly how much went out last month. That number gives context for testing your price, not a ceiling, because it is a record of spending rather than an act of imagination. The questions are laid out on the [validation interview](/en/validation/customer-interviews) page.

After that the work is the same. Name a number and see whether the money arrives. There is no substitute for it.

## What Bangladeshis Are Used to Paying Digitally

Before setting a price, find out how much money people usually move digitally. Bangladesh Bank's figures for May 2026:

| How the money was paid | Transactions | Average amount |
|---|---|---|
| MFS payment to a business | 4.71 crore | BDT 1,336 |
| Card, online | 66.34 lakh | BDT 3,719 |
| Card, at a shop counter | 89.6 lakh | BDT 4,686 |

Two practical points come out of these numbers.

First, the MFS figure is a national average across merchant transactions, not a weekly habit of every customer.[^bb-mfs-stats] Check payment access and preferences in your own segment.

Second, confirm the payment route at the proposed price. MFS, cards and bank transfers have different limits and customer requirements; a higher price does not automatically require a card. See [payment access and card use](/en/payments/low-card-penetration).

Do not read this as a rule. Course fees, electronics, furniture, and B2B invoices run to larger amounts anyway, and cards or bank transfers are normal there. These numbers are not a ceiling, they tell you where to put your first guess.

## Where to Set the First Price

Take three numbers from three places, then set the highest one you can defend.

- **What it costs them now.** Counting staff, travel, fees, and extra hours.
- **What a mistake costs.** How much the last incident took off them.
- **A competitor's published price.** If there is one, and if not, the price of the nearest alternative.

The reason to lean high is simple. Coming down is easy, going up is hard. Start low and your first customers settle into that number, and six months later they are the ones who object most when you raise it.

Say you are offering monthly bookkeeping to small shops. The interviews turn up that shopkeepers call in a man two days a week for this, at BDT 2,500 a month. Compare the scope and value of your offer before choosing test prices. You quote three groups three prices — BDT 1,500 to eight of them, BDT 2,500 to eight, BDT 3,500 to eight. Five agree at 1,500, four at 2,500, one at 3,500. If those agreements become paid purchases, the observed revenues are Tk 7,500, Tk 10,000 and Tk 3,500. Tk 2,500 leads in this small sample, before costs; it is a candidate to retest, not a proven optimum.

Two cautions. Do not quote two prices to the same person, because they will hold on to the lower one and your test is spoiled. And do not say the price in an apologetic tone, because tone of voice settles half the answer.

## Four Tests

| Test | How to run it | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Different prices to different groups | Three groups, three numbers, one offer | Which price brings the most revenue |
| Three-tier packages | Low, middle, high, side by side | Which tier people settle on |
| Discount for paying up front | A six-month package beside the monthly one | Who will part with money now |
| Raising the pilot fee | Nudge the fee up with each new company | Where they start to hesitate |

Three tiers can help customers compare genuinely different service levels. Do not assume most will choose the middle tier: record actual choices, delivery costs and cancellations.

For example, quoting pilots at Tk 30,000, Tk 40,000 and Tk 50,000 to different companies can generate leads for further testing. Their needs and budgets may differ, so the first hesitation does not locate a universal price ceiling. Compare scope and buyer characteristics alongside the price.

## When Cutting the Price Is the Wrong Answer

When a customer says the price is high, ask a follow-up before changing it. Affordability, value and trust are different constraints. The table lists possible interpretations to check, not facts about what the customer secretly means.

| What they said | Possible explanation to check | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The price is too high | I don't know you, and I'm afraid of losing the money | A small first step and a written refund rule |
| Let me think about it | The decision is not mine alone | Find out who decides |
| No budget right now | The problem is not this month's priority | Show them the cost of the loss, or step back |
| I'd try it if it were free | The person may need a trial, have no budget, or see little value | Ask why and test the relevant constraint |

There is a reverse risk in a low-trust market too. A very low price can make people assume the service will not last, or that the work will be sloppy. So a trust problem has to be treated with trust. Keep the first month's amount small, put the refund rule in writing, keep your name, face, and number in front, show what earlier customers said, and deliver by the promised date.

And leave no obstacle in the payment route. The Economic Census 2024 shows that of the units using online systems, 50.89% transact through mobile banking and 29.81% through online banking.[^bbs-economic-census-2024] Someone who has agreed should not get stuck trying to pay you.

## Before You Discount

Discounts are not bad, but take something in exchange for one. Give it away and the customer learns the real price is lower still.

- A discount for paying up front, such as one month free on a six-month package.
- A discount for permission to use their name or an honest testimonial, never conditional on praise. Disclose any incentive wherever the testimonial appears.
- A discount for being among the first few customers, tied to a date.

The last one has a single condition: when the date passes, the price really has to go up. If it does not, nobody will believe you the next time.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Asking "how much could you pay" | The answer is courtesy or bargaining, and no use either way |
| Quoting two prices to one person | They hold the lower one and the test is spoiled |
| Starting low | Raising it later runs into your first customers |
| Discounting on hearing "too expensive" | A trust problem gets the wrong medicine |
| Pricing very low | The service reads as weak or temporary |
| Discounting for nothing in return | The customer learns the real price is lower |
| Setting a price without planning collection | A willing customer cannot pay you |

## Checklist

- [ ] Know the number this job currently costs the customer
- [ ] Set the first price from all three bases
- [ ] Quoted different prices to different groups, never to one person
- [ ] With three tiers, what I want to sell sits in the middle
- [ ] Kept the first month's amount small
- [ ] Gave the refund rule in writing
- [ ] The bKash or Nagad route is open
- [ ] Recorded stated reasons and labelled unverified interpretations
- [ ] Took something in exchange for any discount

Rule of thumb: **Do not ask for a price, offer one. And when you hear "too expensive", ask whether the constraint is affordability, value, trust or something else.**
