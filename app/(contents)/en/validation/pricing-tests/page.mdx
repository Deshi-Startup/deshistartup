# Pricing Tests: What to Charge in a Low-Trust Market

> **Key Takeaway:** Start with what the customer spends now, the value of your offer and your own costs. Then make a clear offer and record who actually pays. A survey answer can help you choose a price to test; it does not prove someone will buy. Compare paid sales and the cost of serving those customers before changing the price. When someone says “too expensive”, ask what is getting in the way.

## Ask About Spending, Then Test an Offer

“How much would you pay?” can start a useful conversation. But an answer may reflect a rough guess, courtesy or bargaining. Keep it separate from an order or a payment.

Ask about past spending too. What does getting this job done cost the customer now? Who do they pay, and how much went out last month? Include staff time, travel and fees where they matter. That gives context for testing your price, not a ceiling. The questions are laid out on the [validation interview](/en/validation/customer-interviews) page.

After that, offer a clear price for a clear job. Record whether the person agrees, pays, uses the service and stays. These are different steps. For a longer business sale, also record who approves the purchase and when a payment decision is due.

## Check How Your Customer Can Pay

National payment averages cannot tell you what your customer will pay for your product. Check the payment method with the people you want to serve.

At the proposed price, can the buyer pay through your chosen method? Do they need an invoice, a bank transfer or approval from someone else? <Term name="mfs">MFS</Term>, cards and bank transfers have different limits and requirements. A higher price does not automatically require a card. See [payment access and card use](/en/payments/low-card-penetration).

Record a payment problem separately from a price objection. Someone who agrees to pay but gets stuck at checkout has not necessarily rejected your price.

## Where to Set the First Price

Use these three clues to choose a starting range:

- **What it costs the customer now.** Include staff, travel, fees and extra hours.
- **What a mistake costs.** Ask about a real past incident, rather than assuming every customer faces the same loss.
- **A competitor's published price.** Compare the scope, support and payment terms, not just the number.

Then check your own costs. Price, the value a buyer sees and the cost of delivering the service are different things.[^yc-pricing] There is no rule that every startup should start high or low. Choose prices you can explain, with clear limits on any loss you accept for a test.

Here is a fictional example. Say you are offering monthly bookkeeping to small shops. In your interviews, shopkeepers describe paying someone BDT 2,500 a month for the work. Compare the scope and value of your offer before choosing test prices. You quote three groups three prices: BDT 1,500 to eight people, BDT 2,500 to eight, and BDT 3,500 to eight. Five agree at 1,500, four at 2,500, and one at 3,500.

If all those agreements become paid purchases, revenue is Tk 7,500, Tk 10,000 and Tk 3,500. Tk 2,500 leads in this small sample, before costs. It is a candidate to test again, not a proven best price. Compare refunds, cancellations and service costs too; more revenue does not always leave more money in the business.

Keep the offer, sales channel and timing as similar as you can across groups. If the buyers or work differ, record that. Price may not explain the result. Explain each offer in the same clear way. If someone sees more than one price, record which they saw and why it changed; do not treat that response as a clean comparison with someone who saw only one.

## Four Tests

A pilot is a small trial of the work before a larger commitment.

| Test | How to run it | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| Different prices to different groups | Offer the same work at different prices to similar groups over the same period | Offers made, paid purchases, revenue, service costs and cancellations for each group |
| Three-tier packages | Put three genuinely different service levels side by side | Which people choose, what each tier costs to deliver and whether they stay |
| Discount for paying up front | Offer a six-month package beside the monthly one, with clear terms | Who pays now, the discount cost and the service or refunds you may still owe |
| Raising the pilot fee | Try a higher fee with a new company for a similar scope of work | Paid pilots, objections, buyer needs and scope differences |

Use the result to choose your next test, rather than treating a handful of replies as a final price. Looking at paid sales and revenue together helps you see the trade-off.[^yc-pricing] Service costs, refunds and renewals tell you whether that price keeps working.

Three tiers can help customers compare service levels. Do not assume most will choose the middle tier. Record their actual choices.

For example, quoting pilots at Tk 30,000, Tk 40,000 and Tk 50,000 to different companies can give you prices to test further. Their needs and budgets may differ, so the first hesitation does not locate a universal price ceiling. Compare scope and buyer needs alongside the price.

## When Cutting the Price Is the Wrong Answer

When a customer says the price is high, ask a follow-up before changing it. Affordability, value and trust are different constraints. The table lists possible explanations to check, not facts about what the customer secretly means.

| What they said | Possible explanation to check | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The price is too high | They cannot afford it, see too little value, or worry about losing the money | Ask which part is difficult; consider a smaller scope or first step where useful |
| Let me think about it | The decision may involve someone else | Ask who decides and what they need to know |
| No budget right now | The problem may not be this month's priority | Ask about timing and the cost of waiting, or step back |
| I'd try it if it were free | They may need a trial, have no budget, or see little value | Ask why and test the relevant concern |

If trust is the problem, a lower price alone may not solve it. Consider a small paid first step, clear contact details, a written refund rule and proof of work you have permission to share. State what you will deliver and when. These make the offer easier to assess; they do not guarantee a sale.

Keep the payment route usable too. A willing buyer should know how to pay, what they will receive and who to contact if something goes wrong.

## Before You Discount

Write down why you are offering a discount and what it costs. A discount can make sense, but check whether the lower price still covers the work and whether you can meet the terms.

- For upfront payment, you might offer one month free on a six-month package. Work out the cost of all six months of service and any refund obligations first.
- If you offer a discount for permission to use a customer's name or an honest testimonial, never make it conditional on praise. Disclose the incentive wherever the testimonial appears.
- An early-customer offer can have a genuine end date. Make its price, scope and renewal terms clear.

Honour the terms you promised. Do not invent a deadline to pressure someone. If you change a later offer, explain the change rather than pretending an earlier price was never available.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Treating a survey answer as a paid order | Interest does not tell you whether payment will follow |
| Changing the price, offer and sales channel together | You cannot tell which change affected the response |
| Pricing below cost without a test budget | Each sale can add a loss you have not planned for |
| Discounting as soon as someone says “expensive” | You may miss a problem with affordability, value, trust or timing |
| Choosing the price with the most revenue alone | Service costs, refunds or cancellations may change the result |
| Discounting without checking future work | Upfront cash can hide service costs you still have to pay |
| Setting a price without planning collection | A willing customer may be unable to pay you |

## Checklist

- [ ] I know what the customer spends now and what my offer costs to deliver
- [ ] I chose test prices I can explain and set a limit on any test losses
- [ ] I kept the offer and groups comparable, and recorded important differences
- [ ] I recorded paid purchases, costs and cancellations, not just “yes” replies
- [ ] With three tiers, I recorded actual choices instead of assuming the middle will win
- [ ] I made the scope, payment and refund terms clear
- [ ] I checked that the buyer can use the payment method
- [ ] I kept stated objections separate from my guesses about them
- [ ] I checked the cost and future commitments of any discount

Rule of thumb: **Use interviews to choose a sensible test. Use paid responses and costs to judge it. When you hear “too expensive”, ask what is getting in the way.**
