# Product-Market Fit: What the Customer Evidence Tells You

> **In short:** Look for a clear group of customers who get useful results and keep choosing your product when they need it. Check actual use, payment and the work needed to serve them. No single percentage proves product-market fit or makes expansion safe.

Use this after people have tried a working product or service. You will leave with a short evidence sheet and one next decision: fix a problem, test again with a clearer group, or try a small expansion. If nobody has tried the offer yet, start with [testing demand](/en/validation/demand-without-building).

<Term name="pmf">Product-market fit</Term>, often shortened to **PMF**, means a product meets a meaningful need in a market. For an early startup, make that idea concrete: which customers choose it, what do they get done, and what do they do when the need returns? Michael Seibel's advice at Y Combinator puts customer demand ahead of funding or team size and warns about expanding before that demand is there.[^yc-pmf]

## Choose the customer group and useful action

Write down one group, one problem and one action that shows a customer received value. “All small businesses” is too broad for a useful first check. “Small distributors who prepare a weekly stock order” gives you a task to observe. The [customer-group worksheet](/en/validation/consumer-personas) helps you make that choice.

For that software, opening the app is weaker evidence than completing a real stock order. Record who uses it, who decides and who pays. A staff member enjoying a free trial does not show that the owner will approve a paid renewal.

Choose a review interval that fits the task. A weekly workflow needs time for several weekly uses; a monthly subscription needs time for the renewal to fall due. A seasonal service cannot be judged by daily return visits. For a one-off purchase, look at successful completion, payment, refunds and demand from comparable new buyers. Referrals can add evidence, but not every useful product gets frequent referrals.

## Keep the signals separate

Use the table as a set of questions, not a scorecard. The observations can disagree.

| Signal | Record | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Customer gets the job done | The completed task and date | That they will need or pay for it again |
| Customer comes back | The same customer's later useful action | That every customer group behaves this way |
| Customer pays or renews | Amount received, terms and any refund | That serving them makes money |
| Customer recommends it | An actual introduction or referred buyer | That each new customer will refer others |
| Team can serve the demand | Time, support, delivery and other costs | That the process will work at a much larger size |

Downloads, compliments and a waiting list can help you find people to learn from. They do not replace these records. Keep friends, free users and deeply discounted offers visible as separate groups so you can see what the result depends on.

Sequoia's PMF framework looks at how customers relate to a problem. Its authors explicitly say the framework describes possible paths rather than diagnosing whether a company has PMF.[^sequoia-pmf] Do not turn a framework, survey answer or checklist total into permission to expand.

## Worked example: use and renewal tell different stories

Imagine a Dhaka startup offering monthly stock-ordering software to small distributors. This is a fictional teaching example. Ten businesses start the same paid first-month plan together. All ten have reached the end of that month, and the team has checked their records.

| Evidence from those ten businesses | Result |
|---|---|
| Completed a real stock order in week four | 6 of 10, or 60% |
| Paid for month two by the agreed due date | 4 of 10, or 40% |
| Joined last week in a separate group | 10 more; month-two renewal is not due yet |

The team has evidence of continued use and some paid demand. It has not proved PMF. The six week-four users and four renewing businesses measure different things; check the records to see who belongs to both. Do not describe the 60% use rate as a 60% renewal rate.

Do not add the ten new businesses to the total used to calculate the renewal rate. They have not had the same chance to renew. Ten businesses starting together form a **cohort**, a group you follow from the same starting point. Compare groups at the same age, and keep offer terms visible. The [cohort-retention guide](/en/metrics/cohort-retention) explains the calculation in more detail.

These small counts describe this example, not a Bangladesh benchmark or a market-wide rate. If a record is missing, mark it unknown and report that gap. Do not silently treat it as confirmed use or confirmed non-use. A late payment is a payment result; it does not, by itself, explain why someone stopped using the product.

The next step is to speak with businesses that renewed, used without renewing, and stopped. Ask about the last real task, the alternative they used and what happened at payment time. Use the [interview guide](/en/validation/customer-interviews) to understand the events without leading people toward your preferred answer.

## Check what the team is doing behind the scenes

Record the help each customer needs. A founder quietly doing every stock order may be testing a useful managed service, but it does not yet show that customers can use the software themselves. Keep that difference explicit.

Count support time, delivery costs, refunds and discounts alongside payment. Paid demand can exist while costs are too high. Use [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics) to work through the money. Money left over from serving each customer does not prove a large market or guarantee that the next group will behave like the first.

You can start the evidence sheet in a spreadsheet using records you already have. Allow time to check missing records and speak with customers; no paid analytics tool is required for this first review. The observation period depends on when customers can genuinely use or buy again. Set a spending limit for any follow-up test.

## Copy the evidence sheet and choose the next test

This is a suggested review method, not a published PMF test. Link records instead of placing private customer details in a widely shared document.

```text
Review date / owner:
Customer group and task:
Who uses, decides and pays:
Offer, price and help provided:
Useful action / expected repeat interval:
Group start date / number who started:
Who has had enough time for this review:
Useful repeat actions / records checked:
Payments, renewals and refunds / records checked:
Missing or uncertain records:
Reasons people returned or stopped / source and date:
Support time and other costs:
Biggest unanswered question:
Next test / owner / end date / spending limit:
What result would make us proceed, change or stop:
```

Choose the next action from the gap you found:

- **People cannot complete the task:** watch where they get stuck and test a specific fix.
- **People use it but do not pay:** check who controls the budget, the offer and the payment problem before assuming a lower price will solve it.
- **Only one group returns:** focus the next test on that group. Keep the conclusion limited to it.
- **Use and paid demand persist across comparable groups and cycles:** try a small expansion with limits on spending, support load and losses. Continue checking the same evidence.
- **The result stays weak after a fair test:** reconsider the problem, group or offer before adding more features.

Write the decision rule before the next result arrives. The [experiment planner](/en/validation/mvp-experiment-planner) helps you set it. These are working decisions under uncertainty, not a promise that one more test will prove the whole business.

Before increasing spending, have a teammate use the sheet to explain the customer group, the strongest evidence and the biggest remaining doubt. If those answers are unclear, fix the record or run a smaller test first.
