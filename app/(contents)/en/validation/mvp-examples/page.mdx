# What the First Version Looks Like: Seven MVP Shapes

> **Key Takeaway:** Check the devices and payment methods your target customers actually use before building. BTRC counts 12.08 crore mobile internet connections in June 2026 versus 1.51 crore ISP/PSTN connections; connections are not unique people or devices.[^btrc-internet-subscribers] The Economic Census reports computer or IT use in 2.41% of industry-sector units, not all shops and offices.[^bbs-economic-census-2024] Start with the smallest format that can test your assumption, using the seven examples below as options.

## An MVP Is Not a Small Product

The most familiar mistake is building a feeble version of the whole product. Three features instead of ten, all of them half-done. The customer gets nothing out of it, and neither do you.

An MVP has one job, answering a single question, and that question is your biggest risk. Which means an MVP can be one single job done properly. The rest can stay a blank page.

The difference is easy to see. An app with ten half-features cannot tell you which one the customer wants. Do one job well and you learn whether they come back for it, and that is the real news.

## Seven Shapes

| Shape | What you build | Which risk it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Done by hand | No software, you do the job yourself | Whether the job is needed, and how often |
| One feature | One job of the whole product, done well | Whether people return for that job |
| Facebook page and phone | Products on a page, orders by message or call | Demand and price |
| Google Sheet and reports | Customer data in a sheet, regular reports out | Whether the report itself is the value |
| WhatsApp group | The customer group in one group, service by message | Whether people use it regularly |
| Brokering | Matching two sides by hand | Which side is hard to find |
| A stall or counter | A seven-day stall at a fair, campus, or market | Whether people stay once they hear the price |

With manual fulfilment, people perform the work; a one-feature product automates one job. Be truthful about what the service does and who can access customer data. Do not claim automated or private processing while secretly exposing data to manual reviewers.

The last one earns its place for online businesses too. A seven-day stall shows you things a website cannot catch. Which sentence makes people stop, which question everybody asks, and what their face does when they hear the price.

## Build for What the Customer Already Has

The most expensive decision in a first version is not about features, it is about assumptions. What will the customer run your thing on?

Use these figures as context, not a substitute for observing your segment. June 2026 has 12.08 crore mobile internet connections in the country against 1.51 crore broadband-type connections.[^btrc-internet-subscribers] On the business side it is starker still. The Economic Census 2024 counts 11,68,349 units in the industry sector, of which 28,151 use a computer or IT, or 2.41%.[^bbs-economic-census-2024]

The industry-only statistic does not establish device access across every business, and a shared broadband connection can serve several people. Check these design choices with your customers:

- **Build for the small screen.** Start with a dashboard that looks good on a desktop and the customer will never open it.
- **Web or messaging before an app.** An app needs storage and willingness, and it puts an obstacle in place on day one.
- **Keep typing to a minimum.** Bangla on a phone keyboard is slow, so get the job done with choices and buttons.
- **Test report formats.** Excel files can open in mobile apps, but a large workbook may be awkward. Offer a PDF, image or message if that better fits the task.
- **Take money over MFS.** The Economic Census shows 50.89% of units using online systems transact through mobile banking.[^bbs-economic-census-2024] A simple test may not need a gateway, but payment integration may itself be part of what you must test; and the comparison is on [bKash, Nagad, and payment gateways](/en/payments/mfs-and-gateways).

## Tools and Costs

A first version almost never requires buying a tool.

| Job | What to use | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Taking orders or applications | Google Forms, Messenger, WhatsApp | Free |
| Storing data and calculating | Google Sheets | Free |
| Notifying customers | WhatsApp, phone, SMS | Low |
| A one-page offer | A plain web page or a Facebook post | Small, if you have a domain |
| Taking money | A bKash or Nagad merchant number | A rate per transaction |
| Sending reports | An image or PDF out of the sheet | Free |

The money side is secondary here. The real cost of a first version is your time, and the thing that cuts it most is writing less code.

## Where Each One Breaks

Capacity depends on the task and team. These are warning signs to measure, not universal customer or order limits. Breaking is not failure, it is the signal that it is time for the next step.

| Shape | Where it jams |
|---|---|
| Done by hand | Time per customer exceeds the hours you have available |
| Facebook page and phone | Untracked messages lead to missed orders; measure this before expanding |
| Google Sheet | Errors creep in once several people write at once |
| WhatsApp group | As members grow, the important messages disappear |
| Brokering | Matching stops being possible as both sides grow |
| Stall | When the stall comes down, so do the sales |

There is a simple rule for deciding when to move to the next step. Whichever task eats most of your time when done by hand is the one to put into software first. Decide by the clock, not by the feature list.

## An Outline of the First Week

Say you are thinking about supplying raw ingredients to small restaurants. There is one big risk: whether a restaurant owner will leave a known supplier for someone unfamiliar. No code is needed for that.

On Monday you open a WhatsApp group and add six restaurants. Every night you post the next day's prices in the group. Anyone who wants something orders by message, and in the morning you buy at the kitchen market and deliver. The accounts sit in a Google Sheet, the money comes over bKash.

What you have after seven days is not available from any survey. Who ordered and who did not, who ordered twice, which item drew bargaining over price, which delivery slot they need, and how many days it took to get paid. If software has to be built after that, you will know what to build first.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Building half of the whole product | Ten weak features and not one answer |
| Building for the desktop | May exclude phone-first customers |
| Asking for an app install on day one | Most people drop out at the download step |
| Adding an unnecessary gateway before testing the core assumption | Setup money and setup time both go early |
| Sending reports as Excel files | A format untested on the customer’s device can block the task |
| Being embarrassed to do the work by hand | You give up your best chance to learn |
| Staying manual after the breaking signal | Customers start dropping and the reason stays unknown |

## Checklist

- [ ] The biggest risk is written in one line
- [ ] Picked the shape among the seven that matches that risk
- [ ] Built for the small screen
- [ ] Checked whether an app install is necessary for this test
- [ ] bKash or Nagad is available as a payment route
- [ ] Reports go out in a form that opens on a phone
- [ ] Measuring which task eats most of my time
- [ ] Know where this shape will break

Rule of thumb: **In a first version, do by hand whatever can be done by hand. Write code for the one task that is eating the most of your clock.**
