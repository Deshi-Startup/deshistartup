# Validating Outside Dhaka: Where Six-Sevenths of the Country Is

> **Key Takeaway:** Finish your validation sitting in Dhaka and you have looked at a small slice of the country. Per the Economic Census 2024, 27.08% of the country's 1,17,02,792 economic units are in Dhaka division, and only 13.47% of the national total sits in Dhaka division's urban areas. So even covering every town in Dhaka division perfectly gets you one economic unit in seven.[^bbs-economic-census-2024] Going outside is not charity, it is where the units are. The work is harder for two reasons: doors do not open without an introduction, and the same problem has different economics in a district town. Both have answers, and a two-day trip is enough to start.

## Where the Numbers Are

The country's economic units by division, per the 2024 census:

| Division | Units | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| Dhaka | 31,69,284 | 27.08% |
| Chattogram | 20,48,840 | 17.51% |
| Rajshahi | 16,80,717 | 14.36% |
| Khulna | 14,89,445 | 12.73% |
| Rangpur | 13,34,966 | 11.41% |
| Mymensingh | 7,75,946 | 6.63% |
| Barishal | 6,56,944 | 5.61% |
| Sylhet | 5,46,650 | 4.67% |

Two things catch the eye. Chattogram comes straight after Dhaka, with more than 20 lakh units. And Rajshahi, Khulna, and Rangpur together make 38.50%, which is more than Dhaka division.

## Dhaka Division and Dhaka City Are Not the Same

This mix-up produces a large error. Dhaka division contains Gazipur, Narayanganj, Tangail, Kishoreganj, Manikganj, and many other districts. So the 27.08% figure does not belong to Dhaka city.

The census split makes it plainer still. Of Dhaka division's 31,69,284 units, 15,93,378 are rural and 15,75,906 urban. That urban portion is 13.47% of the national total.[^bbs-economic-census-2024]

With the number in hand the question changes. Say "I validated in Dhaka" and what it means is that you have effectively seen a seventh of the market and are guessing about the rest. If someone in the room catches that gap, it is hard to answer.

## Which Town to Visit

You do not have to go everywhere; picking two places gets you started. Balance four things when choosing.

- **Unit count.** Pick a division off the table above, then a large district town within it.
- **Your own reach.** Where you have a relative, a friend, or a former colleague, the first door of introduction already exists.
- **One difference.** Do not let the two towns be alike. Take one large divisional city and one smaller district town.
- **Ease of travel.** Somewhere you can reach on an overnight bus or train keeps the trip to two days.

Do not pick the hardest, most distant place for a first trip. Go to a nearby town, get the method working, then go far.

## Doors Do Not Open Without an Introduction

In Dhaka, a stranger walking into a shop and asking questions at least gets a conversation going. In a district town the arithmetic is different, because business runs on acquaintance there, and an unfamiliar face means caution. That is not an obstacle, it is how the system works.

So find a thread of introduction first.

- **A trade association.** Pharmacy, cloth, electronics, food — nearly every line has an association in the district. Sit with the general secretary or president first.
- **The market committee.** To get into a particular market, speak to someone on the committee.
- **One shopkeeper you know.** A good conversation with one and he will walk you to the shop next door himself, and that single introduction eases the rest of the day.
- **A local companion.** With a college student, or someone's younger brother you know, along, you stop being "the man from Dhaka".

The time arithmetic differs too. The first half hour often goes on tea and general talk, and that is not wasted time, it is the price of entry.

## The Language Arithmetic

Two separate jobs: understanding and speaking. Understanding the regional dialect matters, because the numbers and the conditions will arrive in it. But do not attempt to speak in the local accent yourself, because it usually sounds fake and widens the distance.

A few working rules:

- Speak standard Bangla, but slowly and in short sentences.
- Confirm amounts and units of measure as soon as you hear them, because figures may come in mon, kilos, sacks, dozens, or halis.
- If you do not follow something, do not pretend, ask.
- Get permission to record where you can, so you can check the numbers later.

## The Same Problem, Different Economics

The problem may be the same, but the figures change in a district town, and that is this trip's most valuable output.

| What changes | In Dhaka | In a district town |
|---|---|---|
| Price expectation | Comparatively higher | Lower, and bargaining is normal |
| Who the competitor is | A company or an app | A known individual or a broker |
| How money arrives | Card, MFS, bank | MFS and cash, and late |
| Delivery time | Same day is possible | You run on the courier's schedule |
| Cost of labour | Higher | Lower, so doing it by hand is cheaper |

That last row overturns a lot of plans. A job that is profitable to do with software in Dhaka may be cheaper to hand to a person in a district town. Then your competitor is not an app, it is an employee on BDT 8,000 a month, and the comparison has to be made against that salary.

And the third row ties into price. The habitual routes and amounts for taking money are on the [pricing tests](/en/validation/pricing-tests) page.

## A Two-Day Plan

| When | Work |
|---|---|
| The week before | Phone the association or someone you know, fix a time |
| Day one, morning | Sit with someone from the association or committee, collect names |
| Day one, midday | Walk the market, four or five conversations |
| Day one, evening | Write the notes up, fix tomorrow's names |
| Day two, morning | Six or seven conversations off referrals |
| Day two, midday | Two people of a different kind, a supplier or a courier agent |
| On the way back | Fill in the decision box, write down which assumption broke |

Do not skip the evening box. Go to sleep without writing the notes up and two days of conversation become one blurred memory later. The order of questions and the note fields are on the [customer interview scripts](/en/validation/interview-scripts) page.

## What It Costs

What a two-day trip takes is nothing next to the cost of one wrong decision. Bus or train fare, one night's stay, food, and local transport. In most district towns that comes to a few thousand taka.

Make the comparison this way. Develop for three months on a Dhaka sample, then discover the price will not work in a district town, and the loss is three months plus two salaries. Put a bus ticket beside that loss and the decision takes no time.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Sizing the country off a Dhaka sample | One unit in seven seen, the rest assumed |
| Conflating Dhaka division with Dhaka city | The percentage inflates more than twofold |
| Walking into shops with no introduction | Careful answers come back, numbers do not |
| Trying to speak in the local accent | It sounds fake and widens the distance |
| Offering Dhaka prices | "Too expensive" ends the conversation there |
| Leaving labour cost out of the model | You misread who the competitor is |
| Deciding after one town | One town's peculiarity gets taken for a national rule |
| Not writing the notes up in the evening | Two days of conversation become a blur |

## Checklist

- [ ] Chose towns after looking at unit counts by division
- [ ] Took two different kinds of town
- [ ] Spoke to an association or someone I know before travelling
- [ ] Arranged a local companion
- [ ] Confirming units of measure and amounts as they come
- [ ] Did not offer Dhaka prices
- [ ] Counted the cost of labour as a competitor
- [ ] Wrote the notes up each evening
- [ ] Recorded which assumption broke on the way home

Rule of thumb: **Dhaka division's urban areas hold 13.47% of the country's economic units. To speak about the rest, you have to get on a bus once.**
