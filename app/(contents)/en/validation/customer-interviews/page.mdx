# What to Ask in a Validation Interview, and Which Answers to Distrust

> **Key Takeaway:** An interview has one job: collecting events from someone's past. Not collecting their opinion of your idea. In Bangladesh there is an extra layer on top of that, because people agree out of courtesy, and saying "no" to a stranger's face reads as rudeness here. So however good "yes, we'd definitely need that" sounds, it is worth nothing as evidence of price. Get three things out instead: when the thing last happened, how the job gets done today, and how much money or time went into it last month. And keep your own solution out of the room until the last five minutes.

## An Interview Has One Job

A validation interview is not a sales call and it is not a survey. You are there to collect events. Who did what, when, what it cost, where it jammed. Events, not opinions.

The difference shows up in one example. "Keeping the shop's books is hard" is an opinion. "Last Saturday I sat until eleven at night reconciling the credit ledger, and a BDT 4,000 discrepancy came out of it" is an event. Nothing can be built from the first. From the second you learn when the job happens, how long it takes, how much a mistake costs, and roughly how much this person might pay to make it go away.

So ask yourself one question when the interview ends. How many dates and numbers made it into your notes? If the answer is none, you had a conversation, not a validation.

## A Yes Is Cheap, an Event Is Not

The biggest trap is asking about the future. Hear "would you use something like this?" and a person starts imagining, and everyone is generous in their imagination. People are different when the money has to come out.

| Invites a polite answer | Ask this instead | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Would you use a service like this? | When did you last have to do this job, and how did you get it done? | An answer about the future is imagination, one about the past is memory |
| How much a month could you pay? | What did this cost you in total last month? | Not a guess at price, a record of spending |
| Is the problem a big one? | The last time it happened, what exactly did you lose? | A quantity of damage instead of an adjective |
| Does this seem useful to you? | What have you tried and given up on, and why did you drop it? | The reason for abandoning is where the real obstacle hides |
| When would you want to start? | Who makes the decision, and who releases the money? | The enthusiasm and the budget may sit with different people |

The questions on the right have one advantage. Courtesy cannot dress up their answers. If someone cannot remember the date, they will say so, and that is information too. Trouble whose last occurrence nobody can recall is not big trouble in that person's life.

Keep one more thing in mind. Show your solution at the top of the interview and the rest of it is spoiled. From then on they answer to please you, and stop describing their own reality. Bring the solution up at the end, and change the question when you do: "we're working on this — could we sit with someone like you again?"

## Whom to Approach, and Where to Find Them

The best respondent is someone already doing something about the problem. Patching it together, paying a person to handle it, spending money on it. Someone doing nothing about it does not have a problem.

A few places to look:

- **Around whatever workaround they use now.** Facebook groups, business groups on WhatsApp, association meetings, the wholesale market.
- **On location.** Shops, warehouses, the courier counter, the pharmacy outside the hospital. Pick the slack hours in the afternoon, because nobody will sit with you during the rush.
- **Through referrals.** End every interview by asking who else does the same work. A good interview brings you the next two.

Be careful in two places. Friends and relatives cannot validate anything, because they are there to encourage you. And a sample drawn entirely from Dhaka will tilt the picture. According to the Economic Census 2024, of the country's 1,17,02,792 economic units, 73,85,828 are rural and 43,16,964 urban — roughly two-thirds sit outside the cities. Twenty interviews in Dhaka will not tell you about the national market, and that gap shows up later in sales.

## What to Say Before You Sit Down

When a stranger turns up asking about the books, the first reaction is suspicion. Are you from the tax office, running an NGO survey, or collecting intelligence for a competitor. That is not rudeness, it is ordinary caution. So make four things clear in the first thirty seconds.

1. Who you are, and why you came.
2. That you are not here to sell anything, and will not be selling anything today.
3. How long this will take, and where what they say will be used.
4. That neither their name nor the business name goes anywhere.

Then ask permission. May I write this down? That single question changes the tone of the whole interview, because it tells the person they are not the subject of the conversation, they own it.

Give some thought to place and company as well. If your respondent is a woman, pick an open or familiar setting, let a family member sit in if they want to, and keep it to daytime hours. For some customer groups, answers come much more freely when a female colleague asks the questions, particularly on household spending, health, or savings. That keeps things courteous, and the quality of the data goes up too.

## By Phone, or Sitting Face to Face

Both have a use, but not the same use.

|  | Face to face | Phone or WhatsApp |
|---|---|---|
| Time it takes | More, half a day with travel | Less, 15 to 25 minutes |
| What you get more of | The ledger, the screen, how the work runs, the silences | Reaching many people fast |
| Where it works best | The first five to seven interviews | When you are checking a pattern |
| Risk | Costs time and money | People want to wrap up, so it stays shallow |

The early interviews teach you most in person, because you can see the ledger or the screen with your own eyes. Someone tells you they keep the accounts in a Google Sheet, then opens the sheet and it has not been touched in three months. That does not surface over the phone.

The phone has reach. BTRC counts 12.08 crore mobile internet connections in June 2026, so most customer groups can be reached by phone. But having a connection is not the same as giving you time, and on the phone people want to be brief.

If you are thinking of doing the job with a written form, remember one figure. Per the 2022 census, literacy among those aged 7 and above is 74.80% — 71.68% rural and 81.45% urban. Sending a written form to one in four people means getting no answer, and in rural areas that gap is wider. Given that two-thirds of the country's economic units are rural, you can see which way the arithmetic runs.

## How to Take Notes, and When to Stop

Do not write summaries during an interview, write the person's own words. Note down "has trouble reconciling accounts" and six days later nothing will come back to you. Note down "the last three days of the month the manager and I sit until late, and there are still gaps" and that sentence will later earn its place in your product copy.

Keep three sections in your notes, which nearly every interview will fill:

```text
Person and type of business:
Place and date:
Event (when it last happened, what happened):
How it gets done now, and by whom:
Numbers (time, money, frequency):
Verbatim quotes:
Who decides, who releases the money:
Next action:
```

There is no rule about how many is enough. You can plan for 20 to 30 at the start, but the real signal is not the count. When four or five interviews in a row teach you nothing new, you have understood that customer group. At that point either move to a decision, or move to a different kind of person.

The opposite mistake exists too. Stop after ten similar people and it will look like everyone agrees, when you have only looked from one angle. Build variation into the sample by place, business size, and age.

### Say You Are Building Ordering Software for Pharmacies

On day one you visit six shops and ask, "would you use an app for placing orders?" All six say yes. Your notes hold six yeses and not a single number.

On day two you change the question. "How many times did you order last week, and how?" Now the answers come out differently. Four say they phone the sales rep. One says the rep comes twice a week himself, looks at the list, and takes it away. One says he forgot to order last week, ran two days without a particular medicine, and four or five customers walked out because of it.

Look at the gap between the two days. Nothing can be decided from day one's six yeses. Day two's answers have events, numbers, and a real loss in them. And something surfaced that you had not considered: the rep comes and collects the order himself, so your competitor is not an app, it is a person.

## Deciding After the Interviews

Piling up notes is not validation. Every five to seven interviews, sit down for half an hour and write the answers to three questions.

- Which same event came back, from how many people, and what kind of people were they.
- Who is already spending money or time on this, and who is not.
- Which assumption broke, and what you now think in its place.

The third is the most valuable. Fail to write down a broken assumption and you will make the same mistake again in two months as though it were new. How to pick the next experiment is on the [idea validation](/en/validation) page.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Showing your solution at the start | For the rest of the interview people answer to please you |
| Asking about the future | Everyone says yes and not one number lines up |
| Interviewing friends and relatives | You get encouragement, not evidence |
| Drawing the whole sample from Dhaka | Two-thirds of the country's units drop out |
| Writing summaries instead of words | Six days later the notes yield nothing |
| Not asking for numbers | Both the price ceiling and the size of the loss stay unknown |
| Stopping after ten similar people | A one-angle picture gets taken for the whole market |
| Collecting notes without deciding | The work happens, the validation does not move |

## Checklist

- [ ] The questions are about the past, not the future
- [ ] Remembered to keep my solution for the last five minutes
- [ ] Respondents are already doing something about the problem
- [ ] The sample includes people outside Dhaka
- [ ] Stated who I am, why, how long, and what stays private before sitting down
- [ ] Asked permission to take notes
- [ ] Got at least one date and one number out of every interview
- [ ] Wrote down two verbatim quotes
- [ ] Sat down and wrote decisions after every five to seven interviews

Rule of thumb: **Collect events, not opinions. If no date and no number made it into your notes, that was a conversation, not a validation.**
