# Interviews or Surveys: Choose the Right Research Method

> **In short:** Use interviews to learn why people do something. Watch a task to see how they do it. Use a survey to count clear answers across a group you can define and reach. Often you need more than one method. Neither a friendly conversation nor a large online poll proves that people will buy.

## Start with the decision

Use this before writing an interview script or sending a form. You will leave with a research question, a method and a clear limit on what the result can tell you.

An **interview** is a conversation that lets you ask follow-up questions. A **survey** asks the same set of questions in a standard way. **Observation** means watching a real task, with permission.

There is no sound basis for saying interviews always work better in Bangladesh. The right choice depends on your question and whom you can reach. Government user-research guidance recommends matching each question to a method that can answer it.[^gds-methods]

Use this table to choose your first step.

| Your question | Start with | What it cannot prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Why do shopkeepers call again after sending an order? | Interview plus a look at the last order | How common the problem is across the country |
| Can people finish this task on their phone? | Watch them try a working version | Whether they will use it every week |
| Which of these clear problems do our current customers report? | A carefully written survey | What non-customers need |
| How many people in a district have this need? | A properly designed population study | That everyone with the need will buy |
| Will someone pay this price for this offer? | An honest offer they can act on | Long-term demand from one small test |

Write the decision in plain words: “We need to choose whether to fix scheduling or payment reminders first.” Then ask what you need to learn to make that choice.

## Use interviews when the answers are still unclear

If you do not know the steps, the words people use or the main source of trouble, a form can force people into your guesses. Start with open questions about a recent event.

```text
Tell me about the last time you did [task].
What started it?
What did you do first, and then what happened?
Where did you wait, repeat work or ask someone for help?
What did you try to solve that problem?
What happened when you left it unsolved?
```

Ask one question at a time. Avoid selling your idea during the interview. A person may agree that your app sounds useful just to be polite. A recent event gives you more to examine than praise.

Take notes on what happened, what the person said and what you think it means. Keep those three things separate. Ask before recording, and keep private records out unless you truly need them and have permission.

You might begin with five or six varied cases as a small learning round. That is a work plan, not a magic sample size. Add people when important views are missing or new cases keep changing the explanation.

## Use a survey when you know what to ask

A survey needs more than a list of questions. Define the group, how you will reach them, the time period and how you will handle people who do not reply.

Pew Research Center explains why clear wording, answer choices, question order and testing the form matter.[^pew-questions] A large number of replies cannot repair a question that people read in different ways.

For example, replace “Is our fast and easy app affordable?” with separate questions about a specific recent use and price. The first question combines speed, ease and cost, while also telling the reader the answer you want.

Try this short set for people who actually used a class-planning tool:

```text
1. In the last 7 days, did you create or change a class schedule?
   Yes / No / Not sure
   If No or Not sure, skip to question 5.

2. Which method did you use most for that task?
   Paper / Spreadsheet / This tool / Another tool / Someone did it for me

3. What, if anything, was difficult about that last task?
   [Free answer]

4. Did you have to enter the same information more than once?
   Yes / No / Not sure

5. May we contact you about this answer?
   Yes, using [work contact] / No
```

These questions are examples to test, not a ready-made research instrument. Ask a few intended readers to explain each question in their own words. Fix confusing terms before sending it widely. Use the language they understand best and check translated wording with readers.

## Worked example: 50% of whom?

Imagine a Sylhet startup with 60 active training-centre accounts. It interviews eight centres and learns that duplicate schedule entry may be a problem. It then sends a short survey to all 60 accounts. This example and its figures are made up.

Forty-two accounts reply. In this example, all 42 say they changed a schedule in the last seven days, so they answer the follow-up questions too. Twenty-one say they entered the same details twice in that week.

| Number | Calculation | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Reply rate | 42 / 60 × 100 = **70%** | Seven in ten invited accounts replied |
| Reported problem among replies | 21 / 42 × 100 = **50%** | Half of those who answered reported it |
| Accounts with a known report | 21 / 60 × 100 = **35%** | At least this share of invited accounts reported it |

The last row does not mean the other 65% had no problem. Eighteen accounts did not reply. You do not know their answers. Also, all 60 are already users. This tells you nothing precise about every training centre in Sylhet or Bangladesh.

The next action is to watch the duplicate-entry task and test a fix. Do not jump from “half of respondents reported a problem” to “half the country will buy our product.”

## Keep the people you missed visible

An online form shared in your own network mostly reaches people near that network. A Facebook group may leave out people who do not use it. A survey sent in English may miss people who would answer in Bangla. People with a strong complaint may be more likely to reply.

You do not fix these gaps by collecting more answers through the same narrow route. Change how you recruit, use another method or state the limit clearly. If a market-size or high-cost decision needs reliable population estimates, work with a qualified researcher.

## Make a small plan

```text
Decision and date:
Question we need to answer:
Group we mean:
Method and why it fits:
How we will find people:
Who this plan may miss:
Questions or task to try:
Time and cash budget:
What result would change our decision:
What we cannot conclude from this round:
```

For a small exploratory round, you could budget half a day to plan and test the questions. Add recruitment, interview or visit time, then time to review the notes. Survey costs depend on who you need to reach and how; there is no useful universal price per answer.

## Before using the result

- [ ] The method answers a specific question.
- [ ] People can understand the questions without your help.
- [ ] The group and time period are clear.
- [ ] Missing replies and excluded groups remain visible.
- [ ] Every percentage says who is being counted.
- [ ] Reported opinions are separate from observed actions.
- [ ] The finding leads to one next test or decision.

Start by writing the decision and choosing one row from the first table. Use the [field research plan](/en/validation/field-research-plan) when you need visits, or [demand tests](/en/validation/demand-without-building) when the next question is whether people will act.
