# Choose Your First Customer Group

> **In short:** Start with the people who have a specific problem you can see, reach, and test. Put their real actions on a one-page card. A made-up age, name, or photo does not tell you whether they will buy.

Use this guide after you have talked to potential customers or watched them try to solve the problem. If you have not done that yet, start with [the customer interview guide](/en/validation/customer-interviews). By the end, you will have two short customer-group cards, one group to test first, and a clear reason for that choice.

A **customer group** is a set of people who face a similar problem in a similar situation. A **persona** is a short description of such a group that helps your team make decisions. Early on, call it a *working profile*: interviews give you clues, but they do not prove how common the problem is. Useful profiles focus on what people are trying to do and how they behave, rather than an invented character's name or age.[^nng-personas]

## Start with the job, not an age label

Read your notes and look for a repeated job, a moment when it comes up, and what people do now. For example, “Facebook sellers who check cash-on-delivery (COD) payments against courier returns each week” is more useful for a first test than “women aged 25–35.” The first description tells you whom to find and what to ask. Age or location matters when it changes access, buying, delivery, or the problem itself; do not add it as decoration.

Keep the **user**, **payer**, and **decision maker** separate. In a business, the person entering orders may not own the budget. A family purchase can work the same way. Strategyzer's customer-profile work distinguishes people's jobs, obstacles, and desired outcomes, and its B2B guidance warns against treating a whole company as one person.[^strategyzer-profile][^strategyzer-roles]

For each note, mark what it is:

- **Assumed:** your team thinks it is true but has not checked.
- **Reported:** a person said it happened. Keep the date and context.
- **Observed:** you saw the action, a record, or a completed purchase. Note exactly what you saw. One observation does not prove that everyone in the group behaves that way.

Ask about the last real event before showing your solution. A polite “I would use it” is weaker than seeing the workaround or a payment. An interview still differs from a buying test.[^strategyzer-evidence]

## Copy this card for each group

Use one card per group. Leave a line blank when you do not know; do not fill it with a guess and quietly call it fact.

```text
Customer-group card — name / date / version:

Who has the problem, and when does it come up?
What are they trying to get done?
What do they do today? What does that cost them in time or money?
Who uses a solution? Who pays? Who decides?
Where can we reach people in this group, with their consent?
What have we observed? Source and date:
What have people reported? Source and date:
What are we still assuming?
What action would show interest beyond a polite yes?
Next test / owner / date:
```

Do not put a real interviewee's name, phone number, or private account data on a card you will share. Keep the underlying notes securely with the team that collected them.

## Choose one group to test first

Compare two cards using four plain questions:

1. **Problem:** Which group has a recent, specific example of the problem? Which part is still your guess?
2. **Reach:** Can you find and speak with people in this group without relying only on friends?
3. **Action:** What have they already done to solve it? What small next action could they take now?
4. **Delivery:** Could you serve this group with the time and money you have? What must you check?

Do not add these answers into a fake “persona score.” Write the trade-off and pick the group whose next test will teach you most. If both cards are mostly assumptions, interview more people before building. If the user and payer differ, test the problem with the user and the purchase decision with the payer.

**Fictional example:** A team wants to build a tool that helps online sellers match courier payments to returned orders. Its first card describes sellers who dispatch COD parcels and check returned parcels against payouts. Its second card describes sellers who take payment first and hand-deliver. The team has seen a courier report in one conversation, but has not checked how often the first group's problem occurs or whether anyone would pay. It chooses the COD group for the next *test*, not as a proven market. It asks sellers to show a recent payout mismatch and tries a simple manual reconciliation with permission. If no one will share a real report or try the fix, the team revises the card before writing software.

That example's people and events are invented for teaching. It says nothing about actual Bangladeshi sellers.

## Check whether the card helped

You are ready for the next test when a teammate can use the card to find the right people, ask about a recent event, and name the action that would change your mind. After the test, keep the old date and write a new version. Do not turn a few interviews into a market-size claim. Use [market sizing](/en/ideas/market-sizing) for that different question, and put the group you chose into your [business model worksheet](/en/validation/business-model-design).
