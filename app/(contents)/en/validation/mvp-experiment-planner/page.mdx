# The Experiment Plan: One Page to Fill In First

> **Key Takeaway:** An experiment does not fail, an experiment without a plan is simply meaningless. Set the thresholds after the result arrives and any result can be turned into a good one, which is the most familiar trap there is. So fill in a few fields on one page before you start. Which assumption I am testing, what I will measure, at what number I proceed, at what number I stop, how many people the offer reaches, how many days it runs, and who does it. The page takes fifteen minutes, and it settles whether the experiment will hand you a decision or merely keep you busy.

## The One-Page Sheet

Fill this in before starting. In a notebook, a notes app, or a sheet, whichever you prefer.

```text
Experiment name:
Date:

1. The assumption I am testing (one line):
2. The biggest loss if it is wrong:
3. Which test I will run:
4. What I will measure (a single number):
5. Threshold to proceed:
6. Threshold to stop:
7. How many people the offer reaches:
8. Start and end dates:
9. Spending cap:
10. Who does it:

Result (fill in afterwards):
Decision: proceed / change / stop
Reason:
```

## How to Fill the Fields

**The assumption.** "People will like this" is not an assumption, it is a hope. A working assumption is one that can be proven false. Something like, "small shop owners will pay BDT 1,500 a month for a bookkeeping service." That sentence can be proven wrong, which makes it testable.

**The metric.** Choose one primary decision metric, such as paid bookings or second-month retention. Record supporting measures and guardrails such as refunds, complaints and delivery costs so a good headline result cannot hide harm or unviable economics.

**Two thresholds.** Writing only "proceed if it succeeds" is not enough, the stopping threshold has to be there as well. Without it an experiment never finishes, it just continues.

**How many.** Most people leave this field empty, and that is what ruins the experiment. Without a number fixed in advance, you stop at five people and whatever came back gets treated as the result.

**Dates.** Set an end date long enough to observe the behaviour. A first-order test may take one to three weeks; second-month retention necessarily needs longer. Use checkpoints without cutting the observation period short.

**Spending cap.** If ads are involved, set the ceiling up front. Spending continues even when results do not arrive, and you notice late.

## How to Read Small Numbers

Small samples can be described with counts and percentages, but estimates are uncertain. A convenience sample of twenty people does not represent the whole market, regardless of how precisely you calculate its rate.

| What you got | What can be said | What cannot |
|---|---|---|
| 0 of 20 paid | No payments in this sample; investigate offer, group and delivery | "There is no market" |
| 3 of 20 paid | Observed conversion is 15% (3/20); check repeatability | "The market converts at 15%" |
| 12 of 20 paid | Promising in this sample; check delivery costs and repeatability | "More than half the market will buy" |
| 2 of 5 paid | Observed conversion is 40% (2/5), with high uncertainty | A reliable population estimate |

Always show the numerator, denominator, recruitment method and offer terms alongside a rate. “Two of eight sent an advance (25%)” describes the observations; it does not establish that 25% of the target market will buy.

Keep reasons beside the numbers. A repeated objection suggests a hypothesis to test. Different objections may reflect different needs, trust or access constraints; they do not prove the underlying problem is weak.

## A Filled-In Example

Say you are considering a medicine order-coordination service for small pharmacies outside Dhaka. Filled in, the page looks like this.

```text
Experiment name: Pharmacy orders, round one
Date: 10 August 2026

1. Assumption: small pharmacy owners in Bogura town will pay
   BDT 800 a month for weekly order coordination.
2. Loss if wrong: three months of development and two salaries.
3. Test: advance booking, BDT 300.
4. Measure: how many send BDT 300 under the stated total price and refund terms.
5. Threshold to proceed: 6 or more out of 24.
6. Threshold to stop: 1 or fewer out of 24.
7. How many: 24 owners, in person at the shop.
8. Dates: 10 August to 21 August.
9. Spending cap: BDT 4,000 for travel and phone.
10. Who does it: me, with one other person.
```

These thresholds are illustrative decision rules, not statistical proof. A Tk 300 advance tests willingness to reserve under the stated terms, not completed payment of Tk 800 or recurring renewal. Confirm the remaining payment and delivery before claiming that price is validated. For two to five advances, inspect reasons and choose the next test.

## The Rhythm of a Week

Splitting the week like this keeps the work from stalling.

| Day | Work |
|---|---|
| Mon | Fill the page, write out the words of the offer |
| Tue and Wed | Make the offer, reach half the people |
| Thu | Look at what you are hearing, record feedback; keep the offer consistent or label a separate version |
| Sat and Sun | Reach the other half |
| Mon | Write the result, fill in the decision box |

Wording can change how people understand and respond to an offer, just as price can. Keep the offer consistent within a comparison. If a correction is necessary, label the new version and report the groups separately; do not pool them as one unchanged experiment.

## How Many Experiments at Once

For a small team, start with one experiment to keep execution manageable. Parallel tests can be interpretable if assignment, exposure and outcomes are designed to avoid interference; overlapping uncontrolled changes are the problem.

For example, If two experiments run on entirely separate groups and answer separate questions, they can run side by side. An advance test with shopkeepers on one side, a sign-up test with suppliers on the other. The people are different, so the results can be read separately.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Setting thresholds after seeing the result | Every result proves successful |
| Not writing a stopping threshold | The experiment never finishes, it just continues |
| Not fixing how many people to reach | You stop at five and treat that as the result |
| Measuring three things at once | An argument at decision time about which to use |
| Changing the price mid-way | The two halves stop being comparable |
| Percentages off small numbers | False confidence, caught in the deck |
| No end date | The experiment becomes a project and the decision slips |

## Checklist

- [ ] Wrote the assumption so that it can be proven false
- [ ] Measuring a single number
- [ ] Wrote both thresholds, proceed and stop, in advance
- [ ] Fixed how many people I will reach
- [ ] Put in a start and an end date
- [ ] Capped the spending
- [ ] Running one experiment at a time
- [ ] Noting the reason behind every no
- [ ] Filled in the decision box at the end, with the reason

Rule of thumb: **Thresholds first, results second. An experiment with no stopping threshold written down is not an experiment, it is busywork.**
