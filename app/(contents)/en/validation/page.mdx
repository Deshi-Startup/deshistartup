# Idea Validation: Find Out Whether People Need Your Offer

> **In short:** Start with a specific customer problem, learn how people handle it now, then test one uncertain part of your proposed offer. Record actions and counter-evidence as well as interest. Finish with a reason to continue, change, stop or investigate further—not a declaration that the whole idea is proven.

You have an idea and can imagine the product. Before spending heavily on it, you need to find out whether the people you intend to serve face the problem, whether your approach helps, and whether they will choose it over what they already do. That work is called **validation**. It reduces uncertainty; it does not certify that a business will succeed.

Use this sequence for an initial idea or a new offer in an existing business. Each step answers a different question. A difficult problem does not automatically create demand for your proposed solution, and a sale does not yet establish that you can deliver profitably.

## 1. Describe the problem before the product

“Build an app for shops” describes a solution and a broad audience. A testable starting point names a person, a task, the difficulty and the current alternative:

```text
For [specific people], [task] becomes difficult when [situation].
They currently use [alternative].
I have observed [evidence]. I still need to check [unknown].
```

For a running example, suppose a founder is considering software to match clothing shops' orders with courier settlements. Her first description might be:

> For owners of small clothing shops that dispatch through two couriers, matching delivered orders to money received may be difficult when reports arrive in different formats. They currently compare reports with an order notebook. I have heard one owner describe missing an unmatched order. I do not yet know whether this repeats, what it costs, or whether the existing method is acceptable.

The owner’s account is a lead, not a market finding. The founder should first investigate reconciliation—the act of matching two sets of records—not build a dashboard. If her interviewees instead report that their notebooks work well, that is useful evidence too.

## 2. Speak with people who actually do the task

Choose respondents because they fit the situation, not because they are likely to praise you. In the example, a shop with one courier and no order records may have a different problem from a shop using two couriers. Keep those differences in your notes.

Start with a simple invitation: “I’m learning how shops match courier payments to orders. Could you describe how you handled the last settlement? Would a short conversation be convenient?” Explain how you will use the notes. Ask permission before recording, quoting or following up. A refusal is not permission to try repeatedly.

These questions turn a broad complaint into a specific event:

| Ask | What you are trying to learn |
|---|---|
| When did you last match a courier settlement? | A recent event, rather than a general opinion |
| Could you walk me through what you did? | Steps, people and records involved |
| Where did anything fail to match? | The actual difficulty; it may not be the one you expected |
| What did you do next, and what did it cost? | Consequences and effort; retain estimates as estimates |
| When does your current method work well? | Evidence against replacing it |
| Who would decide to change this process? | User, decision-maker and payer, which may differ |

Do not start with “Would you buy my app?” or suggest a number such as “Does this waste five hours?” You would be supplying the answer you hope to hear. If someone cannot recall a cost, record **unknown**, not zero. Ask for a made-up or redacted demonstration rather than copying customer names, phone numbers or balances.

There is no universal interview count that proves an idea. Set a reachable recruitment plan and review date. Track invitations, replies and completed conversations separately; a few convenient respondents cannot establish how common the problem is across Bangladesh. Use [interview scripts](/en/validation/interview-scripts) when you want a longer question set.

## 3. Understand what customers would be switching from

An alternative can be a notebook, employee, familiar supplier, spreadsheet or doing nothing. A competing app is only one possibility. Ask what the existing method gives the customer and what switching would require.

For the shop example, a notebook may be familiar and already used for other work. A staff member can follow up directly with the courier when something does not match. Replacing either means learning a new process and trusting another provider with business records. Ask which part is genuinely inconvenient and which part the shop would be reluctant to lose.

If the source order records are missing or inaccurate, software cannot simply recover the truth from them. The first useful service may involve improving those records. Finding that out changes what you should test.

## 4. Choose a test for one uncertainty

An **assumption** is something the plan depends on that you have not established. Test the assumption that would most change the next commitment. You do not need software to learn whether someone values a service you can deliver manually.

Choose a method that can produce the evidence you need:

| Uncertainty | Small test | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Does this problem occur? | Reconstruct recent incidents with relevant people | Willingness to buy your solution |
| Can our approach solve the task? | Run an agreed manual reconciliation using redacted records | Reliable operation at high volume |
| Will someone buy this offer? | Present a real scope, price and delivery date; record the response | A large or repeatable market |
| Will customers come back? | Observe another eligible purchase/use cycle | Long-term retention from one repeat |

For a tutoring idea, a bounded test might be one honestly described class; for a business service, one defined pilot. Check the permissions, safety, data and delivery obligations for the actual activity before running it. Calling it a test does not remove those obligations. Do not take deposits for something you cannot explain, deliver or refund under the agreed terms.

## 5. Write the plan before seeing the result

Decide what you will offer, to whom, and what you will record before recruiting participants. Set a time and spending limit. If the test involves payment, agree the actual scope, price, delivery date and cancellation or refund terms before taking money.

For the reconciliation service, a small paid offer could cover one week's existing records and return a list of unmatched entries. Specify whether the job includes contacting the courier or recovering money; those are additional commitments. Agree who can access the records used for the job.

Now write what result would justify your next step. If the question is willingness to buy, count actual purchases separately from people who ask for a demo. If the question is whether you can deliver, track the time taken and whether the owner can act on the report. Set the size of the test around what you can responsibly complete. A small result may justify another small cycle, not a large product build.

A **prototype** lets someone try or understand a proposed experience. A **minimum viable product (MVP)** is an early usable version delivering the core benefit. Use either when it fits the question; neither is a compulsory first step. The [demand-test planner](/en/journeys/test-demand) helps you write an offer, limits and review criteria before seeing the result.

## 6. Read the result without inflating it

Continue the example with fictional results: four relevant owners hear a clearly scoped BDT 500 offer. Two purchase, one asks for a demonstration and one declines because the notebook is sufficient. The founder completes both reports in five delivery hours; one buyer asks about another week. These figures illustrate interpretation, not a recommended price or success threshold.

There are two purchases from this selected group. That does not predict the response of strangers or buyers at another price. The demonstration request is an opportunity to follow up, and the repeat enquiry has not become a repeat purchase. The owner's reason for declining belongs in the notes too: the existing alternative may be good enough for some shops.

The two jobs bring **2 × BDT 500 = BDT 1,000** in revenue. Dividing by five delivery hours gives BDT 200 per delivery hour, before other costs, outreach time and founder compensation. The service has attracted purchases, but attractive profitability is still unproven. Review the slowest step and the owner's use of the report before deciding whether another manual cycle is worthwhile. Building software would require a further reason to believe it can improve the economics.

Keep observations separate from explanations. “Two owners paid” is an observation; “shops will pay for this” is a much wider claim. Do not change the review rule after seeing the result just to call the test successful. An unexpected result can justify a different next test, but record why you changed direction.

## 7. Check whether a reachable market is plausible

A successful test with a few shops does not tell you the market size. Separate the broad group that might face the problem from the people you can identify, reach and serve.

For this service, identify shops in an area you can serve, then check which use the relevant couriers and keep usable records. That gives you a list of candidates to approach. It is not a revenue forecast: you still need to learn buying behavior, repeat demand and delivery costs.

Consider location, language, trust, payment preferences, seasonal peaks and who approves the purchase. Use [market research](/en/ideas) for deeper estimates; do not multiply national population by an imagined conversion rate.

## Choose what to change next

If the reconciliation test fails because order records are missing, improve the input process before designing a dashboard. If customers pay but each job takes too long, try another small manual cycle focused on the slowest step. Measure whether the change reduces work while keeping the report useful; automation is a possibility to investigate, not the conclusion.

If people do not buy, ask whether the problem, offer, price or purchasing process explains the result. If you could not reach the intended people at all, fix recruitment before judging demand. Choose the next action from the obstacle your test exposed, even if that means setting the original product idea aside.
