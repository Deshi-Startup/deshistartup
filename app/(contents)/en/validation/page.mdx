# Idea Validation: From a Problem to a Tested Decision

> **In short:** Start with a specific customer problem, learn how people handle it now, then test one uncertain part of your proposed offer. Record actions and counter-evidence as well as interest. Finish with a reason to continue, change, stop or investigate further—not a declaration that the whole idea is proven.

Use this guide when you have an idea but do not know whether to build it. You can complete a first test plan and understand how to review it here. If you already sell, the same process can test a new customer group or offer.

**Validation** means gathering evidence to reduce uncertainty before making a larger commitment. **An assumption** is something your plan depends on that you have not established. Different assumptions need different tests: finding a painful problem does not establish that your particular solution works or that someone will pay for it.

## 1. Describe the problem before the product

“Build an app for shops” describes a solution and a broad audience. A testable starting point names a person, a task, the difficulty and the current alternative:

```text
For [specific people], [task] becomes difficult when [situation].
They currently use [alternative].
I have observed [evidence]. I still need to check [unknown].
```

**Worked fictional example:** A founder in Sylhet is considering software to match clothing shops' orders with courier settlements. Her starting note is:

> For owners of small clothing shops that dispatch through two couriers, matching delivered orders to money received may be difficult when reports arrive in different formats. They currently compare reports with an order notebook. I have heard one owner describe missing an unmatched order. I do not yet know whether this repeats, what it costs, or whether the existing method is acceptable.

The owner’s account is a lead, not a market finding. The founder should first investigate reconciliation—the act of matching two sets of records—not build a dashboard. If her interviewees instead report that their notebooks work well, that is useful evidence too.

## 2. Speak with people who actually do the task

Choose respondents because they fit the situation, not because they are likely to praise you. In the example, a shop with one courier and no order records may have a different problem from a shop using two couriers. Keep those differences in your notes.

Start with a simple invitation: “I’m learning how shops match courier payments to orders. Could you describe how you handled the last settlement? Would a short conversation be convenient?” Explain how you will use the notes. Ask permission before recording, quoting or following up. A refusal is not permission to try repeatedly.

These questions turn a broad complaint into a specific event:

| Ask | What you are trying to learn |
|---|---|
| When did you last match a courier settlement? | A recent event, rather than a general opinion |
| Could you walk me through what you did? | Steps, people and records involved |
| Where did anything fail to match? | The actual difficulty; it may not be the one you expected |
| What did you do next, and what did it cost? | Consequences and effort; retain estimates as estimates |
| When does your current method work well? | Evidence against replacing it |
| Who would decide to change this process? | User, decision-maker and payer, which may differ |

Do not start with “Would you buy my app?” or suggest a number such as “Does this waste five hours?” You would be supplying the answer you hope to hear. If someone cannot recall a cost, record **unknown**, not zero. Ask for a made-up or redacted demonstration rather than copying customer names, phone numbers or balances.

There is no universal interview count that proves an idea. Set a reachable recruitment plan and review date. Track invitations, replies and completed conversations separately; a few convenient respondents cannot establish how common the problem is across Bangladesh. Use [interview scripts](/en/validation/interview-scripts) when you want a longer question set.

## 3. Understand what customers would be switching from

An alternative can be a notebook, employee, familiar supplier, spreadsheet or doing nothing. A competing app is only one possibility. Ask what the existing method gives the customer and what switching would require.

In the fictional interviews, the founder records:

| Current approach | Why a shop might keep it | What to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Notebook plus courier reports | Familiar, no setup and already used for other work | Whether mismatches are actually missed |
| A staff member checks each report | Someone can ask the courier questions | Time spent and whether software would reduce the work |
| Only check the total amount | Fast when orders are few | Whether an incorrect total has a material consequence |

The lesson is not that notebooks are bad. A new service must improve something the shop values enough to justify effort, cost and risk. If accurate source records are missing, prettier software will not solve that input problem.

## 4. Choose a test for one uncertainty

A **prototype** demonstrates an experience; a **minimum viable product (MVP)** is an initial usable version that delivers the core benefit and supports learning. Not every experiment needs a product. A conversation, a clearly described offer or a manual service can answer an earlier question.

Choose a method that can produce the evidence you need:

| Uncertainty | Small test | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Does this problem occur? | Reconstruct recent incidents with relevant people | Willingness to buy your solution |
| Can our approach solve the task? | Run an agreed manual reconciliation using redacted records | Reliable operation at high volume |
| Will someone buy this offer? | Present a real scope, price and delivery date; record the response | A large or repeatable market |
| Will customers come back? | Observe another eligible purchase/use cycle | Long-term retention from one repeat |

For a tutoring idea, a bounded test might be one honestly described class; for a business service, one defined pilot. Check the permissions, safety, data and delivery obligations for the actual activity before running it. Calling it a test does not remove those obligations. Do not take deposits for something you cannot explain, deliver or refund under the agreed terms.

## 5. Write the plan before seeing the result

After the initial conversations, suppose the fictional founder has three concrete accounts of recurring mismatches. She now wants to test whether a manual reconciliation service is useful enough to purchase.

Her next plan is specific:

- **Offer:** Reconcile one week of existing order and settlement records for BDT 500; return an exception list within two working days. Exclude contacting customers or recovering money.
- **People:** Four shop owners from the researched group who reported a recent mismatch and can supply suitable redacted records.
- **Evidence:** Offers made, acceptances, payments, records received, time to deliver, whether the owner can act on the report and whether another week is requested.
- **Decision:** For this small learning step, at least two purchases plus useful delivery within the time budget would justify another manual cycle. Poor delivery means revise the process. Interest without purchase means examine the offer and buying process before building.
- **Limits:** Two pilots, six hours in total and a review after seven days. Confirm the terms and relevant requirements before offering paid work.

These prices, counts and limits are **fictional planning choices**, not recommended market rates or proof thresholds. State why your own rule would justify the size of your next commitment.

## 6. Read the result without inflating it

Suppose four owners hear the offer. Two purchase, one asks for a demonstration and one declines because the notebook is sufficient. The founder completes both reports in five hours; one buyer requests another week.

The decision record should separate evidence from interpretation:

| Observation | Reasonable interpretation | Still unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Two of four approached owners paid BDT 500 | This offer attracted two purchases in this selected group | Conversion among cold prospects or at another price |
| Both received a usable report | The manual process worked for these records | Whether other formats break it |
| One requested another week | A repeat-use opportunity to follow up | Whether the repeat is actually purchased |
| Delivery took five hours | Time cost is material | Whether automation would reduce it enough |

Revenue is 2 × BDT 500 = **BDT 1,000**, or BDT 200 per delivery hour before other costs and founder compensation. That is not evidence of attractive profitability. The next action is another carefully bounded manual cycle and a closer look at the slowest step—not a large software build.

A paid pilot, demonstration, introduction, booking and compliment are different events. Record each by its actual meaning. Keep contradictory evidence and recruitment failures; do not move the decision rule after the result simply to call the test successful.

## 7. Check whether a reachable market is plausible

A successful test with a few shops does not tell you the market size. Separate the broad group that might face the problem from the people you can identify, reach and serve.

Suppose the founder can list 30 shops in a serviceable area, but only 12 currently use two couriers and have suitable records. Her next outreach list has 12 candidates—not every clothing shop in Bangladesh. Neither 12 nor 30 is a revenue forecast. She still needs to learn buying behaviour, repeat demand and delivery economics.

Consider location, language, trust, payment preferences, seasonal peaks and who approves the purchase. Use [market research](/en/ideas) for deeper estimates; do not multiply national population by an imagined conversion rate.

## Your plan and completion check

Copy this after studying the example:

```text
Customer and situation:
Problem and existing alternative:
Evidence already collected / unknowns:
One assumption to test:
Method and actual offer, if any:
People to approach and how:
What I will record, including refusals:
What would justify continuing / changing / stopping:
Time and money limits:
Permissions, terms or safeguards to resolve first:
Owner and review date:
After the test: observations / interpretation / unknowns / next action:
```

You have a usable plan when another person can identify the respondents, run the method, record the result and explain the next decision without guessing. If an answer is “people like it” or “the market is huge,” make it concrete.
