# Design a Business Model You Can Test

> **In short:** Pick one specific customer group, figure out what they need, how you will deliver it, and who pays for it. Use the Business Model Canvas worksheet below to spot the blind spots in your plan. Remember: a completed canvas is still just a list of guesses. Before you spend time and money building, test the one assumption that could break the whole business.

Use this guide when you have a promising idea, but you are not yet sure how it actually makes money. It also helps when customers like your product, but serving them costs more than they are willing to pay. By the end of this page, you will have a one-page plan and one clear experiment to run. If you are still deciding between building a scalable startup, an agency, or a small business, check out [our business-type comparison](/en/start-here/startup-vs-sme) first.

A **business model** simply explains three things: what value you create for customers, how you deliver it to them, and how you make money doing it. Charging a monthly subscription or taking a 5% cut is just a pricing method. It does not explain why someone buys from you, or what it costs you to deliver that service every day. Alexander Osterwalder and the Strategyzer team created the Business Model Canvas to connect all these pieces on a single page across nine simple blocks.[^strategyzer-canvas]

## Copy the worksheet

Grab a notebook or copy the text block below. Set a timer for 45 minutes for your first draft. This quick session is not about proving your business yet—it is about getting the ideas out of your head and onto paper so you can see where the holes are. Bring any customer notes, supplier quotes, or cost estimates you already have. You do not need any paid software.

Write one or two short sentences for each block. Keep it focused on a single business model. Always separate the person who uses your product from the person who pays for it (in a school, for example, students use the software but principals or parents pay). If you are building a marketplace connecting two sides, list both sides and explain what each gets, how you reach them, and who pays.

Beside every answer, tag it with one of three labels: **assumed** (your best guess), **reported** (someone told you this in an interview), or **observed** (you actually saw it happen or have receipts). Seeing one customer pay proves that single sale happened today; it does not prove they will renew next month.

```text
Business-model worksheet - name / date / version:

1. Customer segments
   Who uses this product? Who pays for it?
   Which specific group will we serve first?

2. Value propositions
   What job does this do for the customer?
   Why will they switch from what they do today?

3. Channels
   How will customers find out about us?
   How will they buy and receive the product?

4. Customer relationships
   How do we earn their trust and keep them around?
   Who handles onboarding, questions, and cancellations?

5. Revenue streams
   Who pays, how much, and for what?
   How often do they pay, and when does the money actually arrive?

6. Key activities
   What daily tasks must we do exceptionally well to keep our promise?

7. Key resources
   What people, tools, data, or equipment do we need?

8. Key partners
   Who outside our team do we rely on to run this?
   What key agreement, supplier, or permission is still missing?

9. Cost structure
   What costs go up with every new sale or customer?
   What fixed bills must we pay even if sales are zero?

For each answer, note: assumed / reported / observed
Evidence source and date:

Our decision notes:
What breaks if our customer count grows tenfold?
Which untested assumption could kill this business if it turns out wrong?
Next test / spending limit / owner / review date:
```

Your worksheet is ready when a friend or teammate can read it and clearly see the connection: who the buyer is, what problem you solve, what work you must do, and how the numbers add up. Words like “everyone,” “social media,” or “great customer service” are too vague and hide real risks. Replace them with a specific first group of buyers, a realistic way to reach them, and a promise you can actually deliver.

## Worked example: matching courier payments

Imagine Nadia in Chattogram wants to build software for small online clothing brands that sell through Facebook and websites.

In Nadia's scenario, the clothing brands she spoke with rely heavily on Cash on Delivery (COD) through local couriers. In her problem hypothesis, after couriers deliver parcels and deduct charges, they send periodic statement reports and transfer payments in lump sums. Shop owners then struggle to match individual order records against deposits to confirm which deliveries cleared, which returned, and where payment discrepancies lie.

**Note: Nadia, the clothing brands, prices, costs, and test targets below are fictional. They are for teaching only, not real market data or quotes from any courier.**

Nadia’s idea is simple: her software imports courier statements, matches tracking numbers against orders, and gives the shop owner a daily list of missing or disputed payments. She decides to start only with brands that use two popular couriers whose reports can be exported as Excel or CSV files.

Here is her first draft of the canvas. Every single row is still **assumed**.

<div role="region" aria-label="Nadia’s hypothetical business model" tabIndex={0} style={{ overflowX: 'auto', maxWidth: '100%' }}>

| Block | Nadia's starting guess |
|---|---|
| Customer segments | The shop owner pays; the owner or an assistant checks the payments. Start with small clothing brands using two major couriers. |
| Value propositions | Catch missing or delayed courier payments in minutes without copying data by hand, while letting staff double-check every flag. |
| Channels | Personal product demos by Nadia to qualifying shop owners; later, test word-of-mouth referrals. |
| Customer relationships | Hands-on setup help, a short video walkthrough in Bangla, and quick support over WhatsApp during business hours. |
| Revenue streams | BDT 1,000 per brand each month, paid in advance before the service month starts. |
| Key activities | Import statement files, match records, flag errors, and fix data issues. |
| Key resources | File parsing and matching code, secure cloud storage, and someone who understands local courier statements. |
| Key partners | Payment gateway for subscriptions; cooperation from couriers if automated data feeds are needed later. No formal deal is signed yet. |
| Cost structure | Server hosting, payment gateway fees, onboarding and customer support time, plus development and admin expenses. |

</div>

Looking at this draft, two big operational questions stand out right away:
1. Can shop owners actually export usable files, and will they give Nadia permission to process their financial records?
2. Can Nadia’s software match the records automatically, or will she end up spending two hours manually fixing messy spreadsheets for every single shop?

Writing this down helps Nadia see her real risks before promising automatic features to anyone. When testing sample records with early users, she must ask for clear consent and delete any unnecessary customer names or phone numbers.

To compare her idea against existing alternatives—like spreadsheets, hiring a part-time bookkeeper, or relying on courier portals—Nadia can use a [competitor map](/en/ideas/competitor-map). For now, she just needs to know what alternative she is replacing, and why switching to her tool is worth the effort.

## Check what one customer leaves

Let's run the simple napkin math for one month. Suppose Nadia charges BDT 1,000 per brand. She estimates BDT 100 per customer for server hosting and payment gateway fees. She also values customer support time at BDT 300 per hour. (Always include the cost of your own labor—even if you are not taking a formal salary yet, founder time is not free!)

If a brand needs 30 minutes of support each month, the labor cost is 0.5 hours × BDT 300 = BDT 150. That leaves **1,000 − 100 − 150 = BDT 750 per brand** to help cover general business costs.

Now see what happens if each brand needs two hours of support because their files are messy:

| Per paying brand each month | 30 minutes of support | 2 hours of support |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly fee collected | BDT 1,000 | BDT 1,000 |
| Hosting and payment gateway fees | BDT 100 | BDT 100 |
| Support labor allowance | BDT 150 | BDT 600 |
| Leftover contribution per brand | BDT 750 | BDT 300 |

Suppose Nadia has BDT 30,000 in monthly fixed costs (software tools, office internet, marketing, and basic development). To break even:
- In the 30-minute scenario, she needs 30,000 ÷ 750 = **40 paying brands**. Those 40 brands will require 40 × 0.5 = **20 hours of support per month**. That is very manageable alongside product development.
- In the 2-hour scenario, she needs 30,000 ÷ 300 = **100 paying brands**. While BDT 300 per customer is still a positive contribution that can eventually cover fixed costs, 100 brands at two hours each requires **200 hours of support per month**. Since the model already includes paid support labor, this is not an automatic failure, but the team must check whether it can provide 200 support hours each month alongside product development. Staffing, automation or a simpler process could help meet that workload.

Keep in mind: this simple calculation is not your net profit, and it is not a cash forecast. It does not include customer acquisition costs, initial onboarding setup, taxes, refunds, or payment defaults. And BDT 30,000 is just a rough estimate for shared bills, not a full budget. If the leftover contribution from each customer is **zero or negative, under unchanged assumptions**, adding more customers cannot cover the fixed costs. For a complete look at these metrics, read our guide on [unit economics](/en/metrics/unit-economics).

Cash timing also matters: if a shop pays at the end of the month, but Nadia has to pay server bills and support staff at the beginning of the month, she could run out of cash even if the business looks profitable on paper. Always track when cash actually arrives versus when your bills are due.

## Can delivery repeat as the business grows?

A **repeatable** business model means you can serve customer #2 and customer #20 using the same reliable steps, without reinventing the wheel each time.

A **scalable** business model means your revenue can grow significantly without your delivery effort, headcount, and costs having to grow at the exact same rate. Just saying “it’s software” does not make a business scalable.

Compare Nadia serving 10 brands versus 100 brands:
- At 30 minutes of support per customer, total monthly support grows from 5 hours to 50 hours.
- At two hours of support per customer, support explodes from 20 hours to 200 hours.

Now ask the same question for onboarding new users, making sales, and fixing messy files. Which step becomes the bottleneck first? What can you automate or simplify so you do not get buried in manual work?

Here, a **sustainable** model simply means that your revenue, cash timing, and daily workload allow the company to keep running month after month. (A business canvas does not measure environmental or social impact.) Watch out for hidden traps that could sink the company later: mishandling customer data, burning out on unpaid overtime that you cannot keep up, or making promises to customers that depend on courier access you do not control.

Accelerating Bangladesh's public business-model curriculum also connects revenue and costs with resources, partnerships and testing.[^ab-models] For Nadia, the useful next step is to measure the manual work before projecting growth.

## Choose the assumption to test first

Look at every unproven answer on your canvas and ask two simple questions:
1. **If this guess is completely wrong, will the whole business fail?**
2. **What real evidence do we actually have right now?**

Pick the assumption that has the highest risk and the weakest proof. Strategyzer groups startup risks into three buckets: **Desirability** (do customers actually want this?), **Feasibility** (can we actually build and deliver it?), and **Viability** (can we make money and survive?).[^strategyzer-testing] Your test should focus squarely on your biggest risk.

For Nadia, the biggest unknown right now is not marketing. It is delivery: *“Can we accurately match statements from both couriers without hours of manual data cleanup?”*

She can test this feasibility assumption with 2 or 3 friendly clothing brands before building the full product. With their permission, she takes last month's courier statements, runs them through a simple matching script, and compares the result against manual human checking. She counts how many transactions were mismatched and logs exactly how many minutes it took to clean up the errors. If the courier files are too messy or the error rate is too high, she knows she must fix her approach before taking anyone's money.

Once she proves the matching works, she can run a small pilot to test customer demand and willingness to pay:

```text
Assumption: Target clothing brands will pay for this reconciliation service.
Offer: One month of service for BDT 1,000, with clear deliverables and easy cancellation.
Recruiting: Reach out to 10 qualified clothing brands and record where each lead came from.
Pass threshold: At least 3 brands pay upfront and actively use the weekly report.
Delivery check: Track initial setup time separately from weekly support time.
Budget cap: BDT 3,000 in cash expenses and 15 hours of Nadia's time.
Review date: 30 days after launch; also track whether brands renew for month two.
Owner: Nadia.
```

These numbers are specific choices for Nadia’s fictional test, not universal industry benchmarks. If 3 out of 10 brands pay, she has enough positive evidence to invest in another small test. It does not prove she has a massive market or that everyone will renew, but it gives her confidence to keep going. If zero brands buy, she needs to find out why: was it the price, lack of trust, bad timing, or the wrong customer group? Reaching a time or budget limit is your signal to pause and evaluate, not an excuse to quietly spend more money.

When designing your own test, write down your offer, the measurable result you want to see, your pass/fail threshold, your budget limit, and your deadline before you talk to customers. You can use a [simple MVP format](/en/validation/mvp-examples), map out details with our [experiment planner](/en/validation/mvp-experiment-planner), or run structured [pricing tests](/en/validation/pricing-tests) if your biggest unknown is what people will pay.

## Before spending more

Review this checklist before writing code, hiring anyone, or spending your savings:

- [ ] You have identified one clear first customer group, who pays, and the specific problem you solve for them.
- [ ] You have mapped out the daily work required to deliver the service, along with needed permissions and partners.
- [ ] You labeled your guesses clearly as guesses, and noted real evidence and dates where you have them.
- [ ] You checked the math on support time, cash timing, and what happens when your customer count grows tenfold.
- [ ] You picked your single riskiest assumption and designed a small, low-cost experiment with a strict deadline.
- [ ] You are ready to update your canvas with what you learn from the test—especially if the results challenge your idea.

Nadia's next move is simple: test file matching on real statements, then run a tightly budgeted paid pilot if the technology works. Your next step should be just as clear and concrete. Keep your dated worksheet so you can look back and see how real customer evidence shaped your business.
