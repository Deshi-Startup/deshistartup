# Trust Signals: Test What Helps a Customer Take the Next Step

> **In short:** Find the question behind the buyer's hesitation. Show real proof that answers it, then check whether people can understand it and act. A badge or testimonial cannot make an unreliable service trustworthy. Keep every version honest and measure what happens after the click, including complaints and cancellations.

## Find the worry before choosing the proof

Use this when people reach your offer but hesitate to try, book or pay. A **trust signal** is something that helps them judge whether your business will do what it says. It might be a working demo, clear contact details, a real review or a plain explanation of returns.

Start with questions and lost sales. Ask about the last time the person considered an offer like yours. What did they need to check? What could have gone wrong? Do not assume every hesitation is about trust: the price, product or timing may simply be wrong.

Match the concern to something you can prove.

| Buyer concern | Useful evidence to test | What does not answer it |
|---|---|---|
| Does this product do the job? | A clear demo or sample task | A row of vague quality badges |
| Will the item match the listing? | Accurate photos, dimensions and what is included | A heavily edited image |
| What happens if there is a problem? | Plain support and remedy steps you can follow | “100% satisfaction” with no terms |
| Is there a real team behind this? | A working contact route and accurate business details | A borrowed office photo |
| What will I pay? | Full price and any known extra charges before commitment | A low headline price with hidden fees |

GoZayaan's Marc Thiry described listening, clear explanations and reliable human support as part of building trust.[^gozayaan-trust] The lesson is that proof and actual service need to agree.

## Copy the test plan

```text
Buyer group and point where people stop:
The concern, in the buyer's own words:
Where we heard or observed it:

Our guess about what would help:
The truthful proof or explanation we will show:
Who has checked that we can keep the promise:
Where it will appear:

Action we want to observe:
What might get worse: confusion / complaints / refunds / support time
How we will check those outcomes:
Time and spending limit:
Review date:
What we will keep, change or stop based on the result:
```

The guess is a <Term name="hypothesis">hypothesis</Term>: an idea to test. Write it so it could be wrong. “A short demo will reduce uncertainty about setup” is testable. “People love our brand” is too broad.

## Check the proof itself

Use only reviews from real people, with permission for any name, photo or quote you publish. Say when a review came from a paid or gifted arrangement. Do not write fake reviews, invent customer counts or show logos without a valid reason to use them.

If you have no customers yet, show how the product works. Label sample records as made up. Explain the current stage and what is available. You can earn trust by being clear about limits.

Future Startup's 2022 account of Shajgoj describes useful product content as part of its content-and-commerce model.[^shajgoj-content] That is a source of ideas, not proof that any article or video will increase your sales. Choose content that answers a real buying question.

Never hide required price, risk or policy information from one test group. Both versions must give people what they need to make a fair choice. Test clearer wording, placement or extra useful proof, not whether concealment produces more clicks.

## Watch people use it first

Show the page or process to a few intended users on the device they would normally use. Give them a realistic task, such as finding the total cost and checking how to cancel. Let them act before you explain.

Government guidance on usability testing recommends realistic tasks that let people use the service in their own way.[^gds-usability] Ask what they think will happen next. If they still cannot explain the offer, fix the unclear part before buying more traffic.

Take notes on the action and the person's explanation. “Found the return steps after searching for a minute” is more useful than “liked the page.”

## Worked example: a small demo test

Imagine a Chattogram startup selling a class-scheduling tool to training centres. Buyers worry that setup will take too long. These figures and the business are made up.

Both page versions show the same price, terms and contact details. Version B adds a short, real setup demo. The team gives similar visitors a random chance of seeing either version during the same period. It does not also change the price or ad message.

Here is the small first result. Each visitor from the intended training-centre group is counted once. Other visitors are left out of this comparison.

| Result | Version A | Version B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Eligible visitors | 40 | 40 |
| Demo bookings | 8 | 10 |
| Booking rate | 8 / 40 = 20% | 10 / 40 = 25% |
| People who attended | 5 | 5 |

The booking-rate gap is **5 percentage points**, but only two extra bookings. Both groups produced five attendees. This is too little to declare a lasting improvement. Chance, who visited and how follow-ups were handled may matter.

The team reviews why people booked but did not attend. If the new demo creates a false impression that setup is instant, more bookings could be a bad result. The next step is to fix that misunderstanding and gather more useful evidence.

With very little traffic, a clear user test may teach more than a weak A/B test. An **A/B test** compares two versions, ideally by random assignment. Get statistical help before treating small differences as proof for a large spending decision.

## Measure the promise through delivery

Pick the action that matches the stage: a completed task, attended demo, paid order or repeat use. Keep page views and likes separate from those outcomes.

Also watch what could get worse. A stronger promise may raise signups and support work at the same time. A return message may be easier to find but unclear about which cases qualify. Look at the questions and complaints behind the count.

If the promise needs a faster reply, budget the staff time. If it needs real product photos, budget the shoot and updates when the product changes. A suggested first pass could take a few hours to gather questions, prepare one change and test it with readers. Your actual cost depends on the proof you need.

## Before keeping the change

- [ ] We know the specific concern we are trying to answer.
- [ ] The proof is real and any permission is recorded.
- [ ] Both versions give the buyer all material information.
- [ ] People can explain the offer and the next step.
- [ ] We checked actions after the click, not just attention.
- [ ] The result states its small-sample limits.
- [ ] The team can keep the promise as more people arrive.

Start with one repeated buyer question. Use the [landing page template](/en/customers/landing-page-template) to make the answer easy to find, and [support scripts](/en/customers/support-scripts) to keep replies consistent when something goes wrong.
