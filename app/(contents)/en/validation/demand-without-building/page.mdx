# Five Tests for Demand Before You Build the Product

> **Key Takeaway:** Validating demand does not need a product, it needs evidence. The strongest form of that evidence is someone parting with money, followed by parting with time or handing over their own data. Signing up to a waitlist or hearing "great idea" sits at the bottom of the list, because neither costs the person anything. This page holds five tests you can run in a week or two without any software. Which one you pick should be decided by your riskiest assumption, not by your feature list. And if the test involves taking money, write the refund promise down first, because trust in an unfamiliar name is not cheap here.

## Which Evidence Is Strong, Which Is Soft

Not all agreement is equal. How much it costs the person is what sets the strength of the signal.

| Signal | How strong |
|---|---|
| Paid an advance or booking amount | Strongest |
| Signed a written pilot with a fee attached | Strong |
| Gave you their own time, such as a two-hour onboarding | Medium |
| Let you look at their ledger or data | Medium |
| Referred someone else | Medium |
| Left an email on a waitlist | Soft |
| "Looks good, let me know when you launch" | Not evidence at all |

Get any of the top three rows and you can act on it. Get either of the bottom two and all you know is that the person was not annoyed.

There is one exception. Even without money, if someone shows you the most guarded thing in their operation — the sales ledger, the supplier's prices — that is a strong signal too. Taking that risk means they genuinely want a solution.

## Test 1: Do It by Hand

Instead of software, do the job yourself, for five to ten people. Before building a reconciliation tool, set up five shops' accounts in a Google Sheet and send a weekly report. Before a matchmaking app, put the two sides together by hand.

- **What it proves:** whether the job is genuinely needed, how often, and how long it actually takes to do.
- **What it does not prove:** whether it can be scaled. Handling ten people by hand and handling ten thousand are two different businesses.
- **Time and cost:** one to two weeks, almost no money, but all of your own time.

The biggest gain from this test lies elsewhere. Doing the work by hand teaches you where the time actually goes, and later you build exactly that part into software. Go the other way round and you build features nobody touches.

## Test 2: Take an Advance or a Booking Amount

Name a price and ask for part of it up front. Not the full amount — BDT 200 or 500 as a booking works. Someone sending BDT 500 over bKash has stopped being merely polite.

- **What it proves:** whether they will pay, and at what price they stop.
- **What it does not prove:** whether they will be satisfied once they have the product, or still be there in month two.
- **Time and cost:** one week, low cost, though you may need ads to find the people.

Settle three things before you start. The delivery date, the rule for refunding if you cannot deliver, and a contact number. There are reasons Bangladeshis are careful about paying online in advance, so saying the refund promise out loud is not enough, it has to be written. A small amount, fast delivery, and an immediate refund together build your first piece of credibility.

One figure helps when choosing how to take the money. Per the Economic Census 2024, of the 1,15,948 economic units that use an online system in their business, 55.78% transact in cash and 50.89% through mobile banking, with online banking at 29.81%.[^bbs-economic-census-2024] So a test that offers only a card link leaves half the people unable to pay you at all. Keep the bKash or Nagad number available, and <Term name="cod">cash on delivery</Term> if that fits.

## Test 3: A One-Page Offer

Build one page with the problem, the solution, the price, and a button. The button leads to a way to pay or a direct WhatsApp message, not a form. Then send a specific group of people to that page, through ads or by posting in the relevant groups.

- **What it proves:** which message people respond to, and how many have to see it before one acts.
- **What it does not prove:** willingness to pay, if the button has no payment step. Emails collected are not demand.
- **Time and cost:** two to three days to build, plus some money to bring an audience.

Keep two numbers separate when reading the result. How many saw the page, and how many pressed the button. The first tells you about your message, the second about your offer. A good message can sit above a weak offer, and confusing the two puts you to work fixing the wrong thing.

## Test 4: Put the Two Sides Together by Hand

For a marketplace or any connecting business, this is the only honest test. Rather than building a platform, you stand between the two sides yourself. Hear the buyer's requirement, find a supplier, settle a price, let the transaction happen.

- **What it proves:** whether both sides actually want this, and which side is hard to get.
- **What it does not prove:** whether people would complete the job themselves given software.
- **Time and cost:** two to four weeks, low cost, high effort.

The real answer here is usually the unexpected one. Most of the time one side turns out to be easy to find and getting the other side is the actual business. Knowing which side is hard can rewrite your whole plan, and finding that out requires no code.

## Test 5: A Paid Pilot

The most useful test in B2B. Tell the company you would like to run a one-month pilot, the fee is this much, and at the end they get these three things. The fee does not have to be large, it only has to be non-zero.

- **What it proves:** whether a budget exists, what the decision path looks like, and who inside will help you.
- **What it does not prove:** whether a contract follows. A pilot and a purchase are two separate doors.
- **Time and cost:** one to three months, with your time as the main cost.

Write three things on paper before the pilot starts. What success will mean, who measures it, and what the next step is if it succeeds. Without that, a pilot ends in compliments and no purchase order arrives.

## One Word About Waitlists

A thousand names on a waitlist feels good, but it is not evidence of demand. Leaving an email costs nothing, and three months later that person will not remember your name either.

A waitlist does have a use, just a different one. It tells you which message stops people, and who to call on day one. So keep the list, but do not make decisions on the size of it. The difference between the two is set out on the [pre-orders versus waitlists](/en/validation/preorders-vs-waitlists) page.

## Which Test to Pick

Choosing starts with one question. Right now, which assumption would cost you most if it were wrong?

| Your biggest risk | The test to run |
|---|---|
| Whether people will pay at all | Advance or booking amount |
| Whether the job is genuinely needed | Doing it by hand |
| Whether the message lands | The one-page offer |
| Whether the two sides can be matched | Matching by hand |
| Whether a company has budget and approval | The paid pilot |

Run one at a time. Run two together and even a result will not tell you which one produced it.

## What a One-Week Test Looks Like

Say you are thinking about a service that tracks attendance and payroll for small factories. There is one big risk: whether the owner will pay for this job. So you pick the second test.

On Monday you speak to eight owners and make an offer — for BDT 1,500 a month you will keep the attendance records and produce the payroll sheet at month end. BDT 500 of the first month up front, refunded if they do not like the work. By Wednesday two have sent money, three say they will look at it next month, and three do not reply.

Two sounds low? Look at the number from the other side. Out of eight, two sent an advance to a stranger on the strength of an offer alone, without seeing a product. And you now know a price around BDT 1,500 is acceptable, which you did not know before. The next step is clear: keep those two accounts by hand, and in three weeks see whether they pay for a second month.

## How to Read the Result

Before you run the test, write down which result means go ahead and which means stop. Decide afterwards and any result can be turned into a good one.

- **Go ahead.** The people who paid did so for the same reason, and they are the same kind of person.
- **Change course.** There is a response, but the people saying yes are not the group you had in mind. Change the group, not the product.
- **Stop.** The offer reached enough people, nobody paid, and everyone's reason for not paying was different.

The third is the hardest, because it feels like a little more effort would do it. If the reasons are all one reason — everybody says the price is too high — then more effort is worth it. If the reasons scatter, the problem itself is weak.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What follows |
|---|---|
| Treating waitlist names as demand | The list grows and nothing sells |
| Running a test with no payment step | Everyone looks interested and price stays untested |
| Taking an advance without a written refund rule | Trust breaks, and once broken it does not come back |
| Offering only a card route in the test | People used to <Term name="mfs">MFS</Term> drop out |
| Running two tests at once | A result arrives with no attributable cause |
| Not setting the thresholds in advance | Any result gets explained as a success |
| Mistaking the by-hand test for the business | It works at ten people and collapses at a hundred |

## Checklist

- [ ] Written my riskiest assumption in one line
- [ ] Picked exactly one test that matches that risk
- [ ] The test has a step where money or time is given up
- [ ] Kept a bKash or Nagad route, not just cards
- [ ] Written down the delivery date and the refund rule
- [ ] Fixed how many people the offer will reach
- [ ] Written the go, change, and stop thresholds in advance
- [ ] Noted the reason behind every yes and every no

Rule of thumb: **Demand can be validated without a product, but not without a payment step. A test in which nobody spends anything is not a test.**
