# Customer Journey Map: Find Where Customers Get Stuck

> **In short:** Write down the steps a customer takes to finish one task. Separate what you saw, what the customer told you and what you still need to check. Then try one change where the customer gets stuck.

Use this when people enquire or buy, but struggle to get the result your startup promised. You will make a **customer journey map**: a record of someone's steps towards a goal, including their questions and difficulties.[^nng-journey] A notebook or shared document is enough for a first draft.

A sales funnel counts how many people reach each stage of a sale. A journey map helps you find out what happens between those stages. It cannot tell you why every customer leaves, and drawing it does not prove a solution works.

## Copy the worksheet

Start with a single recent case. Keep it labelled as one case until you have checked other people's experiences. Use a customer code rather than a name in a widely shared version.

```text
Customer type and role:
Task they wanted to finish:
Start and end of this map:
Case code / dates / sources:

Repeat for each step:
What the customer did / where:
What they needed to know or expected:
What actually happened / time spent waiting:
Evidence: observed, reported or assumed:
Question still to check:

Problem chosen for a test:
Change to try / person responsible:
What we will check / review date:
Result / keep, change or stop:
```

This is a suggested working sheet, not a scoring system. Keep the current experience and any proposed future version in separate documents. Otherwise, a plan can start looking like something customers already do.

Gather the messages, order notes or support records you are allowed to use. Arrange a conversation with the customer if the record has gaps. Budget for that conversation, team review and a follow-up check. There is no fixed completion time; an unanswered question may require another visit or call. You do not need new software, but staff time, travel or phone costs still count.

## Follow the customer's task, not your departments

Choose one point of view and one situation. The person who pays may not be the person who uses the product. Give each a separate map when their steps differ. Journey stages should come from the situation, rather than a fixed list every business must follow.[^nng-journey]

For example, “get a usable packaging sample before placing an order” has a clear end. “Become a loyal customer” is too broad for a first map. Start before the first message if the customer had to search, ask someone or compare alternatives.

Walk through the most recent attempt: What happened first? What did you do next? What were you waiting for? Ask about the actual attempt before asking for feature ideas. Include phone calls, visits, paper documents and work outside your app. Show a return to an earlier step, cancellation or unfinished attempt where it happened.

Use three evidence labels:

- **Observed:** you saw the action or have a relevant record. A sent message proves it was sent; it does not prove the recipient understood it.
- **Reported:** the customer described it. Preserve their meaning and the date. Ask permission before recording the conversation or sharing a quote.
- **Assumed:** the team thinks this might explain the event. Write the question needed to check it.

Leave feelings unknown when you have no evidence. Someone leaving a page does not tell you whether they were angry, busy or simply finished. Compare several people's accounts before combining them. Keep separate maps when their paths are meaningfully different.[^govuk-map]

## Worked example: the sample that never arrived

Imagine a Chattogram startup that helps food producers order packaging from suppliers. This example is fictional. A producer wants a sample box before approving a larger order. The startup thinks sending a price quote completes the enquiry, but the producer is waiting for the next step.

The team checks what happened:

1. **Find an option.** The producer sees the startup's page and sends the box size in Messenger. The message is a record of the request; how the producer found the page is still unknown.
2. **Check the offer.** The startup sends a quote that says a sample is available. The saved quote does not explain how to request one or whether it costs extra.
3. **Wait for the sample.** In a follow-up call, the producer says they expected it to be sent. The team checks its supplier log: nobody passed on a sample request.
4. **Ask for an update.** The producer sends another message and repeats the box size. The conversation ends without a sample date. The team records “unfinished,” not “customer rejected the price.”

The problem is now clear: the quote mentions a sample, but neither side knows who should request it. A new advertisement would not fix that missed step.

The founder checks sample availability, charges and timing with the supplier. They then test a revised quote that explains those terms and asks the producer whether to request the sample. One teammate owns the request and the next update. The guide does not assume that samples are free or promise a delivery date the supplier has not agreed to.

## Test the repair and keep the evidence

For the packaging example, check the next few similar enquiries. Did the customer decide whether they wanted a sample? If they agreed, did someone record the request and send the promised update? Did the producer receive a usable sample? Clearer instructions alone do not finish the customer's task.

Write down which cases you checked, their dates and any results you still do not know. A few conversations cannot tell you what share of all customers will buy. They also do not prove that your new wording increased sales. Compare similar tasks, and look for new problems the change may have caused, such as an unexpected charge.

Ask the people handling the next step to review the map. If the problem sits with a supplier, agree what your team can change and what still depends on them. Put individual promises into your [customer follow-up tracker](/en/customers/customer-crm), so the map leads to work someone owns.

Before keeping the repair, check:

- [ ] The map names one customer role, task and date range.
- [ ] Evidence and guesses are visibly different.
- [ ] Waiting, repeat steps and unfinished work are included.
- [ ] The change has an owner, a review date and a customer outcome to check.
- [ ] Shared copies hide personal details and private messages.

Your next action is to fill in the sheet for one recent unfinished task. Take the biggest unanswered question back to that customer. If you instead need to find people to speak with, start with the [first-customer guide](/en/customers/first-100-customers).
