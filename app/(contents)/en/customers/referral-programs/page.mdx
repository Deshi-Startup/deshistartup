# Customer Referral Programs: A Simple Plan and Cost Check

> **In short:** Ask customers who have already found your product useful to introduce you to someone with the same problem. Make the ask easy to forward. If you offer a reward, explain how it is earned before anyone joins, then count discounts, rewards and staff time before expanding the program.

A **customer referral** is an introduction or recommendation from an existing customer. A referral program gives those introductions a clear process. It can work with or without a reward.

Use this guide when you have customers who can describe something your product helped them do. If you are still looking for that first useful result, start with direct conversations in the [first 100 customers guide](/en/customers/first-100-customers). A reward cannot tell you whether people want the product without it.

## Start with a message you can copy

Choose a customer who has described a useful result, and name that result honestly. HubSpot's referral advice recommends a specific ask after a customer has received value. That is practical vendor advice, not evidence of a guaranteed response rate.[^hubspot-referral-asks]

> Hi [name], you mentioned that [specific result] helped with [task]. Do you know another [type of customer] who has this problem? If someone comes to mind, please forward the note below. They can decide whether to contact us. Please ask before sharing their number with us.

Write the note from your business, so the customer does not have to invent a pitch:

> We help [type of customer] with [one task]. If this is something you need, contact [name and contact method] for [a short conversation or demo]. [If relevant: the person who introduced us receives **reward** after **condition**, and you receive **benefit** under these rules: **link or attached terms**.]

Replace the brackets before sending. Leave out the reward sentence if there is no reward. A customer can decline; do not keep asking or use their name as an endorsement without permission.

## Put reward rules on one page

An invitation, a signup and a paid customer are different events. For example, Dropbox's own program separates an invitation from a completed referral and awards its reward only after the required steps. Use that distinction when choosing your own rule; its specific conditions are not a template for every business.[^dropbox-referral-rules]

Copy this card and complete it before asking anyone to join:

> **Our referral offer**
>
> **Who can join:** [eligible existing customers].
>
> **Who counts as new:** [for example, a business that has never bought from us]. Existing sales conversations [count / do not count].
>
> **What earns the reward:** [the exact paid purchase or other action, and any waiting period].
>
> **What each side receives:** [reward for the person referring; benefit for the new customer; or none].
>
> **When we provide it:** [a clear date or time after the conditions are met].
>
> **Limits:** [start and end dates, entry limit, reward limit and how we confirm a place].
>
> **Exceptions:** [self-referrals, duplicate claims, cancelled orders and refunds].
>
> **Questions:** [one contact who can explain the offer].

Choose a reward you can afford, including the promises already made. When the limit is reached, close new entries clearly. Do not change the rules for rewards people have already earned.

For business customers, check whether their company allows the offer. Do not privately pay a buyer's employee to steer a purchase. An openly agreed benefit for the customer business may be a better fit, but it still needs that business's approval. This guide does not explain reward taxes or industry-specific rules; get advice for your business where those apply.

## Check the cost before promising a reward

**Fictional example:** a Bangladesh startup sells job-tracking software to repair businesses. It tests a referral offer, then reviews the first month of service for each new customer. These are made-up amounts, not market prices or recommended rewards.

Assume four different new businesses meet the offer's conditions. Each pays for and receives its first month of service; none cancels or receives a refund in this example.

- The normal monthly price is **2,000 taka**. A first-month discount of **200 taka** means each business pays **1,800 taka**.
- Providing that month's service costs **800 taka** per business, including direct support and payment costs.
- The startup pays the referring customer **300 taka** for each new business that meets the offer's conditions.
- Setting up and running the test costs **1,000 taka in cash**, plus **four team-hours valued at 250 taka an hour**. That is **2,000 taka** across the test. These costs are separate from the service cost above.

Here is what one new customer's first month leaves:

1. Collected payment: **1,800 taka**. The discount has already been deducted.
2. After the service cost: 1,800 − 800 = **1,000 taka**.
3. After the referral reward: 1,000 − 300 = **700 taka**.
4. Share of the test cost: 2,000 ÷ 4 = **500 taka**.
5. Left after these costs: 700 − 500 = **200 taka**.

All four together leave **800 taka**, before other fixed bills and taxes. This is not net profit. The example puts a value on the team's time to help plan the cost. That amount is only a cash payment if the team is actually paid it. Do not count those same hours again elsewhere in this comparison.

If only two new businesses meet the conditions and the test still costs 2,000 taka, each one's share of that cost is 1,000 taka. The result becomes 700 − 1,000 = **−300 taka each**. Fewer customers change the result even when the reward stays the same.

With zero new paying customers, record the total spent; do not divide by zero. Record any refunds and costs of failed referrals too. Future renewals might help recover the cost, but do not count them before they happen. The [unit economics guide](/en/metrics/unit-economics) explains how to separate the cost of winning a customer from the cost of serving them.

For your own first-month check:

1. Divide the shared test cost by the number of new paying customers. This gives each customer's share of that cost.
2. Start with that customer's payment after the discount. Subtract the service cost, referral reward and their share of the test cost.
3. Record what is left in taka. A negative number means these costs were higher than the payment.

Use actual records and the same period throughout. If customers have different prices or costs, calculate each one's result separately. Include rewards you owe even if you have not paid them yet.

## Keep one record per introduction

A shared sheet or a simple document is enough for a small test. Keep it within the team that needs it; do not publish customers' contact details.

> **Referral record**
>
> Referral ID and date: […]
>
> Referring customer and new business: […]
>
> Permission to contact and preferred contact method: […]
>
> Offer version and confirmed place, if places are limited: […]
>
> Next action, owner and date: […]
>
> Action that earns the reward and evidence, such as a payment reference: […]
>
> Reward owed, due date and payment record: […]
>
> Duplicate, cancellation or refund, and what happened next: […]

Before paying, match the claim to the promised conditions and check for duplicate rewards. Keep a reward that is owed in your cost total even if you have not paid it yet.

At your chosen review date, count invitations, introductions, new paying customers and later repeat purchases separately. A referral code helps trace a purchase; it does not prove the customer would never have bought without the offer.

## Decide the next step

Before inviting more people, check:

- Can both sides explain the reward and its conditions?
- Are you getting introductions to people with the problem you solve?
- Have you counted both sides' benefits, time and unpaid rewards once each?
- Can you keep the promises already made and serve the new customers?

If introductions are useful but few people buy, review the offer and sales conversations before raising the reward. The [B2B sales guide](/en/b2b/sme-sales) can help with that next conversation. If the costs leave too little, change the terms for a future test and explain the change before it starts.
