# How to Get Your First 100 Customers in Bangladesh

> **In short:** Getting a hundred paying customers is not a marketing problem. It is an arithmetic problem with a deadline. Work backwards first. A hundred customers in 90 days is about eight a week. Then work forwards from the conversion rate you actually measure, and you get a weekly target you can hit or miss in plain sight. Test **three** channels properly instead of nine badly. The costly mistake is counting signups, followers or confirmed orders as customers. In Bangladesh a confirmed order is not revenue until it is delivered and paid for. Your next step is the four-week test plan below, with your own numbers in it.

Say Mahin builds a modular study desk for school and university students in Dhaka. It sells for BDT 4,900. The first fifty are made. His plan is "run Facebook ads and see what happens".

Four weeks later he has 340 page followers, 61 people who messaged him, nine confirmed orders, and six desks delivered and paid for. He tells his co-founder they are "getting <Term name="traction">traction</Term>". They are not, yet. But he has something more useful than traction. He has a measured <Term name="funnel">funnel</Term>, and it tells him exactly what to do next.

Most founders never get that far, because they never write the arithmetic down.

---

## Who needs this, and when

- **You have something people can buy today.** This is your page. Even a rough version counts, as long as money changes hands.
- **You have an idea and no product.** Not yet. Test demand before you build: [demand without building](/en/validation/demand-without-building) and [customer interviews](/en/validation/customer-interviews).
- **You have users but nobody pays.** Your problem is the offer or the price, not getting people in. Start with [pricing tests](/en/validation/pricing-tests).
- **You already have 100 paying customers.** You need retention and channel economics now, not this page. See [CAC, LTV and churn](/en/metrics/cac-ltv-churn).
- **You sell to businesses, not consumers.** Same method, different numbers. The differences are marked as you go. Also read [B2C vs B2B](/en/ideas/b2c-vs-b2b).
- **You are selling outside Dhaka.** The method holds. The channels do not. Read [selling outside Dhaka](/en/validation/outside-dhaka) alongside this.

By the end you will have a named group to sell to, three channel guesses, a weekly target worked out from your own <Term name="conversion">conversion rate</Term>, and a four-week plan that ends in a decision.

## What counts as a customer

Settle this before you count anything, because this is where founders fool themselves.

> **Rule:** A customer is someone who has paid you and received what they bought. Not a follower. Not a signup. Not a free trial. Not a confirmed order.

That last one matters more in Bangladesh than almost anywhere. With <Term name="cod">cash on delivery</Term> an order can be confirmed, packed, shipped and then refused at the door. You have paid the courier both ways and you are holding a returned product. Mahin's nine confirmed orders became six real sales. Anyone counting the nine is managing a number that will not pay salaries. The economics of that gap are worked through in [COD and return risk](/en/operations/cod-risk).

For B2B the same trap looks different. It is the signed pilot that never gets paid. Count the invoice cleared, not the letter of intent.

## Step 1: pick a small group, not a market

Your first 100 customers will not come from "students" or "SMEs". They come from a group small enough that you can reach all of them and serve them unusually well.

A group you can work with has four properties.

1. **You can list them.** Not estimate. List. If you cannot name 300 specific people, pages, shops or institutions where they gather, you are still looking at a market.
2. **They share one urgent problem.** Not a nice-to-have. Something they already spend money or hours on.
3. **You can reach them without permission.** A channel you control or can buy into, not one that depends on somebody's goodwill.
4. **They talk to each other.** The first 100 get much cheaper if customer 40 tells customers 41 to 55 about you.

Mahin's market is "students". His group is narrower: **students in shared accommodation near three universities in Dhaka, whose rooms have no desk.** He can list the buildings. The problem is daily and physical. He can reach them through Facebook groups near the halls, and on foot. And they live four to a room, so one desk in a room is a demonstration to three other people.

> **Keep in mind:** You can widen later. Almost nobody fails because they started too narrow. Plenty fail because they started so broad that no single message fitted anyone.

## Step 2: find out where your customers actually are

For most Bangladeshi startups the honest answer is "on a phone, on mobile data". In June 2026 Bangladesh had **135.94 million internet subscribers**. Of those, 120.84 million were on mobile and 15.10 million on ISP and PSTN connections.[^btrc-internet]

Be careful with that number in two ways.

**It counts subscriptions, not people.** BTRC counts an internet subscriber as a subscription that has used the internet at least once in the last 90 days.[^btrc-internet] One person with two SIMs counts twice. So the audience you can actually reach is smaller than the headline. Treating the headline as population is how market sizing goes wrong. If you need the number for a deck, see [market sizing](/en/ideas/market-sizing).

**Mobile-first is a design rule, not a slogan.** If 120.84 million of those connections are mobile, then someone is using your landing page, your checkout and your order form one-handed, on a mid-range Android phone, on a patchy connection. A form that needs a laptop loses you customers you already paid to reach.

Here is the practical channel map for a first-100 push in Bangladesh.

| Channel | Best for | What it really costs |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook and Instagram groups, unpaid | Consumer products with a visible before and after | Time, plus the risk of being removed as a seller |
| Facebook and Instagram, paid | Consumer products where you can afford to test | Ad spend, plus the discipline to switch off a losing ad |
| Messenger and WhatsApp | Closing, not finding. A conversation converts far better than a form | Response time. Slow replies lose the sale |
| Offline: campuses, markets, apartment blocks | Physical products and anything that needs trust | Your feet, and the best conversion rate you will get |
| Direct outreach by email, LinkedIn or phone | B2B, always | Research per prospect. Low volume, high quality |
| Marketplaces like Daraz | Reach without having to build trust yourself | Commission, and you do not own the customer |
| Referrals from existing customers | Every business, once you have ten happy ones | Nothing, which is why founders forget it |

Look at the last row. Almost every founder plans rows one and two and never builds row seven, even though <Term name="referral">referral</Term> is the only line with no extra cost and the most trust behind it.

## Step 3: three guesses, not nine

Pick **three** channels and give each one four weeks. Fewer than three and you learn nothing when one fails. More than three and none of them gets enough volume to tell you anything. Choosing a channel and deciding how you will judge it are one decision, not two. Pick a channel without deciding in advance what result would make you keep it, and by week five you will be arguing about opinions.[^ab-gtm]

Write each one as a sentence you can be wrong about, not a channel name.

> "If we post a before-and-after photo of a desk in a shared room to five hall-adjacent Facebook groups twice a week, at least 2% of the people who click will place an order."

That sentence has a channel, an action, a frequency and a number. "We'll try Facebook" has none of those and cannot be wrong, which is why it teaches you nothing. The format is worked through in [the MVP experiment planner](/en/validation/mvp-experiment-planner).

Mahin's three:

1. **Hall-adjacent Facebook groups**, unpaid, twice a week.
2. **Paid Facebook ads** to a 3 km radius around three campuses, BDT 800 a day.
3. **On-foot demonstrations**, one desk carried into a building lobby, on Saturdays.

## Step 4: work backwards, then forwards

This is the step that turns an intention into a plan.

**Backwards, from the goal:**

```
Target:                       100 paying customers
Deadline:                     90 days = about 13 weeks
Needed per week:              100 / 13 = 7.7  ->  8
```

**Forwards, from what you measure.** Run one week, measure honestly, then work out what volume that rate needs. Here is Mahin's week one across all three channels.

| Funnel step | Week 1 | Rate from the step before |
|---|---|---|
| People reached | 1,000 | — |
| Clicked through to the order form | 60 | 6.0% |
| Started an order | 9 | 15.0% |
| Confirmed an order | 3 | 33.3% |
| **Delivered and paid** | **2** | **66.7%** |

End to end that is 2 paying customers per 1,000 people reached, or **0.2%**. So:

```
Weekly target:                8 paying customers
End-to-end rate:              0.2%
Reach needed per week:        8 / 0.002 = 4,000 people
Currently reaching:           1,000 people
Gap:                          4x
```

Now the plan is specific enough to argue about. Mahin needs four times the reach, a better conversion rate, or a longer deadline. Those are the only three levers, and a week-one table like this tells you which one is cheapest to move.

Look down the rate column to find where his money leaks. Six percent click-through is fine. Fifteen percent of clickers starting an order is weak, so the form or the price is scaring people off. And a third of confirmed orders never turning into revenue is the COD tax, which is an operations problem, not a marketing one.

> **If you sell B2B:** replace "people reached" with "accounts contacted" and expect the numbers to flip. Maybe 100 contacted, 20 replies, 8 meetings, 3 pilots, 1 paid. The arithmetic is the same. Only the volumes and the length of the cycle change. With a one-month sales cycle, 100 customers in 90 days is usually the wrong goal. Set 20 instead, and make them customers who will give you a reference.

Nothing gets cut before week four. Founders kill channels in week two on noise. One bad week at low volume tells you almost nothing, and the channel you kill early is often the one that needed a different message, not a different platform.

Four rules for the four weeks.

- **Same effort, same measurement, every channel.** Otherwise you are comparing your own enthusiasm, not the channels.
- **Fill in the funnel table every Friday.** Five rows. Fifteen minutes.
- **Call every customer and every refusal.** The refusals are worth more. Ten minutes on the phone with someone who cancelled at the door beats a week of ad data. Use [interview scripts](/en/validation/interview-scripts) so you do not lead them.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Change the creative, the price and the landing page in the same week and week five will teach you nothing.

## Step 6: read the result

After four weeks, compare paying customers per channel. Not clicks. Not reach.

  unit=" paid customers"
  max={16}

    { label: "Facebook groups (organic)", value: 7 },
    { label: "Paid Facebook ads", value: 3 }

/>

| Channel | Paying customers, 4 weeks | Share of the 24 |
|---|---|---|
| On-foot demonstrations | 14 | 58% |
| Facebook groups (unpaid) | 7 | 29% |
| Paid Facebook ads | 3 | 13% |
| **Total** | **24** | **100%** |

Mahin's four-week total is 24 paying customers, and the ranking is the opposite of where he planned to spend his money. His slowest, least scalable channel produced more than four times what paid ads did. That is the normal shape of a first-100 result, because at this stage trust converts and reach does not.

This does not make paid ads useless. It makes them a **scaling** channel to come back to once the message is proven, rather than a **discovery** channel. Discovery usually happens by hand.

Three decision rules:

1. **One channel clearly ahead?** Put 70% of next month's effort there, and keep one experiment running.
2. **All three producing something, none dominant?** Your offer works and your reach is too small. Raise volume before you change anything else.
3. **Two of three produced zero paying customers, at a volume you could keep up?** The problem is the offer or the group, not the channel. Switching platforms will not fix it. Go back to step 1.

Founders resist rule 3, and it is the one that saves the most money.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Counting followers, signups or confirmed orders as customers | You improve a number that never becomes revenue | Count delivered and paid only |
| Running nine channels at once | None of them gets enough volume to tell you anything | Three channels, four weeks, equal effort |
| Killing a channel in week two | You throw away the one that needed a new message | Cut nothing before week four |
| No weekly funnel table | You cannot tell whether the problem is reach, offer or delivery | Five rows, every Friday |
| Ignoring the refusals | The most useful information in the business goes uncollected | Call every cancellation within 48 hours |
| Treating paid ads as the discovery channel | Money spent proving a message you have not written yet | Prove the message by hand, then buy reach |
| Setting a 100-customer goal for a B2B product with a long cycle | A target that cannot be hit, and a demoralised team | Set 20 customers who will give you a reference |
| Never asking a happy customer for a referral | Your cheapest channel stays empty | Ask at delivery, while the product is in their hands |

Six of these eight are measurement errors, not marketing errors. That is the argument of this whole page.

## Checklist

- [ ] Group named, with 300 specific people or places listed where they gather
- [ ] "Customer" defined as delivered and paid, written where the team can see it
- [ ] Three channel guesses written as sentences with a number in each
- [ ] Weekly customer target worked out from the goal and the deadline
- [ ] Week-one funnel measured across all five steps
- [ ] Weekly reach needed, worked out from your own end-to-end rate
- [ ] Friday funnel table in place, same five rows every week
- [ ] Every customer and every refusal called
- [ ] Nothing changed mid-test except one variable at a time
- [ ] Week-four comparison done on paying customers per channel
- [ ] Decision recorded against one of the three rules, with next month's plan
- [ ] Referral ask built into delivery

**You are done when:** you can say, in one sentence, how many paying customers each channel produced last week, what your end-to-end conversion rate is, and what number would make you change the offer.
