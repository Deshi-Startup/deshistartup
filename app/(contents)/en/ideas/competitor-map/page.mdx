# Competitor Map: What Your Customer Would Replace

> **In short:** Your competitor is whatever the buyer uses to get the job done today. This could be a supplier, a notebook or a relative's help. The buyer might also leave the task undone. Compare what matters to that buyer. Record where each fact came from. Then choose one reason they might switch to you and test it.

## Copy this map

Use one copy for one customer group and one task. A notebook or spreadsheet is enough.

```text
Customer group and location:
Task they need to complete:
When they last needed it:

Alternative name:
Type: direct rival / different solution / manual work / do nothing
What the customer gets:
Full cost: money + time + travel + switching effort
Payment timing or credit terms:
Why this customer chooses it:
What goes wrong, according to whom:
Evidence: exact URL or interview/observation reference + date
Status: observed / customer-reported / provider-claimed / unknown

Our proposed difference:
What we would ask the customer to change:
What result would show our idea is wrong:
Next test, owner and date:
```

The map is ready when someone else can check your sources and understand the next test. You do not need to list every business in the country.

## Start with a task small enough to compare

Use this before building, changing your offer or entering a new area. If your question is how many possible customers exist, you need market sizing; this tool answers **why one of them would choose you**.

Name a person and a task. For example: a small clothing seller who checks for missing courier payments each evening. “E-commerce” is too broad. It could lead you to compare firms that solve very different problems.

Accelerating Bangladesh's market research course covers rivals, buyer research and how to define a problem.[^ab-market-research] Put those ideas together: speak to buyers while you build the map. A website cannot tell you why someone keeps using an awkward process.

## Find four kinds of alternatives

Ask a prospective customer: “Show me what you did the last time this came up. What else did you consider?” Add each answer before suggesting your own product.

These categories keep the search wider than an app-store comparison.

| Alternative | What belongs here | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Direct rival | Another product solving the same task for the same group | Provider website, public demo, buyer account |
| Different solution | An accountant, agent or bundled supplier service | Customer interview, local service provider |
| Manual work | Notebook, spreadsheet, calls, family help | A permitted observation of the actual process |
| Do nothing | Tolerating errors, delaying work, accepting a loss | A recent example of the task being skipped |

The strongest alternative may charge no subscription. That does not make it free to replace: the customer may trust it, already know it, or receive help you cannot yet provide.

Search in English and Bangla. Check local listings, markets and each provider's own offer. Ask about setup, delivery, support and how to cancel. Be honest about why you are asking. Do not pretend to be a buyer to get private records.

## Compare the things buyers care about

Ask why the buyer chose the current option. Pick three or four things that could change that choice. These might be speed, cost, help when things go wrong or the chance to pay later. Check whether the new tool works on the buyer's phone and is easy to set up.

Avoid “AI-powered,” “modern” or “best quality” unless you can connect them to an observable result. “Works when the shop's connection drops” is testable. “Superior technology” is not.

Label what you know. A listed price is what the provider says it charges. A bill the owner chooses to show you tells you what they were charged. A matching payment record shows what they paid. One buyer's complaint does not show that all buyers have the same problem.

Watch someone do the real task. You may spot a problem that a product page leaves out. This is the idea behind the Government Digital Service's guide to research in everyday settings.[^gds-observation] Ask before looking at records. Remove private details or use made-up records when you share your work.

## Worked example: a courier-payment tool

Imagine a founder building a tool that matches courier statements with payments for small clothing brands in Chattogram. This whole example is made up, including costs and what sellers say.

The founder interviews six sellers. Four use a spreadsheet. Two rely on a notebook and phone calls. Several have heard of software, but nobody wants another daily data-entry job. This is a clue for the next test, not a population estimate.

Here is the first map for sellers who use two couriers.

| Option | Why a seller might keep it | Friction to verify | Evidence in this example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spreadsheet | Familiar; can combine two couriers | Manual copying and unmatched IDs | Two observed sessions |
| Part-time bookkeeper | A person follows up on differences | Cost and availability during busy periods | One seller's report |
| Each courier's dashboard | Already included in the relationship | Cross-courier view may still require manual work | Check both current dashboards |
| Leave small differences unresolved | Avoids another daily task | Lost cash may outweigh the effort saved | Ask for recent unresolved cases |
| Proposed tool | One list of payments to check | Setup effort, access to records and mistakes | A guess still to test |

The map points to one useful promise: “Find missing payments without entering every order twice.” This is a <Term name="hypothesis">hypothesis</Term>: a guess you still need to test. You have not yet shown that the tool can replace a bookkeeper.

Say one seller spends 25 minutes a day matching payments, for 24 working days. That is 25 × 24 = 600 minutes, or 10 hours a month. For this example, value the time at BDT 150 an hour. That gives a time value of BDT 1,500. Now consider a BDT 700 monthly fee. Is it worth paying? The tool might save only half the time. Those five saved hours may not reduce any bill the seller pays.

Run a small test with private details removed. Time the old process, then the new one. Count mistakes in both. Next, offer a trial with a clear price. Watch whether the seller sets it up and pays. That tells you more than a promise to buy later.

## Turn the map into a decision

Write one sentence:

```text
For [specific buyer], [alternative] is hard to replace because [reason].
We think [measurable improvement] is enough to justify [switching effort].
We will test that by [action] and reconsider if [contradicting result].
```

If every row is unknown, do more research. If buyers like your difference but will not change their workflow, reduce switching effort or choose another group. If your only advantage is a lower price, check that you can deliver at that price before promising it.

Plan an initial half-day for public research and a separate round of customer conversations; this is a suggested work budget, not a completion guarantee. Recheck prices before using the map for pricing or a pitch. Update the map after a lost sale teaches you about an alternative you missed.

## Before you use the result

- [ ] The map names one buyer group and one task.
- [ ] It includes manual work and doing nothing.
- [ ] Important claims have a source, date and evidence label.
- [ ] Unknowns remain visible; competitor weaknesses are not invented.
- [ ] Switching costs and payment timing appear beside price.
- [ ] One small test could prove the proposed advantage wrong.

Next, book the evidence you still need using the [field research plan](/en/validation/field-research-plan). If the alternative is clear, use [demand tests](/en/validation/demand-without-building) to see whether people will act on your offer.
