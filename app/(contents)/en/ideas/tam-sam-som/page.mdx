# Easy Method for Calculating TAM/SAM/SOM in Bangladesh

> **Key Takeaway:** TAM is the total market for a defined need and buyer. SAM is the portion your product, price and service geography can serve. SOM is a defensible estimate of the business you can win in a stated period. Internet access and ad reach help plan channels; they do not establish demand. Capacity limits sales, but it does not guarantee them.

## A One-Page Worksheet

Fill this in before you calculate anything.

```text
Product/service:
Date:

TAM (total addressable market)
What the base number measures (people/businesses/money):
Source and year:
Number:

SAM (eligible segment your product can serve)
Filter 1 (channel or platform):
Filter 2 (region, age, or income):
Source and year:
Number:

SOM (realistic in the first 12 months)
Reachable qualified prospects and measured conversion:
Retention and time period:
Capacity constraint (production or delivery):
Assumption:
Number:
```

## How to Fill in Each Row

**TAM's base number.** Define the buyer, problem, ability to pay and unit: customers or annual spending. An internet-user count may be a broad context for an online service, but most internet users will not need any particular product. DataReportal estimated 82.8 million internet users in October 2025; that is not the TAM for a meal plan.[^datareportal-2026]

**SAM's filters.** Use evidence for the intersection of the conditions: customers with the need, in your delivery area, at your price. Multiplying Facebook's national ad audience by the national urban share assumes that audience has the same geography as the whole population. The report does not establish that intersection.[^datareportal-2026] Use local counts or label the missing intersection as unknown.

**SOM's evidence.** Estimate qualified prospects reached, conversion, retention and the time it takes to acquire them. Compare the result with production, delivery and support capacity. Capacity is a ceiling, not evidence that buyers will fill it.

## A Filled-In Example

**All counts, prices and conversion rates below are hypothetical. They teach the arithmetic; they are not measured Bangladesh market data.** Suppose you sell a monthly meal plan to office workers.

| Layer | Assumption and calculation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| TAM | 100,000 eligible workers × Tk 3,000/month × 12 = Tk 360 crore/year | Hypothetical spending if every eligible worker bought |
| SAM | 10,000 eligible workers inside the actual delivery area × Tk 3,000 × 12 = Tk 36 crore/year | Portion this service can serve |
| Sales scenario | 2,000 qualified prospects reached × 10% conversion = 200 paying customers | Assumed acquisitions, before churn |
| Capacity ceiling | 6,000 meals/month ÷ 15 meals/customer = 400 concurrent customers | Maximum service capacity at this usage |

The sales scenario is 200 customers, below the 400-customer capacity ceiling. At Tk 3,000 each, 200 active customers would produce Tk 6 lakh of monthly revenue, or a Tk 72 lakh annual run rate. That is not automatically first-year revenue: acquisition dates, churn, pauses and refunds change the year's total.

If conversion is 5%, the same reach produces 100 acquisitions. If it is 20%, it produces 400, reaching the capacity ceiling before allowing for churn. A small paid pilot should replace these assumptions with observations before you invest in more capacity.

## Never Say "Just 1% of the Market"

One line shows up in nearly every pitch deck: "If we capture just 1% of the market, we'll make this many crore taka." It sounds modest, but it isn't a plan at all. There's no reason given for why 1%, rather than 0.5% or 5%. Investors have heard this line so often that it now works as a warning sign rather than a selling point.

The useful questions are how many qualified prospects you can reach, why they will pay, whether they remain customers and whether you can serve them. In the example, 400 is a capacity limit; 200 is a sales assumption that still needs testing. Neither comes from choosing an impressive percentage of a national audience.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Using total population for TAM with no internet or purchasing-power filter | The market looks several times bigger than it is |
| Not stating the source for a SAM filter | Can't tell if the number is data or a guess |
| Setting SOM as a percentage of SAM | Ignores your own actual capacity limits |
| Saying "just 1% of the market" instead of stating SOM | Reads as a warning sign to investors |
| Multiplying unrelated national percentages | An unverified intersection gets presented as data |
| Skipping one of the three layers and jumping straight to a revenue projection | No layer is left that can be checked |

## Checklist

- [ ] Checked that TAM's base number fits the product (not total population)
- [ ] Wrote SAM's two filters separately, with sources
- [ ] Identified missing local data and avoided unsupported intersections
- [ ] Estimated reach, conversion and retention, then checked capacity
- [ ] Did not replace SOM with a percentage ("1% of the market")
- [ ] Recorded the year next to all three numbers

Rule of thumb: **SOM needs evidence of sales and retention, constrained by capacity.**
