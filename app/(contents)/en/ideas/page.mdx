# Ideas and Market Research

> **In short:** Begin with a difficulty that a specific group of people experiences, then investigate how they handle it today. Choose what to build only after you understand the problem, its consequences and the alternatives customers already have.

You may be looking for a startup idea, or you may have several and not know which deserves your time. An idea such as an app, a delivery service or a marketplace is a possible solution. The earlier question is whose work or life would improve enough for them to use it, pay for it or change what they already do.

You do not need a national survey to start answering that question. You need access to people who experience the problem and a way to understand recent, specific instances of it. This section helps you move from a broad idea to a problem you can investigate. Once you are ready to test whether people will choose a proposed solution, continue to [validation](/en/validation).

## Look for a task that is difficult today

A useful starting point is something people are already trying to do. Where do they spend time correcting mistakes, coordinating with others or working around a missing service? The difficulty may be familiar to you through your own work, but other people may experience it differently. Treat your experience as a place to look, not the whole answer.

Suppose you are interested in software for clothing shops in Sylhet. “Shops need better software” is too broad to investigate. An owner describing a recent problem matching courier payments to orders gives you something more concrete. You can ask to understand that incident: which orders were involved, which reports they used, what did not match and how they resolved it.

That conversation may reveal a repeated, costly difficulty. It may also reveal that the existing process works adequately and the incident was unusual. Both answers help you decide where to spend your time. If you have no starting point yet, [finding ideas](/en/ideas/finding-ideas) describes ways to look for problems you can observe first-hand.

## Understand the alternative before proposing a replacement

Customers already do something about a problem, even when they do not buy a dedicated product. They may use a notebook, a spreadsheet, a staff member's memory or a combination of services. Sometimes they tolerate the inconvenience because changing would cost more than living with it.

For the shop owner, the alternative to your proposed payment-matching software might be an order notebook and two courier reports. Another software company is only one possible competitor. Before designing a dashboard, find out who maintains those records, how often the mismatch occurs and what the owner would have to change to use your product.

The [competitor map](/en/ideas/competitor-map) helps you compare these alternatives. The point is to understand why customers choose what they use now. A list of rival company names alone will not explain that decision.

## Let research narrow the idea

Keep the observation separate from your explanation. One owner reported an unmatched payment; you may suspect many shops have the same problem. The first statement describes something you heard. The second still needs investigation. Ask other relevant owners about their recent settlements without assuming they have the same difficulty.

As you learn more, the customer group may become narrower: perhaps only shops using multiple couriers struggle, or only those processing enough orders to make manual checking difficult. That is useful progress. It makes the next conversation and the possible product more specific.

Market data can then help put the group in context. Population, internet subscriptions or a broad industry count cannot tell you how many of those shops need your service or would buy it. Check what a figure counts, when it was measured and whether it actually describes the people you mean. [Using market data](/en/ideas/using-market-data) explains those checks; the [market research checklist](/en/ideas/market-research-checklist) helps you combine them with direct customer research.

If several problems still look promising, compare how serious each is, how well you understand it and whether you can reach the people affected. Use the [problem-ranking scorecard](/en/ideas/problem-ranking-scorecard) to make those reasons visible, not to calculate a prediction of success.

For the courier-payment idea, the next useful step is another conversation about a recent settlement, not immediately building the dashboard. When you can explain who struggles, what happens and how they cope today, you have a clearer basis for testing a solution.
