# How to Size a Market in Bangladesh

> **Key Takeaway:** Size the market from both directions. Top-down starts with a defined population and filters for the buyer and problem. Bottom-up starts with reachable prospects, tested conversion, price and purchase frequency. Registry entries, completed audits and paying customers count different things; none can substitute for the others.

## Top-down: Narrow a Big Number Step by Step

Start by defining the buyer: for example, private limited companies that need audit-ready bookkeeping software, in the regions and price band you can serve. A company registry is a starting list, not proof that every entry is active or will buy. Conversely, an annual count of verified audit reports does not prove that every company outside it is inactive, evades tax or has no software need.

**Illustrative example, not Bangladesh market data:** suppose a deduplicated directory contains 100,000 companies in the relevant geography. Your documented activity and product-fit checks suggest that 50,000 fit the target segment. At an assumed annual price of Tk 30,000, the segment's annual revenue potential would be 50,000 × Tk 30,000 = Tk 150 crore. This assumes all eligible buyers purchase; it is not your sales forecast.

Keep a source or a clearly labelled assumption beside each filter. Confirm the legal form, business activity, need, budget and geography. An activity measure such as <Term name="trade-license">trade license</Term> renewal or regular filings can help refine a list, but still does not prove willingness to buy.

## Bottom-up: Multiply From One Buyer

This method works in the opposite direction. Take one real buyer, then multiply their count, price, and purchase frequency to get a revenue estimate: number of likely buyers × average price × purchases per year = annual revenue potential.

Back to the same example. Say your sales team can realistically reach 15,000 qualified prospects from that illustrative segment in the first two years. If 20% of that 15,000 buy a Tk 30,000 annual subscription, the math reads 15,000 × 20% × Tk 30,000 = Tk 9 crore. Both the 20% and the Tk 30,000 are assumptions here, ones you'd need to test in the field before real sales begin. That means 3,000 paying customers, not 15,000. Tk 9 crore is the annual subscription revenue at that customer level, not the total cash collected over the two-year acquisition period. Churn, acquisition timing and discounts still affect the forecast.

Bottom-up's strength is that you can check every input yourself, by phone or in the field. Its weakness sits right next to that strength: change one small assumption and the whole number swings. Drop 20% to 5% and Tk 9 crore becomes Tk 2.25 crore.

## Check the Two Against Each Other

Top-down described an illustrative segment of 50,000 eligible companies. Bottom-up estimated 3,000 paying customers from 15,000 reachable prospects. Those figures answer different questions: eligible market, sales reach and expected wins. Compare the definitions, geography, price and period before comparing the totals.

Numerical agreement alone does not validate demand. Investigate a large difference, but do not force the results to converge: the segment may simply be much larger than your near-term distribution capacity. Test the conversion and price with actual buyers.

So run both, and don't stop at one. Top-down alone never catches a bad filter. Bottom-up alone gives you no outside check on how realistic the assumptions are.

## Traps to Avoid in Bangladesh

Don't conflate different kinds of business. The Economic Census 2024 counted more than 1.17 crore economic units nationwide, everything from shops to home-based work.[^economic-census] That number isn't comparable to a registry count of incorporated companies — they're counting two different universes. If your buyer is specifically a private limited company, start with company records and investigate activity and need. If your buyer is any size of business regardless of legal form, the Economic Census figure is closer, but you still need to state that definition.

Don't treat an old census figure as current. Both the population census and the economic census run about once a decade. The market can shift a lot in the years between, so always state which year's figure you're using. See [Where to Find Bangladesh Market Data](/en/ideas/market-data-sources) for more on stale links and shifting definitions.

Don't multiply a foreign per-capita spending rate straight into Bangladesh's population. "The average American spends this much a year on this product, multiply by Bangladesh's population and the market is this big" looks convincing on a slide, but purchasing power, pricing, and habits don't transfer across countries. This kind of math falls apart at the first question in the room.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Treating a registration count as the market directly | Inactive or irrelevant entries can inflate the estimate; the gap must be measured |
| Lumping different business forms (company, sole proprietorship, informal) together | The baseline for the calculation is wrong |
| Doing only top-down or only bottom-up | One method's error never gets caught by the other |
| Multiplying a foreign per-capita spending rate straight into the population | Ignores gaps in purchasing power and behavior |
| Not writing down each assumption (conversion %, price, frequency) separately | Can't defend the number when someone asks |
| Using an old census figure without a date | The market has moved on since then |

## Checklist

- [ ] Ran both top-down and bottom-up
- [ ] Found an activity number one layer below the big headline number
- [ ] Made the buyer's legal form (company/sole proprietorship/informal) explicit
- [ ] Wrote down each assumption (%, price, frequency) separately
- [ ] Compared matching definitions and periods, and investigated differences
- [ ] Recorded the source and year for every number

Rule of thumb: **A registry count is not the market. Activity helps refine the list; customer need and willingness to pay still need testing.**
