# How to Size a Market in Bangladesh

> **Key Takeaway:** There are two ways to size a market: top-down and bottom-up. Top-down starts from a large official number and narrows it step by step. Bottom-up starts from one real buyer and multiplies upward. Neither is enough on its own. Nearly 3 lakh companies are registered with RJSC, and that figure gets used all over the place as the size of Bangladesh's corporate sector. Yet the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Bangladesh (ICAB) verified audit reports for only about 57,000 companies in FY2024-25. The rest have gone years without filing a return, effectively invisible. So trusting one big number can inflate your market by up to fivefold.

## Top-down: Narrow a Big Number Step by Step

This method starts with a large, easy-to-find number, then filters it down toward your real buyer step by step. The problem shows up at the very first step: the big number is usually far more hollow than it sounds.

Say you are building audit-ready bookkeeping software for private limited companies. The opening slide practically writes itself: nearly 3 lakh companies are registered with RJSC, with roughly 10,000 added every year. That's true, and it's not your buyer count. An analysis published in The Financial Express in May 2026, written by a chartered accountant, laid out exactly this gap. ICAB's Document Verification System (DVS) verified audit reports for only about 57,000 companies in FY2024-25. That's roughly the real annual count of companies that actually get audited. In other words, fewer than one in five companies on RJSC's books files a regular audit; the rest file no returns and no taxes.

That 57,000 is your real ceiling, not 3 lakh. Audit-ready bookkeeping software is only needed by companies that actually get audited, or are legally required to. The list shrinks further if you target only mid-size and large companies and drop small private limiteds that face little audit pressure.

The rule of thumb follows directly: never use a registry count as your market. Look one layer down for an activity number instead. Almost every sector has one — <Term name="trade-license">trade license</Term> renewal rates, VAT return filing rates, or a professional body's list of active members.

## Bottom-up: Multiply From One Buyer

This method works in the opposite direction. Take one real buyer, then multiply their count, price, and purchase frequency to get a revenue estimate: number of likely buyers × average price × purchases per year = annual revenue potential.

Back to the same example. Say your sales team can realistically reach 15,000 of those 57,000 regularly-audited companies in the first two years — the rest sit inside large conglomerates or already use other software. If 20% of that 15,000 buy a Tk 30,000 annual subscription, the math reads 15,000 × 20% × Tk 30,000 = Tk 9 crore. Both the 20% and the Tk 30,000 are assumptions here, ones you'd need to test in the field before real sales begin. The number exists only to show the method.

Bottom-up's strength is that you can check every input yourself, by phone or in the field. Its weakness sits right next to that strength: change one small assumption and the whole number swings. Drop 20% to 5% and Tk 9 crore becomes Tk 2.25 crore.

## Check the Two Against Each Other

Top-down brought you down to 57,000. Bottom-up worked up to 15,000 real buyers. The two land in the same order of magnitude, and that's actually the good sign. When the two methods converge, your assumptions are probably realistic. When they differ by tenfold, something is badly wrong in one of them — either the top-down filter wasn't strict enough, or the bottom-up assumptions are far from reality.

So run both, and don't stop at one. Top-down alone never catches a bad filter. Bottom-up alone gives you no outside check on how realistic the assumptions are.

## Traps to Avoid in Bangladesh

Don't conflate different kinds of business. The Economic Census 2024 counted more than 1.17 crore economic units nationwide, everything from shops to home-based work.[^economic-census] That number isn't comparable to RJSC's 3 lakh companies — they're counting two different universes. If your buyer is specifically a private limited company, use the RJSC/ICAB numbers above. If your buyer is any size of business regardless of legal form, the Economic Census figure is closer, but you still need to state that definition.

Don't treat an old census figure as current. Both the population census and the economic census run about once a decade. The market can shift a lot in the years between, so always state which year's figure you're using. See [Where to Find Bangladesh Market Data](/ideas/market-data-sources) for more on stale links and shifting definitions.

Don't multiply a foreign per-capita spending rate straight into Bangladesh's population. "The average American spends this much a year on this product, multiply by Bangladesh's population and the market is this big" looks convincing on a slide, but purchasing power, pricing, and habits don't transfer across countries. This kind of math falls apart at the first question in the room.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Treating a registration count as the market directly | Real buyer count is 5-10x lower, gets caught in Q&A |
| Lumping different business forms (company, sole proprietorship, informal) together | The baseline for the calculation is wrong |
| Doing only top-down or only bottom-up | One method's error never gets caught by the other |
| Multiplying a foreign per-capita spending rate straight into the population | Ignores gaps in purchasing power and behavior |
| Not writing down each assumption (conversion %, price, frequency) separately | Can't defend the number when someone asks |
| Using an old census figure without a date | The market has moved on since then |

## Checklist

- [ ] Ran both top-down and bottom-up
- [ ] Found an activity number one layer below the big headline number
- [ ] Made the buyer's legal form (company/sole proprietorship/informal) explicit
- [ ] Wrote down each assumption (%, price, frequency) separately
- [ ] Checked whether the two methods land close together, and if not, found out why
- [ ] Recorded the source and year for every number

Rule of thumb: **A registry count is not the market. The activity number underneath it is.**
