# How to Cite Bangladesh Market Data Cleanly

> **Key Takeaway:** Before you put a number in a deck or a plan, write three things next to it: who produced it, for what period, and exactly what it counts. Drop any of the three and even a true number turns misleading. In January 2026, LightCastle Partners and Startup Bangladesh reported that Bangladesh startup funding grew 195% in 2025 over 2024. That's true, and about 89% of that year's total came from a single deal. Write only "funding grew 195%" and the reader assumes the whole ecosystem heated up, when the deal count actually fell.

## Every Number Needs Three Things Next to It

Before citing a number, ask yourself who produced it, for what period, and what exactly they counted to get there. No source name, and nobody can verify it. No time period, and a six-month-old number can pass for current. No stated scope, and the reader assumes the number covers as much as it sounds like it does, when it might not.

All three fit into one short sentence; they don't cost extra space. "According to a LightCastle Partners and Startup Bangladesh report, 12 announced deals in 2025 totaled USD 12.4 crore in investment" carries the name, the period, and the scope all at once. Write only "Bangladesh startups raised USD 12.4 crore" instead, and you lose who said so, which year, and whether it's announced or actual investment.

## What a 195% Growth Headline Hides

LightCastle Partners and Startup Bangladesh's joint annual investment report came out on 15 January 2026. It states that 2025 saw USD 12.4 crore invested across 12 deals, a 195% increase over 2024's USD 4.2 crore across 41 deals.[^lightcastle-2025]

That number is entirely true. The very next line of the same report says the top three deals accounted for about 95% of total investment. The single deal that merged ShopUp and Sary into SILQ Group alone accounted for USD 11 crore, roughly 89% of the year's total. The report itself says that excluding this deal, investment activity remained modest. It calls 2025 a "strategic transition year", one of concentration rather than volume. Deal count also fell from 41 to 12 in a single year, a drop of roughly 71%.

Put the two sentences side by side and the gap is obvious. "Investment grew 195% in 2025" reads as if the whole ecosystem picked up. "89% of total investment came from a single merger; the other 11 deals combined were fairly modest" is the same underlying fact, and far more useful for a decision. Both come from the same report, and both are true. The difference is only in how much got said.

So before citing any growth percentage, open the report and check whether that growth is spread out or sitting in one or two large deals. That check takes a minute; the answer is usually right there in the summary page.

## A Company's Claim, or an Independent Count

Who produced a number tells you more than just a name — it tells you their interest too. Say a food delivery app writes on its own blog that it holds 60% of the country's food delivery market. That figure need not be false, but the company hasn't disclosed exactly how it defined "market" or "share". A competitor's app might sit outside the count, or the comparison might cover only the largest city.

When citing this kind of number, mark it "according to the company's own claim" and never place it in the same row as an independent source like LightCastle or BBS. A company's own promotional figure can be useful context, but don't give it the same weight as independently verified data in a decision.

## Don't Treat an Old Number as a Fresh Claim

Market numbers age fast, especially funding, subscriber counts, and inflation figures. Before reusing a number from a report you read six months ago, go back to the primary source and check whether a newer edition has come out. The habit of separating a link from the file itself is covered in more detail on [Where to Find Bangladesh Market Data](/en/ideas/market-data-sources).

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Result |
|---|---|
| Citing a number with no source name, period, or scope | The reader can't verify it |
| Citing a growth percentage without checking for concentration | A single deal becomes the whole sector's story |
| Placing a company's promotional figure at equal weight with independent data | The reader assumes both are equally reliable |
| Lifting a number from a news article without checking the underlying report | Conditions or definitions the article dropped go missing |
| Using an old report's number without checking for a newer edition | A figure that's already changed gets published anyway |
| Mixing numbers from different sources without matching their definitions | Two different things get treated as one |

## Checklist

- [ ] Wrote the source name next to every number
- [ ] Wrote which period the number covers
- [ ] Wrote exactly what the number counts
- [ ] Checked for concentration before citing a growth percentage
- [ ] Kept a company's claim separate from independent data
- [ ] Went back to the primary report to confirm it's the latest edition

Rule of thumb: **Never write a number without a name, a period, and a scope next to it.**
